CRT Shaders for Emulators: Complete Setup Guide

By Logan Pierce • Published January 20, 2026

CRT shaders can make emulators look authentic without sacrificing clarity when configured with intent. This guide lays out a complete setup, from shader stacks and scaling to backend choices, so you avoid muddy output and unnecessary latency. You will learn how to evaluate presets, tune masks and scanlines, and mitigate artifacts while maintaining a predictable workflow that carries across emulators.

Key takeaways

  • Select a shader preset that matches display resolution and target look.
  • Use integer scaling first, then add smoothing only when required.
  • Order shader passes to upscale, then apply mask and scanlines last.
  • Prefer Vulkan when available for performance and wider shader support.
  • Validate tuning with motion, gradient ramps, and high contrast edges.

Foundations

The most reliable way to approach CRT shaders is to define the goal image before touching settings. Decide whether you want a clean aperture grille look, a softer shadow mask, or minimal embellishment that only counters nearest neighbor harshness. For example, a 1080p monitor may favor lighter masks to avoid phosphor moiré, while a 4K panel can support stronger subpixel detail. The tradeoff is performance and stability, since heavy masks increase cost and can reveal banding on poorly calibrated displays. Clarifying the target helps choose presets and informs scaling, because upscaling stages and mask strength interact with native resolution and viewing distance more than people expect.

Accurate terminology prevents missteps when reading shader docs and presets. A mask simulates the phosphor triad or grille structure, scanlines represent vertical blanking darkness between lines, and beam dynamics control bloom and falloff of bright pixels. As a rule of thumb, apply upscaling early in the pipeline, then shape the beam, and finally place mask and scanline passes. Doing this in reverse can amplify aliasing and multiply ringing artifacts. Edge cases appear on handheld content and 480i sources, where interlacing and subpixel layouts complicate assumptions. Understanding naming conventions and pass roles ensures changes produce predictable outcomes instead of chasing unexplained side effects.

Display characteristics define sensible defaults more than emulator brand or core. A 60 Hz IPS monitor with standard gamut behaves differently from a 120 Hz OLED with near-zero black level, so identical shader settings will not match. As a practical example, masks that look rich on OLED can crush midtones on LCDs unless gamma and post-brightness compensate. The limitation is simple brightness headroom. Heavier masks reduce average picture level, and aggressive scanlines can make HDR desktop modes clip or tone map unexpectedly. Baseline your display with neutral color temperature and disable exotic vendor sharpeners. Then tune shaders atop a known-good, repeatable base.

Backend choice influences shader language availability and execution precision. Vulkan typically offers stronger parallelism and good support for modern slang shaders, while OpenGL may be sufficient on older hardware or odd driver stacks. The tradeoff is compatibility, because some systems or wrappers only expose OpenGL reliably. As a rule, prefer Vulkan when both are stable, then fall back to OpenGL when drivers or overlays conflict. If you mix desktop overlays, test for frame pacing issues, since presentation mode can interact with v-sync differently per API. Lock a backend early in your process so preset behavior does not change halfway through tuning due to a silent switch.

Core practices

Start with integer scaling whenever geometry allows, because it preserves pixel edges and reduces resampling blur. For instance, scale 240p content by 4x to 960 lines, then pad or lightly filter to reach your panel’s height. When exact integer multiples do not fit, blend strategies like integer-plus-fractional scaling where the last step uses a gentle filter. The tradeoff is slight softness, but it controls moiré and ringing better than forcing full fractional scaling. This choice interacts with mask pitch and scanline thickness, so verify the result on scrolling text and checker patterns. If shimmer appears during motion, revisit the scaling stage before chasing shader parameters.

Ordering shader passes matters more than picking a famous preset name. Place upscalers and edge-aware resamplers before beam and mask passes, then finalize with post-brightness or gamma trims. As a concrete pattern, structure the pipeline as scale, linearize if needed, beam shape, mask, scanlines, tone, then presentation. This sequence prevents the mask from being scaled improperly and keeps scanlines tied to the final geometry. The limitation is preset portability. Some packs assume specific input sizes and will misbehave if you reorder them. When working inside a preset, adjust variables rather than reordering until you confirm dependencies, then test both static and scrolling scenes for stability.

Beam and mask intensity should rise with resolution and viewing distance to preserve apparent detail without crushing mids. A safe range for mask strength on 4K panels is moderate to strong, while 1080p often benefits from lighter values. Example: raise beam width slightly when the image looks too brittle after a sharp upscaler, then offset with a small boost in post-brightness to maintain overall luminance. The tradeoff is latency and noise. Heavier dynamic effects can add cost per frame and may expose dithering patterns on gradients. Keep changes incremental and validate with gray ramps, black to white transitions, and high contrast sprites to avoid overfitting.

Backend selection and shader language compatibility affect both feasibility and performance, so test with a representative scene. Vulkan excels with slang pipelines and often yields better frame pacing under load, while OpenGL can be more predictable on legacy GPUs. When pushing complex masks or multi-pass pipelines, stress test by enabling integer scaling plus fast motion to see if frametime spikes occur. If they do, reduce the number of passes, simplify the mask pattern, or swap to a lighter resampler. Next, set integer scaling where possible and confirm aspect ratio. Only then refine beam width, mask intensity, and scanline darkness, followed by small gamma or brightness trims. Use a known test scene: a scrolling platformer level, a gradient ramp image, and a static menu with text. The tradeoff is patience. Moving too quickly often stacks compensating changes that hide root causes. A fixed order lets you diagnose, because when artifacts reappear, you know which stage to revisit.

Pitfalls and edge cases

Ringing and moiré often come from scaling choices that work on still images but break in motion. When you see bright halos around edges or zebra patterns on diagonals, audit the resampler and scale order before touching beam or mask. A gentler final filter or switching to an edge-aware upscaler usually reduces artifacts more effectively than lowering sharpness globally. The limitation is that some content mixes pixel art with transparent overlays, so one setting may not satisfy both. Diagnose differences with test screens that include text, fine lines, and scrolling patterns, and revisit the scaling decision tree rather than masking symptoms with heavier scanlines.

480i interlaced sources require an early decision about deinterlacing strategy. Bob or blend methods are simple and low latency but can shimmer or soften detail, while advanced motion-adaptive approaches stabilize edges at added cost. Use motion-heavy scenes to test stability and bezel-free views to expose line twitter. If you need deeper guidance, review choose deinterlacing methods for 480i, balancing clarity, latency, and artifacts to align method with your tolerance for lag and softness. Deinterlacing choices precede beam and mask tuning, because the output field cadence affects scanline simulation. Fix cadence first, then refine beam width and mask pitch.

Color and tone mapping mismatches can hide detail or create false contrast in dark scenes. If HDR is active on the desktop, shader output may pass through tone mapping that alters gamma and blacks unpredictably. The fastest fix is to set SDR for emulators unless your preset explicitly supports HDR, then calibrate white point and gamma before tuning masks. The tradeoff is reduced peak brightness on some displays, but stability makes shader tuning meaningful. Tune color once, then avoid repeated toggling to keep results consistent.

Feature overlays and third-party post processing can undermine native shader pipelines. Tools like post-process injectors add flexibility but may complicate ordering, latency, and color. Some overlays intercept presentation paths and change v-sync behavior, which affects scanline stability. The safest route is to perfect a native preset first, measure frametime and latency, then introduce overlays only with a narrowly defined goal. Frequently asked questions

Do CRT shaders increase input lag?

Most shaders have negligible added latency on modern GPUs, but heavy multi pass pipelines can raise frametime. Monitor frame pacing and reduce pass count or complexity if you see spikes.

Should I use Vulkan or OpenGL for shaders?

Prefer Vulkan when stable because it supports modern shader stacks and often yields smoother pacing. Use OpenGL when drivers or hardware limit Vulkan or when compatibility proves better in testing.

How strong should scanlines be at 1080p?

Keep scanlines light at 1080p to avoid crushing mids and readability. Start with thin lines and modest opacity, then increase gradually only if text and fine geometry remain comfortably legible.

What causes moiré with CRT masks?

Moiré arises when the simulated mask pitch clashes with the panel’s subpixel layout or scaling. Adjust scaling strategy, lighten mask strength, or change mask pattern to break the interference.

Is HDR recommended with CRT presets?

Use SDR unless the preset explicitly targets HDR and your display handles tone mapping predictably. HDR can shift gamma and blacks which complicates tuning and can undermine mask visibility.
