Onion OS Shaders and Scaling: Crisp, Authentic Looks

By Jordan Kelly • Published January 10, 2026

Onion OS offers shaders and scaling controls that shape how classic games look and feel on a small screen. Understanding which shader families suit your handheld, and how scaling preserves pixel proportions, prevents blur and shimmer that distracts during play. This guide clarifies definitions, then builds a practical lens for choosing pixel-perfect modes, CRT aesthetics, and light filters that do not add lag. You will learn default choices for clean results, plus when exceptions make sense for specific systems or displays. By the end, you can keep sprites crisp, add tasteful character, and maintain responsive input, all without over-tuning.

Key takeaways

  • Start with integer scaling and no shader, then add tastefully.
  • Prefer lightweight CRT masks on low resolution screens to reduce moire.
  • Use pixel shaders for sharp edges and avoid subpixel shimmer.
  • Match aspect and crop rules to the target system’s native geometry.
  • Keep latency low by avoiding multi-pass and heavy scaling filters.

Concepts and definitions

Shaders in Onion OS are small GPU programs that alter how each frame is drawn, while scaling controls how source pixels map to your handheld’s panel. The core idea is simple, yet the impact is large. A pixel-art platformer benefits from nearest-neighbor scaling and a single-pass shader, whereas a 3D system might need mild smoothing to hide dithering. The tradeoff is that every pass can add cost or ringing artifacts on low resolution screens. Think of shaders as seasoning, not the meal. Start with none, ensure integer scaling locks pixel ratios, then layer a light CRT mask or scanline if you want vintage texture without blur.

Integer scaling multiplies original resolution by whole numbers to prevent uneven pixel sizes that cause shimmering during motion. For example, a 160 by 144 Game Boy frame fits cleanly at 4x or 5x on many panels, while fractional scaling introduces subpixel sampling and blur. The limitation is wasted border space when the integer does not perfectly fill the screen, yet the payoff is stable edges that preserve sprite clarity. If a title scrolls horizontally, stable pixels improve legibility more than a slightly larger but fuzzy picture. When space matters, a mild fractional upscale with sharpness clamp can be a reasonable compromise.

CRT shaders simulate phosphor triads, scanlines, and beam profiles to mimic tube displays. A gentle mask can add perceived depth and reduce banding on flat panels, but heavy multi-pass shaders may create moire on small handhelds with tight subpixel layouts. The why here is physical: fine masks interact with panel subpixels and camera-like patterns to produce false detail. Choose low-intensity masks and soft scanlines, especially on screens under five inches. Pixel shaders that only enforce hard edges are safer when portability and battery life matter. Reserve advanced beam and bloom profiles for docked play where resolution and power budgets are higher.

Frameworks and decision lenses

A reliable selection framework starts with three checks: native system geometry, panel resolution, and latency budget. If integer scaling fits, choose it and run without shaders for baseline clarity. If not, prefer fractional scaling with a thin sharpness clamp and no ringing. For style, decide between pixel purity and CRT character. When you need character, apply a single-pass mask with low strength, then evaluate motion on scrolling scenes. The tradeoff is size versus stability. Bigger pictures can look worse during motion. Re-test with HUD text and diagonal edges, since both reveal shimmer quickly when scaling is off.

Aspect handling should follow the target system’s original output rather than filling modern screens. Preserve 4 by 3 for most 8 and 16 bit consoles, 5 by 4 for some microcomputers, and original handheld ratios for portable systems. Cropping can remove junk overscan, but avoid trimming gameplay edges or status bars. The simple rule is crop only when the emulator core flags safe regions. If your panel is close but not exact, center the image with clean borders instead of stretching. Many players accept side mats for accuracy because stretch makes circles look oval and sprites lose intended proportions.

Examples and short scenarios

On a 640 by 480 docked display, SNES at 256 by 224 upscales cleanly at 2x or 3x with borders, so choose integer scaling and keep shaders minimal. If you crave character, add a soft scanline shader at low opacity to reduce banding. The tradeoff is that scanlines consume brightness, so compensate with a small backlight bump rather than increasing shader strength. For handheld play at 480 by 320, integer scaling may leave larger borders. In that case, accept the mats for stability, or use a fractional upscale with a gentle sharpness clamp to reduce blur. Always test using Mode 7 scenes for diagonal artifacts.

For Game Boy Advance at 240 by 160 on a 720p TV, 4x integer creates a 960 by 640 image with side borders, which preserves crisp edges and keeps latency low. A light CRT mask can add texture at sofa distance, but avoid triad patterns that fight the TV’s subpixels. When using a small handheld panel around 640 by 360, prioritize pixel shaders that maintain hard edges without masks. The why is straightforward: strong masks are too dense relative to the pixel pitch and cause moire during camera pans or quick motion, which distracts more than it delights.

If you want to balance responsiveness with smooth motion, confirm VSync and latency settings before judging a shader’s feel. A clean visual can still feel sluggish if frames queue. Looking ahead

The most sustainable approach is adopting defaults that look good on most titles, then iterating with intent. Start every new system with integer scaling and no shader to set a reference. Create a single light CRT preset for docked play and a pixel-sharp preset for handheld sessions. The tradeoff is managing a couple of profiles, but it reduces time spent retuning per game. When your taste shifts, adjust only one variable at a time and keep notes about what changed and why. This discipline helps you diagnose issues quickly and return to a known good state when experiments fail.

As firmware and cores improve, revisit assumptions about scaling and shader cost. Some updates optimize sampling and reduce pass overhead, which makes previously risky presets viable. Watch release notes for mentions of scaling kernels, mask algorithms, or color management changes, since these directly affect artifacts. If you maintain shared configurations, test changes on a variety of content, including dark scenes, diagonal lines, and fast scrolling backgrounds. When something breaks, roll back only the last change and retest. This methodical loop avoids conflating improvements with regressions and keeps your library consistent across handheld and TV use.

Finally, keep your visuals aligned with broader setup hygiene. Back up configs before large changes, capture reference screenshots, and log measured settings. If you discover that HUD text remains fuzzy even with clean scaling, review base emulator options and controller mappings in a broader tuning pass rather than pushing heavier shaders. Frequently asked questions

Should I use integer scaling for every system?

Use integer scaling whenever it fits your panel because it preserves pixel stability and edges. When it does not fill the screen well, try a mild fractional upscale with a light sharpness clamp. Always test motion to catch shimmer.

Do CRT shaders increase input lag in Onion OS?

Light single-pass CRT shaders usually have negligible impact, but heavy multi-pass effects can add cost. If you feel sluggishness, disable extras, verify VSync settings, and confirm a steady frame rate before reintroducing gentle masks.

What causes moire patterns on small handheld screens?

Dense CRT masks interact with the display’s subpixel layout, creating interference patterns. On small panels, use low-intensity masks or stick to pixel-only shaders. Testing with diagonal lines and camera pans helps spot the issue quickly.

Are scanlines recommended on low brightness displays?

Scanlines reduce perceived brightness, so start with low strength. If the image looks too dim, raise backlight a small step rather than increasing the shader effect. Avoid strong scanlines on already dim screens to maintain legibility.
