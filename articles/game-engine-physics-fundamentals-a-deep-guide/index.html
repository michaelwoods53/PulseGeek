Game Engine Physics Fundamentals: A Deep Guide

By Ethan Palmer • Published December 20, 2025

Physics in a game engine turns motion, contact, and constraint into responsive play. This guide focuses on fundamentals that translate across engines, showing how solver settings, collision modes, and materials shape feel and stability. Practitioners will find guardrails and workflows that connect abstract rules to outcomes at frame time.

Key takeaways

  • Use a fixed timestep with interpolation to stabilize physics and visuals.
  • Pair broadphase pruning with precise narrowphase for predictable contacts.
  • Tune friction and restitution together to avoid sliding or pogo effects.
  • Prefer continuous detection only for fast movers that tunnel in tests.
  • Choose joints by gameplay intent, then adjust iteration counts conservatively.

Foundations

Solid foundations begin with a clear model of bodies, forces, and constraints that an engine steps forward at discrete intervals. Rigid bodies carry mass and inertia, while colliders define contact geometry that narrows from broadphase to precise manifold generation. A stable simulation usually targets a fixed timestep like 50 to 120 Hz, then interpolates for rendering so motion remains smooth without leaking integration error. The tradeoff is cost at higher rates and latency at lower rates, so start with a middle ground that keeps your worst-case frame under budget. Engines like Unity, Unreal, Godot, and Box2D differ in defaults, but the principles align. Ask which quantities you integrate per step, how impulses are resolved, and which objects are kinematic or dynamic to keep intent crisp.

Collisions anchor believable interaction, yet their detection involves layered approximations that manage cost. A broadphase pass groups objects with bounding volumes and spatial hashes to discard unlikely pairs, while a narrowphase tests exact shapes for overlap and computes a contact manifold for the solver. This two-stage pattern enables dense scenes to remain interactive without checking every triangle or polygon. Tradeoffs emerge when shapes are concave or highly detailed, since narrowphase cost rises and small features may cause jitter. Use convex decomposition or approximate hulls for dynamic parts so the contact manifold remains stable. When accuracy is crucial, restrict precision to local areas such as the player’s feet or projectile tips, rather than the entire scene, and verify with instrumented test levels.

Materials encode surface behavior through friction and restitution that combine with mass and solver settings to shape feel. As a rule of thumb, keep dynamic friction near or slightly under static friction to reduce unexpected stick-slip transitions, and cap restitution below fully elastic unless you want arcade bounce. Materials interact with timestep and iteration counts, since insufficient solver work can exaggerate slippage or penetration. Edge cases appear with stacked bodies and moving platforms, where friction alone cannot preserve relative motion. There, consider kinematic motion with explicit velocity transfer or constraints that guide intent. Think of materials as local rules that complement global stability choices. Start conservative, test interactions like sliding ramps and corner rubs, then expand to nuanced presets per biome or level theme.

Core practices

A dependable loop starts with a fixed integration step and visual interpolation that decouples rendering spikes from physics. Accumulate real time, step the solver in fixed quanta, and update render poses by blending previous and current transforms. The benefit is determinism across frames, reduced jitter, and easier reproduction of bugs. The cost is potential input latency if the step is too coarse or if you over-buffer interpolation. A good baseline is to find the highest step that keeps collision stacks stable in your densest scene, then clamp your accumulator to avoid spiral of death. Collision strategy should combine aggressive pruning with precise contacts only where gameplay needs it. Use simple bounding shapes or grids to reduce candidate pairs, then reserve exact shape tests for areas where accuracy changes outcomes, such as character footing or projectile impact. Fast movers pose a special case. Continuous detection can prevent tunneling but adds cost, so enable it on bullets, dashes, and thin obstacles only. Movement models should express intent first, then physics second, to keep controls readable. Character controllers often move kinematically with swept tests to avoid stairs-catching and capsule snagging, while rigid bodies are better for fully physical props that accept impulses and torque. The tradeoff is between animation authority and dynamic reactivity. Workflows

A reliable physics workflow connects input, simulation, and rendering without letting spikes desync motion. The typical pattern accumulates delta time, advances the physics solver in fixed increments, then interpolates transforms for the current render frame. This allows networking or replay systems to align on tick counts while artists animate at fluid display rates. In engines that expose both update and fixed update hooks, keep authoritative physics writes in the fixed path and read-only interpolation in render. Pitfalls and edge cases

Fast or thin objects often tunnel because discrete steps miss intersections. The first remedy is to shorten the step for critical scenes, but cost scales with frequency. A more surgical fix is enabling continuous collision on specific movers and colliders likely to miss hits, such as bullets or sword tips. Validate by firing across varied thicknesses and angles, then check for false positives on crowded geometry. Even with continuous checks, you may need to enlarge proxy shapes slightly to capture edge grazes without visual artifacts. Avoid blanket continuous settings project-wide. For a deeper lens on precision choices and hybrids that mix modes, read the discussion that helps you decide when precision matters most without wasting cycles elsewhere.

Stacked bodies and suspended chains magnify solver weaknesses because small penetrations compound over iterations. Increasing position and velocity iterations can help, but pushing counts too high only hides underlying geometry or mass issues. Start by reducing mass ratios across connected parts, add joint limits and damping, and replace detailed triangles with convex primitives. If an assembly must hold shape under stress, consider a light kinematic driver that preserves pose while allowing limited compliance. When you need a fast reference of constraints and when each shines, the catalog on joint types and relevant stability choices offers a checklist to encode intent rather than relying on friction patches to keep things together.

Characters on moving platforms can slide or jitter because relative motion conflicts with solver order. A resilient approach treats platforms as kinematic and transfers velocity to riders, either through explicit velocity inheritance or a temporary constraint during contact. Blend out the link as the character departs so motion feels natural. Pure friction cannot always compensate, especially at low iteration counts or coarse steps. If your control system demands full authority, keep the controller kinematic and restrict impulsive forces to special moves. Frequently asked questions

What timestep should I start with for real-time physics?

A common starting point is 60 Hz with interpolation for rendering. Validate by stress testing stacks and fast movers. If penetrations persist, lower the step or raise solver iterations incrementally while watching frame budget.

When should I enable continuous collision detection?

Enable it for fast or thin objects that tunnel during tests. Apply it selectively to projectiles, dash moves, or thin obstacles. Keep discrete detection for most objects to control CPU cost and simplify tuning.

How do I stop characters from sliding on moving platforms?

Treat platforms as kinematic and transfer velocity to riders or attach a temporary constraint during contact. Relying on friction alone often fails under coarse steps or low iteration counts.

Should I use rigid bodies or a character controller for players?

Use a controller for authoritative movement and predictable stairs. Choose rigid bodies for fully dynamic behavior and reactive impacts. Hybrid setups are possible when gameplay needs both.

How many solver iterations are enough?

Increase only as needed for stacks, joints, or ragdolls that fail under tests. Start with engine defaults, then adjust a few steps at a time while measuring contact convergence and frame time.
