VSync and Video Timing on Miyoo: Smooth Playback

By Jared Butler • Published February 11, 2026

VSync and video timing decide whether your Miyoo shows smooth motion or visible tearing. The goal is steady frame pacing that matches the display’s refresh without adding avoidable input delay. On handheld hardware, the best setting depends on the emulator core’s workload, the audio driver, and how often the game produces frames. Treat VSync as a guardrail for page flips and treat timing as the scheduler that feeds it. When both align, motion feels fluid and audio stays locked. When they fight, cadence breaks into micro stutter or latency balloons. This article maps the tradeoffs and offers simple lenses to pick options that fit your game mix, helping you balance stability and snappiness across cores.

Key takeaways

  • Match frame pacing to display refresh before deciding on VSync.
  • Prefer consistent frame times over occasional faster but uneven frames.
  • Use audio sync to hide jitter only when drift becomes audible.
  • Lower latency with runahead, then recheck VSync interaction carefully.
  • Test stressful scenes, not menus, to validate timing stability.

Concepts and definitions

VSync controls when the GPU presents a finished frame to the display, while video timing governs when frames are produced, queued, and paced. On Miyoo, both mechanisms interact with RetroArch’s frame throttle and the core’s internal cadence. The claim is simple: matching production to presentation prevents tearing and jitter. For example, if the screen refreshes at 60 Hz, target a stable 16.7 ms frame time. The tradeoff appears when workloads exceed budget, since VSync can force missed deadlines into visible hitches. In such cases, loosening VSync or reducing latency features can restore consistency. The why is predictable physics of queues: when producers and consumers stay in phase, buffers stay short and motion remains even across scenes.

Swap interval sets how many refreshes pass before a presented frame becomes visible, and a value of one is typical for 60 Hz targets. The insight is that interval one minimizes both tearing and latency when frame times are met. Consider a platformer holding 16.7 ms steady; VSync interval one yields clean motion and responsive input. If a core intermittently hits 20 ms, presentation slips a refresh and cadence feels sticky. The limitation is that strict sync punishes small overruns, so smoothing helps only after you regain headroom. Understanding this interplay informs decisions about optional techniques like runahead or reduced buffering, which can reclaim perceived responsiveness without destabilizing pacing when tuned carefully.

Frame pacing describes the regularity of frame intervals, independent of the average frame rate. The claim is that even spacing matters more to perceived smoothness than occasional higher peaks. For example, a game that alternates 10 ms and 23 ms frames averages near budget but looks jittery, while consistent 17 ms frames feel steadier despite being slightly slower. The edge case is content designed for 50 Hz or odd refresh timings, where matching cadence may require specialized timing or audio resampling. The reason pacing dominates perception is motion integration in the eye and display scanout synchronize to cadence more than mean rate. Prioritize consistent intervals, then optimize for speed once stability is proven.

Frameworks and decision lenses

Start with a goal-first lens: pick priority order among visual stability, responsiveness, and compatibility, then tune toward it. If smooth motion is primary, enable VSync at swap interval one and cap frame rate to refresh, verifying stable 16.7 ms pacing with on-screen statistics. If responsiveness leads, disable extra buffering and consider runahead once performance headroom is confirmed. The tradeoff is that every gain steals budget elsewhere, so changes must be validated under load, not in menus or static screens.

Apply a compatibility lens when a core or game targets non standard cadence. For 50 Hz content, choose timing that favors even spacing and let audio resample modestly to follow. When emulation spikes occasionally, prefer slightly higher average frame time if it eliminates variance spikes that cause hitching under VSync. The limitation here is power and thermal constraints on handhelds, so persistent boosts are preferable to risky peak settings that collapse under heat.

Use a diagnostic lens to separate cause from symptom. If you see tearing, presentation is decoupled, so verify VSync or match the frame cap to refresh. If motion stutters without tearing, production timing is uneven, so profile frame times and reduce heavy shaders, overlays, or rewind features. When audio drifts but video looks even, enable audio sync or modest buffering to mask micro variations. The why is that each artifact maps to a distinct subsystem, and fixing the wrong one increases latency without improving motion. Build confidence by testing a tough scene for a full minute, then rechecking inputs and timing counters after each change to confirm the intended effect.

Looking ahead

Plan a maintenance loop that revalidates VSync and video timing after core updates or firmware changes. The claim is that small upstream tweaks can shift timing margins, so scheduled retests prevent regressions. For a practical cadence, run the same thirty second stress scene after any update and compare frame time variance. Keep a log of which overlays, shaders, or latency options were active to spot interactions over time. The edge case is a core that improves accuracy at a small cost, where the right answer is dialing back optional visuals to recover margin. This routine preserves smooth playback long term without constant tinkering.

Create per system presets that encode decisions about swap interval, frame cap, audio sync, and optional latency features. The benefit is faster iteration and fewer accidental conflicts between cores with different cadence expectations. For example, a 60 Hz preset may pair VSync on, swap interval one, modest audio buffering, and conservative overlays, while a responsiveness preset trims buffers and uses careful runahead only where headroom allows. The limitation is preset drift when hardware or drivers change, so keep labels tied to outcomes rather than specific numbers. This approach keeps settings clear and avoids mixing incompatible toggles during quick experiments.

Finally, invest in observations that reduce guesswork. Watch frame time graphs for sustained regularity and listen for repeating audio artifacts that reveal jitter. A short home test comparing control responsiveness across two presets can validate perceived lag without instruments. When ambiguity remains, prefer the setting that yields even pacing during challenging scenes rather than impressive averages on easy content. Over time, these habits give you a grounded feel for when to tighten VSync or relax it to regain fluidity. With practice, Miyoo playback stays consistent, and tuning sessions become quick confirmations rather than open-ended hunts.
