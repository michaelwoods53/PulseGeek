How to Reduce Contention on 320 MHz WiFi

By Rachel Morgan • Published February 7, 2026

Reducing contention on 320 MHz WiFi starts with explicit design goals, not wishful channel width. The steps below assume indoor enterprise 6 GHz with managed APs, MLO capable clients, and a controller that supports OFDMA scheduling and RU puncturing. You will plan lanes first, then shape access rules and power, and only then tune policy. This sequence keeps coverage stable while improving airtime and latency on wide channels. We also balance BSS Coloring and OBSS PD so neighboring cells remain coordinated rather than chatty. By the end, you will have a repeatable checklist and validation routine you can run before and after each change.

Key takeaways

  • Design channels first, then apply policy to shape contention.
  • Use OFDMA with cautious puncturing to preserve usable RU density.
  • Cap EIRP to reduce CCA overlap and inter‑BSS interference.
  • Validate with latency percentiles, not average throughput alone.
  • Start with 160 or 80 if 320 MHz is unstable.

Plan the work

Start by defining contention in operational terms, because fixing what you can measure avoids guesswork. Use median and 95th percentile airtime utilization per BSS, plus latency percentiles for key apps like voice. For example, set a guardrail that median airtime stays below 55 percent and 95th below 80 percent during a known busy hour. This provides a target that accounts for bursts without overreacting to single spikes. A limitation is that utilization varies by device mix and day, so anchor your baseline to a repeatable window and traffic profile. This approach clarifies whether 320 MHz width is helping aggregate throughput or just making the clear channel assessment overlap too wide to coordinate neighbors.

Document constraints before touching configs, because 6 GHz rules and client support limit safe options. Inventory client radios for MLO capability, RU size support, and whether they honor BSS Coloring and OBSS power detect adjustments. Note regulatory EIRP caps for standard power or low power indoor devices to keep cells tight. As a rule of thumb, prefer lower EIRP with more APs to reduce contention domains, instead of high power that enlarges CCA footprints. This tradeoff sometimes demands more infrastructure, yet it avoids wide area talkers that block OFDMA gains. When 320 MHz is non negotiable, aim to confine overlap through careful placement and directional mounting where feasible indoors.

Choose a channel strategy that can be automated, because manual tweaks drift under changing neighbors. Map primary segments and potential puncture patterns so RRM or your automation can adapt without collapsing service. If your environment routinely hosts dense clients that transmit small frames, wide lanes can worsen CCA overlap unless OFDMA scheduling is consistent. Planning enforces triggers, such as falling back to 160 MHz during specific busy windows when latency targets slip.

Prepare environment

Align radio capabilities with your design so the network can execute the plan. Confirm AP support for 320 MHz, OFDMA scheduling, RU puncturing controls, BSS Coloring, and OBSS PD tuning. Validate clients at the chipset family level so you know which features are safe to use. For example, if a meaningful percentage of voice endpoints lack MLO or smaller RUs, enable OFDMA but keep puncturing conservative. The tradeoff is slightly lower peak throughput in exchange for steadier latency under mixed loads. This alignment avoids enabling advanced features that some endpoints partially support, which can yield inconsistent communication patterns that feel like intermittent contention.

Set power and primary channels intentionally to limit contention domains, then let automation maintain them. Cap EIRP to a value that keeps median neighbor counts modest, often requiring indoor power below the regulatory maximum. This keeps the clear channel assessment range tighter, which allows multiple basic service sets to transmit concurrently. Choose distinct primary segments with enough separation to reduce two APs choosing the same primary under dynamic bandwidth operation. If your controller supports policy based primary selection, use it to prefer less occupied segments. A limitation is that dynamic policies can flap without hysteresis. Ensure any automation includes decay timers and change windows.

Represent these decisions as code so you can audit changes and roll back cleanly. A simple declarative snippet can capture width, primary, puncturing, and fairness weights for a given site. Apply it through your normal automation tooling so all APs converge consistently. Expect more stable behavior because drift and quiet UI edits are eliminated. The downside is a steeper initial setup, yet the traceability quickly pays off during troubleshooting. Execute steps

Apply changes in an order that preserves service so contention does not worsen during transition. Stage policies on a small AP set during a scheduled window and enable OFDMA first, because better scheduling reduces contention even before width changes. Next, set primary segments and EIRP caps, then introduce puncturing patterns with strict limits. Consider scheduling fallback conditions inside the controller to revert to 160 MHz if latency percentiles exceed thresholds. Finally, communicate the change plan to stakeholders, including service owners for voice and video. Explain expected behavior shifts, like steadier latency with slightly lower top end throughput due to conservative puncturing. Set a monitoring window and name the exact metrics and pass thresholds you will watch. This transparency avoids rollback pressure based on momentary speed test results. It also creates a shared understanding that reducing contention is about predictable performance under load. The tradeoff is spending extra time up front on communication, yet it consistently reduces escalations and change friction when users feel informed and able to compare outcomes to a clear baseline.

Validate results

Validate results using repeatable tests that match your workloads, because synthetic throughput alone hides contention. Capture pre and post 50th and 95th percentile latencies for voice or transactional apps, along with station retry rates and scheduling efficiency reported by the controller. For example, in a busy hour sample, look for p95 latency reductions and fewer medium busy indications while OFDMA use increases. If throughput dips slightly yet latency steadies, that is a successful trade for most user experiences. The limit of this approach is it depends on representative traffic, so coordinate with app owners to generate realistic flows rather than only iPerf tests that bias toward large frames.

Correlate spatial factors to verify that power and primary choices actually reduced CCA overlap. Use floor plan heatmaps of BSS Color distribution, neighbor counts, and channel busy time to spot unintended high talkers. A quick win is finding APs that still see too many same color neighbors near their primary segment. Correct by nudging EIRP down or reassigning the primary for those cells. This fixes contention at the source rather than treating symptoms. The tradeoff is investing time in mapping and interpretation, yet it prevents endless policy tweaks that do not address geometry. Geometry beats settings when it comes to medium sharing in ultra wide lanes.

Close the loop by documenting pass or fail against your initial guardrails and updating the automation policy. Persist the measured thresholds and any exceptions into the code comment header or a change log in your repository. This creates a living record that explains why OBSS PD value or puncturing mask changed, and under which conditions fallback should trigger. It also simplifies audits months later when staff or client mix changes. Troubleshoot and optimize

When results disappoint, treat symptoms as clues rather than reasons to undo 320 MHz entirely. If small clients stall, reduce OBSS PD aggressiveness and verify RUs are not too large for their capabilities. If voice sees jitter spikes, tighten airtime weights or reduce puncturing to restore a more contiguous RU grid. In mixed client environments, consider pinning voice SSIDs to 160 MHz while keeping best effort on 320 MHz. This hybrid model often improves net experience. The tradeoff is added design complexity and more profiles to maintain. Still, it allows you to serve legacy radios without sacrificing the benefits that modern clients gain from wide lanes with good scheduling.

Address noisy neighbors and unpredictable bursts systematically. First, confirm whether interference is internal by temporarily disabling a subset of APs and rechecking busy time. If external, schedule narrower operation during peak interference windows through your fallback policy. Next, audit automation flaps. If primaries or power move too often, extend hysteresis and change intervals. The goal is stability that users can depend on. Finally, revisit antenna patterns and placement for outlier APs, since even indoor environments can have reflective corridors that inflate CCA reach. Physical adjustments remain a potent way to reduce contention without touching policy.

When you need sustained reliability for sensitive apps, pair these optimizations with access policy and security that reinforce predictable airtime. Rate limit guest SSIDs, disable multicast where not required, and ensure fast transition settings are consistent. Align QoS markings end to end so controller queues honor voice and video.   1. Baseline metrics: record p50 and p95 latency and airtime by BSS.

  4. Choose primaries: assign non overlapping primary segments for neighboring cells.

Looking ahead

Controlling contention on 320 MHz WiFi is an ongoing practice, not a single change. As new clients introduce different MLO and RU behaviors, revisit your guardrails and automation every quarter. Add site specific puncture patterns where noise is consistent, and expand hybrid width use in areas with legacy devices. Continue to measure latency percentiles as your north star and evolve weights, OBSS PD, and EIRP with caution. When your environment changes materially, repeat the plan, prepare, execute, and validate loop to keep results predictable. This cadence builds confidence that wide lanes are a benefit for users rather than a periodic source of instability.

Frequently asked questions

Should I use 320 MHz everywhere or only in some areas?

Use 320 MHz where client density and feature support justify it. In mixed areas, run hybrid widths by SSID or zone to keep latency predictable for sensitive apps.

How aggressive should OBSS PD be on 6 GHz with wide lanes?

Increase modestly to shrink the contention domain without starving edge clients. Validate with latency percentiles and adjust only when retries and jitter remain acceptable.

Does puncturing always help with interference on 320 MHz?

Puncturing helps when a few subchannels are noisy. Overuse reduces capacity and RU density. Start conservatively and expand only when measurements show sustained improvement.

What metrics prove contention actually dropped after changes?

Look for lower p95 latency, reduced medium busy time, improved OFDMA utilization, and lower retry rates during the same busy hour compared to baseline.

When should I fall back to 160 MHz widths?

Trigger fallback when p95 latency exceeds your threshold or when retries spike for sensitive apps. Automate the condition so reversion happens predictably.
