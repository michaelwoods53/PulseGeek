Does 320 MHz Increase Range?

By Rachel Morgan • Published January 10, 2026

Short answer first. 320 MHz does not increase range. Wider bandwidth raises the noise floor and requires higher signal to maintain a given modulation and coding scheme, so usable distance often shrinks, not grows. You gain peak throughput when signal to noise ratio is excellent and interference is controlled, but you trade coverage headroom. This article explains why bandwidth affects SNR and MCS, where 320 MHz delivers value, and how to validate with surveys and throughput testing. You will also see when to prefer narrower channels for reliability and how to recognize client behavior that masks range limits in 6 GHz.

Key takeaways

  • Bandwidth does not extend range because wider lanes raise the noise floor.
  • Use 320 MHz only where SNR is strong and interference is tightly bounded.
  • Validate with repeatable RSSI, MCS, and bidirectional throughput measurements.
  • Fallback to 160 or 80 MHz when data rates collapse at target edges.
  • Client steering and MLO can mask coverage gaps without improving range.

Short answer and nuance

Range is governed by link budget and required SNR for the target MCS, and widening to 320 MHz does not add energy to the signal. Because noise power scales with bandwidth, increasing from 80 to 320 MHz raises the noise floor by roughly the sum of octave steps, requiring more received power to hold the same MCS. In practice, that means the distance at which a given throughput is sustainable becomes shorter unless you accept lower rates. When RF conditions are excellent, 320 MHz can deliver striking peak throughput, but only within a tighter radius. The physics do not change just because channels are wider, so plan for performance zones rather than assuming broader lanes stretch coverage footprints.

A practical way to think about this is target data rate versus minimum SNR. For example, if your design goal is consistent high-order modulation near the cell edge, doubling and redoubling bandwidth forces the client to fight higher integrated noise across the channel. You can offset some of this by adding spatial streams or leveraging beamforming gains, yet those tools also hinge on clean SNR and stable multipath. As width increases, the margin for fading and body loss narrows. This is why well engineered networks reserve ultra-wide operation for spaces where geometry, materials, and density yield stable high SNR rather than attempting one width everywhere.

Interference management also grows harder with 320 MHz because adjacent energy and partial-band blockers occupy more spectrum relative to your lane. Co-channel contention scales with how many APs share the same wide resource, while adjacent interference can clip subchannels and reduce effective MCS. Features like preamble puncturing help salvage capacity when specific resource units are unusable, but they do not change the fundamental SNR requirement across the remaining band. Treat 320 MHz as a throughput tool for short, clean paths and ensure roaming devices have viable options on narrower channels where signal conditions vary or clients carry legacy radios.

When it applies vs when it does not

320 MHz fits when you control geometry, client mix, and airtime so SNR remains strong throughout the served area. Think short rooms, line-of-sight seating, or device pods within a single open space where the AP can be placed overhead and power set modestly. In these cases, you harvest headline throughput in bursts like large file transfers or short renders, while latency sensitive control traffic uses smaller resource units under OFDMA. This setup fails in long corridors, obstructed layouts, or multiroom floors where signals must penetrate walls. There, edge devices drop MCS early, and retries erase any theoretical gain, making narrower channels the safer default.

Client capability is another limiter because not all radios support 320 MHz, and those that do may prefer narrower widths when roaming or conserving power. Mixed fleets with older 6 GHz devices will commonly negotiate 160 or 80 MHz, which instantly changes your interference profile and capacity plan. If your performance objective depends on every client holding wide lanes, your design is brittle. A better approach is optional width: allow capable devices to burst at 320 MHz near the AP, while the rest operate efficiently on narrower lanes without starving. This reduces surprises when firmware or drivers evolve and behavior shifts under load.

Regulatory and environmental constraints further decide suitability. Power spectral density rules often spread total transmit power across the chosen bandwidth, so widening can reduce per-hertz energy and effective reach. Building materials like low-e glass, dense drywall, and metal shelving attenuate 6 GHz faster than many planners expect, compounding the SNR hit that comes from width. If your survey shows margins under 10 dB at the intended service edge for the target MCS, 320 MHz is a gamble. Start with narrower lanes, then trial widening in controlled pockets where measurements prove stability and client logs confirm steady MCS under movement.

Alternatives and related questions

If range is the priority, narrower channels often outperform because they lower the noise floor and keep higher MCS alive farther from the AP. 160 or 80 MHz can deliver stable rates across larger footprints while still providing ample capacity when client counts scale. You can pair narrower width with more APs at lower power to reduce contention and improve spatial reuse. Consider features like OFDMA scheduling to serve many modest flows efficiently. When interference is a concern, review practical steps to limit co-channel and adjacent issues at 320 MHz in 6 GHz so you can compare designs that mix widths without unpredictable airtime loss across the floor.

Another alternative is selective widening with policy. Enable 320 MHz on specific radios where geometry supports it, and keep neighbors on 160 or 80 MHz to bound contention domains. Use dynamic bandwidth algorithms cautiously because frequent width changes can confuse rate adaptation and skew roaming decisions. Multi-Link Operation can help with reliability by allowing devices to maintain flows across multiple links, yet it does not extend the RF reach of any single channel. Your design still lives or dies on SNR and clean spectrum. Where segmentation is tricky, trial preamble puncturing to route around known blockers while preserving usable airtime.

Frequently asked questions

Does 320 MHz improve WiFi range at the same transmit power?

No. Wider bandwidth raises the noise floor, so more SNR is required for a given MCS. With equal power, usable distance usually decreases unless you accept lower data rates.

When should I use 320 MHz channels in 6 GHz?

Use 320 MHz only in high SNR areas with short paths and controlled interference. Favor spaces with clear line of sight and minimal attenuation where devices can sustain high-order modulation.

Will preamble puncturing increase range for 320 MHz?

No. Puncturing avoids specific interferers by disabling parts of the channel. It can preserve throughput in noisy bands, but it does not change the SNR needed for range.

Does Multi-Link Operation help extend coverage distance?

Not directly. MLO can improve reliability and throughput by using multiple links, yet each link still follows the same SNR and path loss constraints that define range.

Should I lower transmit power to make 320 MHz work better?

Lower power can shrink contention domains and improve spatial reuse, but it does not add range. Set power to match your cell size and ensure edges maintain the target MCS.
