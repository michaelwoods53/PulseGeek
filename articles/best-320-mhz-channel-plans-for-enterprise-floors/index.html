Best 320 MHz Channel Plans for Enterprise Floors

By Rachel Morgan • Published January 8, 2026

These recommendations focus on 320 MHz channel plans that hold up on enterprise floors, not just lab benches. Each item describes where a plan fits, when puncturing and Multi Link Operation help, and how to tune power and placement. I prioritized repeatable designs that reduce interference risk across varied office layouts while keeping 6 GHz throughput predictable for modern clients.

Key takeaways

  • Start with floor segmentation before enabling any 320 MHz width.
  • Use puncturing to dodge incumbents without collapsing capacity.
  • Right size transmit power and TPC to stabilize airtime fairness.
  • Steer MLO traffic across links to protect wide lanes during peaks.
  • Adopt exception-based enabling rather than global 320 MHz policies.

1) Segment by floor quadrants

Segmenting a floor into RF quadrants reduces co-channel interference and preserves 320 MHz airtime. The claim is simple: physical segmentation plus channel reuse boundaries create cleaner contention domains. Picture a large office floor split into east and west halves by fire corridors, then further into north and south zones using stair cores as RF baffles. Assign a single 320 MHz group to each quadrant so neighboring areas do not share the same primary channel. This approach stabilizes throughput for client groups that rarely roam across partitions. The tradeoff is reduced flexibility when space use shifts, since quadrant boundaries can become misaligned with new seating or densification. You also need careful AP mounting to keep main lobes contained inside each quadrant, otherwise bleed through erodes the isolation you worked to create.

In practice, use structural elements as boundaries, then pin APs inboard to contain signals and keep primary channels unique per quadrant. For example, designate UNII‑5 groupings on the east side and UNII‑7 on the west, ensuring nonadjacent primaries at the quadrant edges. This keeps wide lanes separated so a single talker does not throttle the whole floor. The limitation is that not all buildings provide convenient baffles, so you might need to add directional antennas to strengthen isolation.

2) Selective 320 MHz enablement

Enable 320 MHz only in zones where client density and application mix justify ultra wide lanes. The rule of thumb is to turn it on for media labs, conference clusters, or collaboration areas showing sustained high PHY rates, and keep adjacent zones at 160 MHz or 80 MHz to cap interference. Imagine two adjacent neighborhoods on a floor: a huddle area with video boards and a quiet work zone with laptops and VoIP. You could enable 320 MHz only across the huddle APs and leave the quiet zone at 160 MHz, minimizing cross talk. The tradeoff is administrative complexity since mixed widths require careful roaming tests and controller policies. Also, some clients may prefer wider channels but cannot sustain them, leading to transient retries unless you enforce a minimum MCS policy or rate limit background devices at busy hours.

Controller profiles can enforce width by AP group so changes remain auditable and reversible. Combine this with Multi Link Operation steering so latency sensitive flows can move onto narrower, less contended links when the wide lane saturates. The drawback is that per zone policies increase the risk of drift between maps and controller configs, which you mitigate by tying AP names to floor plan coordinates and enforcing change windows for any width adjustments.

3) Preamble puncturing as a guardrail

Preamble puncturing preserves usable airtime when parts of a 320 MHz block face interference or incumbents. The claim is that dynamic nulling of busy Resource Units prevents a full width collapse into retries. As an example, if an AFC update restricts slices near microwave links or a neighboring tenant runs high power in a subset of subchannels, puncturing lets your AP advertise a trimmed but still wide channel. This reduces collisions while holding aggregate throughput closer to expected levels. The tradeoff is scheduling complexity, since clients and APs must agree on the available pieces, and some legacy devices may not adapt cleanly. You also need reliable telemetry to avoid over-puncturing, since removing too many parts can cause fragmentation that undercuts latency sensitive traffic and makes debugging more opaque for field engineers.

Operationally, adopt thresholds that trigger puncturing only when error rates or clear channel assessment failures cross a stable baseline for multiple intervals. Combine this with alerting that flags which subchannels were nulled and for how long, so you can correlate with site events. When planning, compare subchannel maps against corridor walls and elevator shafts to reduce exposure. The caveat is that puncturing cannot fix persistent wideband noise or poor AP placement, so start with placement and power before relying on dynamic nulling.

4) Primary channel alignment

Aligning primary channels across reuse boundaries reduces backoff storms and shortens contention windows. The claim is that primary alignment ensures neighboring quadrants do not share the same primary control channel, which is where most management and initial control traffic lands. For example, in a two quadrant design, assign a lower primary in the east and a higher primary in the west within the same 320 MHz mask so beacons and association bursts do not stack up. This improves discovery and stabilizes roaming because clients see less simultaneous primary activity. The tradeoff is that misalignment during maintenance can reintroduce beacon overlap, so you need validation after any controller push. Additionally, some clients might prefer a specific primary due to driver heuristics, producing transient band steering artifacts that must be smoothed with consistent probe response settings.

Implement a simple rule: never reuse the same primary on facing edges, and audit primaries after AFC list changes. Place APs so antennas face inward, keeping primary leakage away from corridor seams. If a tenant next door uses 6 GHz, verify your primaries do not sit adjacent to theirs around elevator banks. For sustained alignment, schedule quarterly site surveys focused on discovery traffic, not just throughput. The limitation is that primary tuning alone cannot offset poor height or tilt, so include a mechanical check along with the channel plan audit.

5) TPC and power right sizing

Right sizing transmit power and Transmit Power Control stabilizes airtime fairness for 320 MHz. The claim is that wide channels do not need maximal power to deliver expected throughput inside office footprints. For instance, lowering 6 GHz downlink power 3 to 6 dB relative to 5 GHz often keeps cells compact, reduces sticky clients, and protects reuse boundaries. A practical target is matching power to the smallest cell that sustains your minimum MCS under typical load. This reduces talker dominance when a single client grabs the medium. The tradeoff is that dropping power too far can create coverage holes for legacy devices with weaker uplinks. You must also consider emergency roaming paths around elevator banks, where a small power change may strand devices during handoff if thresholds are misconfigured or if retry limits are too strict.

Use staged TPC with audit logs so changes apply gradually and remain traceable. Validate with bidirectional tests that include uplink, since client radios usually control range. In dense areas, cap maximum power on APs facing corridors and allow marginally higher settings for interior nodes to reduce seam interference. The downside is operational overhead, since staged policies require scheduled windows and rollbacks, but that cost is modest compared to weeks of unstable performance from overpowered wide lanes in mixed client fleets.

  • Stage TPC changes with uplink validation to avoid hidden holes.

6) MLO path steering

Steering Multi Link Operation paths protects wide 320 MHz lanes during peaks by shifting certain flows to alternate links. The claim is that MLO-aware scheduling keeps latency sensitive or small burst traffic on a narrower but quiet link while bulk transfers use the wide lane, reducing head of line blocking. Consider a design where conference rooms support MLO on both 6 GHz and 5 GHz: video conferencing control and audio can be steered to 5 GHz during bursts while screen share and file sync occupy the 6 GHz wide lane. This reduces jitter without sacrificing throughput. The tradeoff is client diversity, since not all devices implement robust MLO policies. You also risk path instability if the controller overreacts to transient signals, so rate smoothing and hysteresis are essential to prevent ping pong behavior that frustrates application flows.

Start with threshold-based steering that triggers only when queue depth or medium busy exceeds a consistent baseline over several seconds. Make the policy asymmetric so return to 320 MHz is slower than departure, stabilizing oscillations. In shared office towers, coordinate with building IT on 6 GHz etiquette so neighboring tenants do not inadvertently swamp your preferred paths. The limitation is that MLO cannot fix badly sized cells, so make steering the second step after segmentation and power have been validated with roaming walk tests.

7) AFC aware channel mapping

AFC awareness keeps 320 MHz usable by aligning channel maps with allowed power and incumbent protection constraints. The claim is that reading the current Automated Frequency Coordination results per AP avoids picking 320 MHz groups that will be throttled or restricted after audits. For example, an indoor floor near a fixed service receiver may face EIRP caps in parts of UNII‑7. Choosing a 320 MHz grouping that lives mostly in less constrained spectrum reduces future adjustments. You should also precompute fallbacks if AFC revisions remove subchannels. The tradeoff is potential fragmentation across a campus, since different buildings may end up with distinct preferred groupings. That complicates roaming profiles and might require per building policy objects rather than a single global 6 GHz template across every controller domain you manage.

Designate one preferred 320 MHz group and one secondary that avoids known incumbents, then codify both in your RF policy so rollbacks are a one click change. Validate each map with a limited pilot before broad enablement to catch unexpected constraints or neighbor activity.
