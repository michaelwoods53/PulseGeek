Mitigating 6 GHz CCI and ACI: Practical Playbook

By Olivia Adams • Published January 14, 2026

Mitigating 6 GHz interference starts with understanding how CCI and ACI appear under real traffic, not just heatmaps. This playbook aims to reduce co-channel contention while limiting adjacent-channel bleed through disciplined channelization and power control. We assume enterprise APs with Wi‑Fi 7 or Wi‑Fi 8 features, multi‑floor coverage, and clients that may not all support the same widths. You will plan with PSC awareness, set practical bandwidths, and validate airtime outcomes before scaling. The steps emphasize stable roaming and predictable latency over peak benchmarks because consistency matters most to applications. Tradeoffs are explicit, such as narrower channels yielding steadier performance when noise or neighbor activity rises. By the end, you can translate design choices into measurable differences that persist across locations and weeks of usage.

Key takeaways

  • Plan widths with PSC alignment to reduce scan delays and CCI.
  • Right-size power so cells meet but rarely overlap across floors.
  • Favor fewer, cleaner channels over fragile peak throughput claims.
  • Validate with airtime, retries, and MCS spread, not speed tests.
  • Adjust adjacent channels where ACI spikes during busy intervals.

Plan the work

Start by defining success in terms of airtime fairness and predictable latency because 6 GHz CCI and ACI hinge on contention and leakage. For example, a VoIP target could be median one‑way latency below 30 ms and retry rates under five percent during busy hours. Translate these into design constraints like maximal transmit power, preferred width, and maximum AP overlap area. Edge cases include mixed client capabilities that force conservative widths to reduce re‑transmissions. Scope the area, noting vertical adjacency to understand floor‑to‑floor coupling. The aim is to limit co‑channel overlap to small handoff zones while reserving adjacent channels for true concurrency. Planning this way clarifies which metrics to monitor later and how to justify narrower channels if noise rises.

Choose channel widths with intent rather than defaulting to the widest option because adjacent-channel interference grows with spectral occupancy. As a rule of thumb, 80 MHz works for moderate density, while 40 MHz fits high density or heavy overlapping floors. In very busy venues, 20 MHz may outperform wider lanes by lowering collisions and stabilizing MCS selection. When balancing capacity against isolation, account for device realities such as client support for 320 MHz being limited. Review regional rules to confirm availability and power constraints before assigning lanes. A tradeoff appears when fewer wide channels exist, making reuse harder and increasing CCI risk. Plan a small palette of lanes that you can reuse predictably without creating hidden overlaps.

Anchor planning with PSCs to accelerate discovery and maintain roaming predictability, then spread PSC channels to avoid crowding. Preferred Scanning Channels keep initial probe costs reasonable and shape where clients dwell while searching. In dense designs, place service sets on PSC‑aligned centers first, then fill non‑PSC secondaries only as needed. If you target multi‑link operation, ensure primary links follow this pattern so association time stays consistent. The tradeoff is that PSCs can attract many SSIDs if you are not disciplined with reuse spacing, which can inflate probe traffic and re‑scan delays.

Prepare environment

Begin with a passive and active survey to capture the noise floor, neighbor occupancy, and bursty interferers because baseline truth informs all later choices. Collect at least a weekday busy hour and a quieter morning sample to confirm patterns. Inspect retry rate spikes and degraded MCS ranges that might indicate ACI during adjacent AP backoffs. If you find variable noise above normal thermal levels for long spans, plan narrower widths or adjusted channels for those zones. Edge cases include intermittent radar or non‑Wi‑Fi sources that confuse measurements, so validate with multiple tools where possible. Record floor attenuation between AP locations to estimate vertical reuse feasibility. These inputs let you model realistic cell edges and set initial power caps before moving to configuration.

Inventory client capabilities so width and MLO plans reflect actual devices instead of wishful assumptions. Gather supported bandwidths, MCS tables, and whether the clients prefer PSCs for faster scanning. A simple rule is to design for the 50th to 70th percentile client rather than the newest flagship so the median user benefits. If many clients are 2x2 without 320 MHz support, wider lanes add little and may increase ACI. Note power save behaviors that can shift association after idle intervals, which affects roaming stability. Identify any client categories with known quirks, such as strict DFS avoidance or slow update cycles. This set of facts narrows viable options and preempts surprises when you later validate under real traffic.

Create a reproducible lab profile before production by configuring an AP with conservative power, PSC‑aligned channels, and your candidate width. Execute steps

Channelize methodically to shrink co‑channel overlap while preserving enough lanes for reuse. Start by laying PSC‑centered channels across nonadjacent APs so immediate neighbors are separated. Where floors couple strongly, rotate lanes vertically to avoid stacking identical channels above each other. In higher density areas, prefer 40 or 80 MHz rather than 160 or 320 because the latter reduce reuse diversity and raise ACI risk. If you must deploy a very wide channel for a lab or specialty zone, isolate it physically and cap power more aggressively. For practical reuse patterns including PSC spacing and width strategies, see this comprehensive guide to lanes, reuse, and timing in 6 GHz. Document the layout so future adds maintain the pattern.

Set transmit power to meet at the edges rather than overlap deeply because CCI grows with cell size. A starting heuristic is to cap 6 GHz around mid‑teens dBm EIRP for indoor enterprise gear, then refine per site survey and building materials. Reduce power on APs near open atriums or glass walls to avoid spray into adjacent spaces. Raise power cautiously where walls are dense and absorption is high, watching that roaming delay does not increase. If clients begin hanging onto distant APs, use band‑steering rules and minimum RSSI thresholds sparingly, since aggressive enforcement can induce oscillation. Record client RSSI around intended roaming points to ensure smooth transitions at consistent levels across floors and corridors.

Keep SSID count minimal and align primary services to PSC channels so clients spend less time scanning and more time transmitting. More beacons consume airtime and raise the chance that probe bursts collide with data frames, increasing retries that look like ACI. If IoT or guest networks must exist, place them on less reused lanes or reduce their beacon rate within policy bounds. Ensure beacon intervals and DTIM settings are consistent per service type to avoid unexpected power save wake patterns that complicate roaming. For MLO, designate primary links on cleaner lanes to anchor association, while secondary links can explore broader widths where spectrum allows. This balances discovery speed with opportunistic concurrency without forcing every client to adopt the widest lane.

Validate results

Measure airtime, retries, and MCS spread during controlled traffic because these reveal interference more reliably than raw throughput. Generate steady downstream and upstream flows at realistic rates rather than saturating links. Track retry percentage, average MCS, and distribution tails to detect subtle CCI. If retries spike when adjacent APs transmit, investigate overlap or beacon storms that mask as ACI. Validate during busy hours and at two additional times to confirm repeatability. Edge cases include client drivers that under‑report MCS, so compare with AP counters and spectrum views. Pass criteria could be median retries under five percent and a stable MCS mode over contiguous minutes. Keep annotated traces to compare future changes against this baseline.

Verify roaming consistency along expected handoff lines because inconsistent transitions point to power or lane spacing issues. Walk predefined paths, initiating application flows like calls or teleconferences, and log RSSI, roam time, and packet loss. Watch for areas where clients cling to distant APs or jump across nonadjacent channels which hints at SSID crowding or secondary widths attracting scans. If roam latency exceeds targets, reduce transmit power or adjust lane assignments to remove ambiguous overlaps. Balance changes with vertical reuse strategy to avoid fixing one floor while harming another. Confirm outcomes by repeating the same path after adjustments so differences track directly to your edits rather than ambient variability.

Assess adjacent-channel behavior by placing test traffic on neighboring lanes and observing noise elevation and frame errors on the victim channel. The goal is to confirm that your width and separation choices keep ACI within safe margins while typical bursts occur. If the victim’s error rate rises when the neighbor bursts at peak, consider reducing width or moving one service to a quieter PSC lane. Troubleshoot and optimize

When retries climb suddenly in a zone, distinguish CCI from ACI by checking which transmitters are active and where the errors occur. If multiple APs on the same channel show concurrent activity and clients back off frequently, reduce power or swap one AP to a different lane. If errors rise primarily when an adjacent lane spikes, narrow the offending width or move one service to a quieter PSC. Consider beacon rate adjustments and SSID pruning to free airtime. Edge cases include event days where occupancy multiplies, so prepare alternate profiles you can schedule. The aim is a reversible change set that solves the specific interference mode without destabilizing neighbors or increasing roam delays elsewhere.

Address sticky roaming by shaping edges rather than forcing clients with rigid thresholds. First lower power slightly on the dominant APs, then observe if transitions normalize along intended lines. Only add minimum RSSI or probe response shaping if soft power edits fail because aggressive parameters can cause ping‑pong. For vertical issues, rotate or reassign one floor’s lanes to rebalance reuse. Track improvement with identical walk tests and keep a changelog with timestamps to correlate effects. If a few legacy clients misbehave with MLO or wider channels, create a limited SSID on a conservative width in hard areas. Retire exceptions once replacements are rolled out.

Finally, tune for concurrency by placing high‑duty services on cleaner lanes and scheduling heavy transfers outside peak user windows. If your platform supports it, prefer primary links on PSC channels and let secondary links chase wider lanes opportunistically based on current utilization. This approach limits ACI exposure while allowing bursts to complete faster when conditions are right. Monitor over weeks so that seasonal or tenant changes do not drift the interference picture. Frequently asked questions

How do I tell CCI from ACI in test results?

CCI appears as contention on the same channel across multiple APs with backoffs and retries. ACI shows errors or noise increases on a victim channel when a neighboring lane transmits. Compare counters and spectrum views during controlled traffic.

Should I ever use 160 or 320 MHz indoors?

Use very wide channels only when isolation is strong and client support is verified. Keep them localized, cap power more aggressively, and expect reuse to suffer. For general areas with neighbors or vertical coupling, 40 or 80 MHz is safer.

What is a safe starting transmit power for 6 GHz?

A mid-teens dBm EIRP cap is a common starting point for indoor enterprise APs. Validate per building materials and floor coupling, then adjust in small steps while measuring roaming and retry behavior along defined paths.

Do PSC channels always perform better?

PSCs improve discovery and roaming predictability, but they can get crowded if overused. Space them thoughtfully, align primary services first, and keep SSID count low to prevent scan traffic from consuming airtime.
