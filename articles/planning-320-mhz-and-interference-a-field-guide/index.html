WiFi 8 320 MHz Channel Planning and Interference Guide

By Rachel Morgan • Updated January 4, 2026

Ultra wide channels promise high throughput, but WiFi 8 planning decides whether 320 MHz becomes performance or interference. This guide shows where 320 MHz excels, how to contain side effects, and how to validate results without guesswork.

Key takeaways

  • Start with client mix, density, and airtime goals before widening channels.
  • Use measured interference domains to decide if 320 MHz fits the floor.
  • Apply AFC and puncturing rules to preserve usable 6 GHz spectrum.
  • Tune transmit power and dwell to stabilize multi link operation clients.
  • Validate with repeatable surveys that mirror peak duty and roaming paths.

Foundations

Begin with a clear premise: 320 MHz helps when spatial reuse is limited and peak throughput outweighs concurrency. In practice, that means wide lanes in areas with moderate AP density, shorter roaming paths, and clients that can exploit multi link operation. A typical example is a lab with a few heavy flows that benefit from large PHY rates, compared with a dense open office. The tradeoff is reduced channel count for reuse, which raises channel contention across adjoining cells. Decide by modeling simultaneous talkers against required median throughput, and by mapping physical attenuation. The why is simple: wider channels invite more overlap, so designs should increase isolation using walls, ceiling height, or selective channel assignment to keep contention domains small.

Define interference correctly: co channel contention limits airtime, while adjacent channel energy raises error rates. With 320 MHz, both risks expand because the spectral footprint is large and edges may sit near incumbents. Core practices

Treat interference domains as the unit of design, not the whole floor. Map where the same 320 MHz channel would be audible at or above medium clear channel assessment thresholds, and keep those areas small. A simple example is staggering AP placement vertically or using construction to break line of sight, which reduces co channel contention without adding noise. The tradeoff is path planning becomes tighter, so survey points should reflect true roaming routes. For methodical steps to reduce co channel and adjacent channel issues at 320 MHz in 6 GHz, apply guidance from this primer on interference mitigation strategies. The how comes down to restraint: fewer audible APs on the same channel, controlled power, and disciplined channel reuse.

Preamble puncturing is your friend when a narrow sub channel is dirty. By excluding the bad segment, you preserve the remainder of the 320 MHz channel without retreating to narrower widths. A useful scenario is an indoor space near a known incumbent where only a portion of the band shows elevated noise. The tradeoff is that not all clients handle puncturing equally, and aggregate rates drop when many segments are excluded. Power and dwell settings decide if multi link operation adds stability or chaos. Keep transmit power modest so roaming edges meet your target minimum MCS on both links, and limit probe dwell to shorten scan cycles on 6 GHz. A cautious starting point is setting transmit power at or just below 160 MHz profiles, then tuning by measuring edge SNR during busy hours. The limitation is that some controllers treat 320 MHz as a different policy object, so verify per band configurations actually stick. Pitfalls and edge cases

A frequent failure is assuming 320 MHz increases range. Channel width changes throughput potential, not free space path loss, so edges still depend on power, antennas, and environment. A straightforward example is seeing similar receive sensitivity thresholds at the cell edge for 160 and 320 MHz while retries rise due to higher noise bandwidth. The tradeoff is clear: wider channels can push marginal edges into lower MCS states if power is not tuned. Another trap appears when teams compare a single 320 MHz channel to coordinated 160 MHz across multiple APs and forget reuse effects. In open offices, two or more 160 MHz channels assigned intelligently can outperform one ultra wide domain because more clients transmit concurrently. A practical test is to measure median throughput during a busy hour with both designs on equivalent power settings. An edge case arises when client radios do not support 320 MHz but do fine at 160 MHz, tilting results further. Frequently asked questions

When does 320 MHz make sense over 160 MHz?

Choose 320 MHz when you have clean spectrum, limited co channel domains, and workloads that benefit from peak PHY rates more than concurrent talkers. Labs, controlled venues, or short roaming paths are typical fits. Dense open offices often favor coordinated 160 MHz.

Does 320 MHz improve range in 6 GHz?

No. Width affects potential throughput, not free space path loss. Range depends on transmit power, antennas, environment, and receiver sensitivity. Wider channels can increase noise bandwidth, which may reduce edge MCS if power and placement are not tuned carefully.

How should I set transmit power for 320 MHz?

Begin at or slightly below your 160 MHz power profile, then validate edge SNR and minimum MCS under busy hour load. Avoid overdriving cells, which expands contention domains and masks overlap problems. Adjust per band if your controller treats 320 MHz as a separate profile.

What if part of the 6 GHz band is noisy?

Use preamble puncturing to exclude the dirty sub channels while keeping the remaining bandwidth. Confirm client support and watch aggregate rates, since heavy puncturing lowers throughput. If noise persists across wide segments, reduce channel width and prioritize reuse.

How do AFC rules affect indoor planning?

AFC can limit available channels or power in some locales. During planning, check eligibility and expected power levels, then select channels that remain viable after coordination. Revalidate after policy updates to ensure previously chosen channels are still permitted.
