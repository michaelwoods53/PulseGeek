WFC Tile Constraints Examples: Patterns that Always Fit

By Jacob Reed • Published December 10, 2025

These WFC tile constraints examples focus on patterns that fit reliably while preserving variety. I selected techniques that show how constraints, tiles, and adjacency interact, each with a clear example and a measurable tradeoff. Items were chosen for coverage across edge handling, path continuity, symmetry, pruning, and ordering so you can combine them safely without brittle rules. Where configuration matters, I include a minimal encoding or a decision heuristic. Key takeaways

  • Socket based constraints localize compatibility and scale cleanly with tiles.
  • Border and frame rules prevent leakage while preserving interior variety.
  • Path tags ensure continuity without micromanaging full route geometry.
  • Symmetry handling reduces assets while keeping patterns consistent.
  • Entropy heuristics guide collapse order to minimize contradictions.

1) Socket based adjacency matrix

Define tile compatibility with sockets to encode adjacency once per edge type, not per neighbor pair. Assign each tile four edge labels such as road, grass, or wall, then allow adjacency where opposite sockets match a simple relation like equal or inverse. For example, a straight road tile with east and west sockets road aligns with another road tile but not with water. This cuts the constraint table from quadratic pairs to a small dictionary of socket types, which scales as you add tiles. The tradeoff is granularity. If sockets are too coarse, you permit awkward joins like road-to-bridge without supports. If they are too fine, you explode the type count and slow propagation. A practical rule groups macro semantics first, then refines with a limited set of subtypes.

When you adopt sockets, also encode rotations to avoid duplicating tiles. Instead of separate north-road and east-road sprites, store a canonical sprite plus rotation metadata and rotate sockets accordingly. As a concrete pattern, a T-junction can be represented once with sockets road on three edges and grass on the fourth, then rotated to produce all orientations. This reduces asset management and keeps the adjacency matrix compact. The tradeoff is that rotation may introduce asymmetries in art such as shadows or decals that do not rotate cleanly. To mitigate, either provide rotation-safe variants or disable rotation for specific assets by marking them non-rotatable in metadata and supplying explicit orientations only where needed.

2) Border and frame rules

Constrain map edges with frame tiles to prevent leaks where interior-only tiles would spill off the map or connect to nothing. A common technique whitelists a small set of perimeter tiles whose outward sockets match a virtual boundary like void or cliff, while inner sockets still match normal neighbors. For example, a coastal map might enforce water or cliff tiles along the outermost ring so grass cannot appear at the edge. The gain is stability and fewer contradictions near borders. The tradeoff is visible repetition if the frame set is too small or if rotations are disabled. To balance, prepare two to three visually distinct border variants per edge type and randomize among them while keeping sockets compatible with the outside.

Corner handling often breaks naive frame rules, so treat corners as first-class tiles with shaped sockets that resolve both axes. For instance, allow a convex cliff corner that matches cliff sockets on north and west while exposing void on the outer corner. This avoids L-shaped artifacts that violate your visual language. A small rule of thumb sets corner weights slightly higher than straight edges to seed clean frames early in collapse, which reduces backtracking later. The tradeoff is that over-weighted corners can overconstrain adjacent cells and reduce interior variety. If that happens, lower corner weights or delay frame enforcement until a shallow band inside the outer ring has collapsed.

Section highlights

  • Whitelist perimeter tiles whose outward sockets match a virtual boundary.
  • Treat corners as special tiles and tune their weights carefully.

3) Path continuity with tags

Use path tags to enforce connectivity without dictating full route geometry. Instead of a single road socket, attach a continuity tag like path=A to all tiles that must connect into one network and require that tag to propagate across adjacencies. For example, intersections, straights, and turns would carry path=A, while decorative cobbles carry path=B to form a separate network. This guarantees that every collapsed road cell remains reachable within its network. The tradeoff is that strict continuity can dominate the layout, especially on small maps where one network consumes most low-entropy cells. To counterbalance, cap the maximum fraction of cells tagged with a given network or introduce occasional sink tiles that terminate paths gracefully with cul-de-sac graphics.

Dead ends are a frequent edge case when the solver collapses a low-degree cell too early. A practical mitigation pairs tags with degree constraints, such as requiring at least two incident path sockets for interior road tiles and allowing one for ends next to landmarks. In practice, a turn tile might be illegal unless both participating edges remain open during candidate evaluation. This prunes dangling path fragments and reduces later contradictions. The tradeoff is that degree checks slightly increase compute per propagation step and can prematurely reject creative side branches. Consider enabling the degree rule only after a minimum path length is achieved, which preserves early exploration and saves pruning for consolidation.

Section highlights

  • Attach continuity tags to networks to keep roads or pipes connected.
  • Combine tags with degree rules to prune dangling fragments safely.

4) Symmetry and rotation policies

Codify rotational and reflective symmetry to reduce assets while preserving consistent patterns. If a tile is fourfold symmetric like a plus-shaped hub, treat all rotations as equivalent and avoid duplicating weights. If it is only bilateral, permit 180-degree rotations but not 90-degree ones to maintain art fidelity. As a concrete example, city blocks with directional markings should not mirror because text and arrows invert, while stone patterns can rotate freely. The benefit is fewer unique adjacency entries and easier balancing across tiles. The tradeoff is lost microvariation from subtle art differences between orientations. To reintroduce variety without exploding constraints, create cosmetic variants that share sockets and symmetry flags but differ in textures, decals, or wear patterns.

Symmetry also shapes entropy behavior because fewer orientations reduce candidate counts in many cells. This can bias collapse toward symmetric tiles, crowding out asymmetric landmarks. A balancing tactic assigns slightly lower weights to highly symmetric filler tiles so asymmetric features retain presence. For example, give a symmetric cobble tile weight 0.8 and an asymmetric fountain weight 1.2 within the same socket class, keeping overall compatibility intact. The cost is manual tuning and playtesting to verify distribution feels natural. To streamline, sample outputs and track frequency histograms per tile class, then nudge weights iteratively until the observed mix aligns with your design target across several seeds.

Section highlights

  • Define which rotations or mirrors are valid per tile to cut assets.
  • Rebalance weights so symmetric fillers do not overwhelm landmarks.

5) Forbidden pairs and soft penalties

Introduce forbidden pairs to ban specific adjacencies that slip through coarse sockets, and use soft penalties when you want rarity rather than impossibility. For instance, water next to lava might be a hard no because their visual interfaces lack a mixing tile, while industrial next to park is allowed but undesirable. Implement hard bans by removing candidate pairs from edge compatibility tables. Implement soft discouragement by reducing weights when the last placed neighbor matches a risky context, which steers collapse away without forcing backtracking. The tradeoff is complexity in debugging, because weight shifts can explain outcomes only probabilistically. Keep a log of applied penalties and sample seeds to verify emergent frequencies match your intent.

Edge cases appear when multiple soft penalties combine and unintentionally zero out a tile’s effective weight. To prevent accidental impossibility, normalize weights after penalties and enforce a minimum floor such as five percent of the original unless a hard ban applies. As a concrete example, if a residential facade near a highway and a factory collects two penalties, it still remains available for rare grit. The benefit is occasional surprise that remains believable. The cost is that rare configurations can still produce hot spots that some players dislike. Offer a seed option that narrows randomness for more predictable layouts, and document that wider ranges yield more eclectic neighborhoods. 6) Macro tiles and multi scale structure

Use macro tiles to encode structure beyond local edges by composing small patches into larger motifs that act as single tiles. A four by four plaza with a fountain in the center can be a macro tile whose border sockets align with streets while its interior guarantees a coherent pattern. This injects medium range order that vanilla local constraints cannot guarantee. The tradeoff is reduced recombinability because macro tiles cover more area and limit fine grained variation. A pragmatic approach keeps macro tiles sparse and mixes sizes so they seed anchor features without dominating. Also ensure macro tiles expose enough border diversity to avoid checkerboard repetition when multiple instances align.

Macro tiles interact with backtracking cost because contradictions in larger placements invalidate more cells. To mitigate, gate macro placements behind higher entropy thresholds so they collapse only when enough context exists to verify compatibility. For example, place small two by two motifs early, then reserve four by four plazas for later steps. This reduces failure cascades and preserves solver performance. The downside is scheduling complexity and the need for more metadata, such as minimum surrounding context or preferred neighbors. Keep the metadata compact by reusing socket vocabulary at the macro level and limiting special cases.   • Schedule large motifs later to reduce contradictions and rollbacks.

7) Entropy ordering and ties

Choose entropy heuristics that minimize contradictions without starving expressive cells. The common rule selects the lowest entropy cell first, breaking ties by weight or randomness. This focuses pruning where uncertainty is small, avoiding costly speculative choices. For example, in a road network, corners near the frame often reach low entropy early and collapsing them stabilizes the neighborhood. The tradeoff is tunnel vision in open interiors where entropy stays high and the solver delays important commitments. To address this, mix global and local criteria by occasionally promoting a high impact cell such as one adjacent to multiple tags. A simple schedule uses a low entropy pick nine times and a strategic pick once.

Tie breaking subtly shapes layouts. If you always break ties by lowest weight sum you risk overusing rare tiles because their candidates look stable. If you always break randomly you introduce noise that can stall convergence. A balanced approach alternates tie strategies per step or per region. For example, prefer weight-based ties near borders to clean edges and prefer randomness in interiors to foster diversity. The cost is variability across seeds that complicates QA. To manage, generate batches with fixed seeds to compare solver behavior under each policy.   • Blend low entropy selection with strategic picks for high impact cells.
  • Alternate tie rules by region to balance stability and variety.

Frequently asked questions

How many sockets should a tile set start with?

Begin with a small vocabulary that reflects core semantics like road, grass, and wall. Expand only when you see ambiguous joins. More than a dozen types early on slows iteration and complicates debugging.

Should rotations be separate tiles or metadata?

Use metadata when art supports rotation to reduce duplication and simplify weights. If art includes directional features that break under rotation, provide explicit oriented variants and mark them non rotatable.

Why does my solver create many dead ends?

Dead ends often arise from early collapse of low degree cells without continuity checks. Add degree constraints for path tiles and delay path heavy decisions until enough neighbors are known.

When should I use macro tiles instead of more rules?

Choose macro tiles when local constraints cannot enforce a medium scale motif like plazas or courtyards. Keep them sparse and schedule larger motifs later to avoid contradictions and heavy backtracking.
