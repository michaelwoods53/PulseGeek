Deterministic Latency with EHT for AR and VR

By Courtney Lawson • Published February 17, 2026

Deterministic latency in AR and VR depends on EHT features applied with restraint. We selected methods that remove randomness at its sources: contention, scheduling ambiguity, queue growth, and PHY timing. Each item includes a concrete example and the tradeoff you accept to stabilize latency variance.

Key takeaways

  • Deterministic latency comes from disciplined channel plans and admission limits.
  • Trigger based OFDMA removes backoff jitter during uplink motion frames.
  • QoS admission with small buffers caps delay and smooths delivery.
  • Guard interval and MCS choices should track margin, not peak rate.
  • Measure RU airtime budgets and enforce them with clear thresholds.

1) Channel discipline in 6 GHz for bounded latency

Deterministic latency starts with a conservative 6 GHz plan that reduces collisions and backoff variance. Pick the narrowest bandwidth that meets throughput needs, then place access points so primary channels avoid co-channel neighbors. For example, a venue with 60 AR headsets can run 80 MHz lanes per room instead of a single 320 MHz span, cutting contention domains and stabilizing queueing. This improves percentiles of motion to photon delay because fewer stations share Clear Channel Assessment time. The tradeoff is peak throughput per client drops, and wideband bursts for downloads slow down. The why is simple: latency variance mostly emerges from contention and retransmits. Limiting neighbors and primary overlap gives the MAC scheduler predictable airtime slices and fewer deferrals, which matters more than chasing headline rates.

Channel discipline also means anticipating puncturing patterns and avoiding partial primary blockage that forces dynamic fallbacks. If a neighboring network occupies part of your candidate channel, preselect an allowed preamble puncture that keeps your primary subchannel unpunctured and consistent across cells. In practice, set the same puncture mask for all APs in a zone so stations do not relearn topology per roam. A practical example is reserving a clean 80 MHz core and puncturing edge subchannels where needed to preserve pilot positions and sounding stability. The downside is you leave spectrum idle at times. The benefit is that the PHY and MAC no longer pivot unpredictably, which tightens jitter bounds during AR frame delivery.

Section highlights

  • Favor narrower, clean channels to cut contention and retransmit variance.
  • Standardize puncture masks per zone to stabilize station behavior.

2) Triggered OFDMA for uplink motion frames

Using trigger based OFDMA for uplink motion frames removes random backoff from the path, which tightens latency spread. Schedule periodic trigger frames that grant Resource Units to AR devices at a rate aligned to their sensor cadence, such as 120 Hz for head tracking. For instance, reserve a 106-tone RU every 8.3 ms for each headset, and use multi RU grants when devices need asymmetric bursts for keyframes. This converts contention into planned transmission opportunities, so the only delay reflects RU timing and channel retries. The tradeoff is scheduler rigidity can strand airtime if devices miss windows, and beacons plus management frames must still fit. The mechanism works because coordinated OFDMA collapses the randomness of CSMA into predictable slots that match application timing.

Triggering also helps downlink by aligning buffered frames with grant cycles, but you must size RU allocations using measured MCS under realistic SNR, not optimistic lab rates. A concrete rule of thumb is allocate at 70 percent of the median MCS throughput seen at the cell edge, which leaves headroom for short fades without blowing deadlines. Tie this with target wake time so devices wake just before their grant, then return to doze after delivery to keep queues shallow. The limitation is that devices and APs must support the same EHT trigger variants and multi RU policy, and mixed client populations may fall back to contention. Still, for supported headsets, triggers shape a near time division system that keeps uplink motion frames on schedule.

Section highlights

  • Grant RUs at the application cadence to replace random backoff.
  • Size allocations using conservative MCS to preserve deadlines.

3) QoS admission and bounded queue depth

QoS admission, not just prioritization, stabilizes latency by limiting how many flows occupy premium queues and by capping buffer depth. Map AR tracking to Voice Access Category and video overlays to Video, but require admission control using a token per device so only a fixed number can transmit at once. For example, allow ten tokens for tracking and twenty for overlays per cell, and reject any association that would exceed those budgets. Keep A-MPDU aggregation short, like 4 to 8 subframes for latency sensitive queues, to bound serialization delay while preserving some efficiency. The tradeoff is reduced spectral efficiency and more MAC overhead. The benefit is queues stay shallow under load, preventing head-of-line blocking that ruins smooth motion even when average throughput looks fine.

Queue discipline depends on DSCP to TID mapping and station compliance, so validate with packet captures and enforce at the AP if clients mis-mark. A concrete process is to police DSCP at the wired edge, rewrite to expected values, and mirror to the AP so WMM priorities are consistent. Then set per station airtime limits that align with RU grants, making sure aggregate airtime across admitted flows never exceeds 80 percent of usable airtime. This keeps slack for retries and management traffic, avoiding deadline misses when conditions vary. A limitation is that strict admission can block new users during peaks, which is politically hard. If that is unacceptable, create a fallback SSID with relaxed limits and warn users that motion smoothness may degrade.

Section highlights

  • Use admission and short aggregates to cap serialization and queueing delay.
  • Police DSCP and enforce airtime per station to hold slack capacity.

4) Timing choices for guard interval and MCS margins

Short guard intervals reduce symbol time and help latency, but only when multipath and SNR support the choice with margin. Select the shortest guard interval that still yields low retransmit rates at the worst seat in the room, then lock it for the cell so stations do not pivot per frame. As an example, choose 0.8 microseconds if a site survey shows minimal delay spread and 10 dB SNR headroom at the cell edge, otherwise prefer 1.6 microseconds for stability. Pair that with a conservative MCS floor derived from 5th percentile SNR, so frames do not oscillate between rates. The tradeoff is peak bitrate drops and throughput tests look lower. The payoff is that application deadlines are met more often because PHY retries no longer spike under small fades.

Bandwidth selection should also follow margin. Only use 160 or 320 MHz channels when the environment is sparse and co channel energy is reliably low. In many offices, 80 MHz with strong SNR yields better deadline percentiles than wider channels that invite partial blockage and busy times. To make the choice concrete, measure jitter with an AR traffic generator at candidate widths, then pick the narrowest width that holds jitter within your budget, such as under 5 ms p95. The drawback is you forego headline rates during large downloads. The rationale is that immersive motion is sensitive to delay variation, not bulk transfer speed. Stable PHY timing and modest width give EHT schedulers a calmer backdrop to place predictable windows.

Section highlights

  • Choose guard interval and MCS floors using worst seat margins.
  • Prefer narrower bandwidth if jitter improves under load tests.

Looking ahead

Treat deterministic latency as a budget with slack, not a single target. Start with a clean 6 GHz plan that protects your primary channels, then add trigger based OFDMA grants mapped to the motion cadence for uplink and aligned buffering downlink. Enforce admission with short aggregates so queues cannot grow unchecked, and lock guard interval plus MCS floors using worst seat margins. In your first pilot, log jitter distributions, not only averages, and adjust RU sizes to hold p95 within your budget. The tradeoff is reduced headline throughput, but the gain is a stable experience that does not swing as users move. Iterate with small changes and keep a rollback path so experiments do not disrupt sessions.

Key terms

  • 802.11be Extremely High Throughput — The IEEE spec that defines Wi‑Fi 7 features and limits.
  • Channel State Information Reference Signal — Known signals used to estimate the channel for CSI.
  • Differentiated Services Code Point (DSCP) — A packet header field used to mark priority.
  • Modulation and Coding Scheme — An index pairing of modulation order and coding rate.
  • Modulation and Coding Scheme Index — A number indicating modulation and coding level.
  • Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiple Access — A multi-user transmission method dividing channels into subcarriers.
  • Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiple Access — A scheme where multiple users share subcarriers simultaneously.

Frequently asked questions

Is 320 MHz required for low latency in AR and VR?

No. Wider channels increase peak rate, not predictability. Narrower, clean channels often produce tighter jitter because they reduce contention and partial blockage. Choose the smallest width that meets throughput while holding p95 delay within budget.

Do I need trigger based OFDMA if client support is mixed?

You gain benefits where supported, but mixed stations may fall back to contention. Use triggers for compatible headsets and keep conservative channel plans plus admission control so legacy devices do not overwhelm priority queues.

Should AR tracking use Voice or Video access category?

Tracking traffic benefits from the Voice category because smaller frames and tight deadlines prefer low contention windows. Overlays and scene updates fit the Video category. Always pair categories with admission control and short aggregates.

How do I pick a guard interval without special tools?

Use a site survey to estimate SNR and test jitter with both short and longer guard intervals. If short GI yields retry spikes at the worst seat, prefer the longer value. Favor stability over small throughput gains.
