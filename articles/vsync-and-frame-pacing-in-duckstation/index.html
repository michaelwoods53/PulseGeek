VSync and Frame Pacing in DuckStation

By Brian Rogers • Published March 6, 2026

Stable motion depends on how DuckStation manages vsync, frame pacing, and the display timing you actually own. These settings decide whether frames arrive evenly or bunch up, which you notice as stutter or tear lines. We will define the timing pieces, show a decision lens for choosing sync modes, and walk through realistic scenarios like high refresh monitors and handheld graphics limits. If you prefer quick presets, start with safe defaults, then tune one variable at a time so you can attribute any change. Along the way, we will reference renderer choices and common pitfalls that influence consistency even when vsync looks correct in isolation.

Key takeaways

  • Even frame pacing matters more than absolute FPS for perceived smoothness.
  • VSync trades tearing for latency and sometimes introduces periodic stutter.
  • Renderer choice and queue depth strongly affect timing predictability.
  • Match refresh, frame limit, and audio buffer to avoid drift.
  • Test with camera pans and frame time graphs, not just FPS.

Concepts and definitions

VSync synchronizes completed frames to the display’s refresh interval, which removes tearing by only presenting at vertical blank. The cost is extra waiting if a frame misses the window, often doubling frame time and causing a noticeable hitch. Frame pacing describes how evenly those frame times arrive, regardless of the average FPS, and it is what makes camera pans feel fluid. In DuckStation, pacing depends on emulator timing, renderer pipelines, and OS compositors. A rule of thumb is that stable 16.7 ms frame times at 60 Hz look smoother than fluctuating 12 to 25 ms deliveries. An edge case occurs when the compositor also applies sync, stacking with the emulator’s vsync and creating periodic stutters that repeat every few seconds due to cadence mismatches.

Display refresh rate sets the cadence target, while the emulator’s frame limiter and audio timing keep emulation speed steady relative to original hardware. When these clocks drift, you hear pitch wobble or see motion judder, even with vsync enabled. Practical setups align three things: base refresh, frame rate cap, and audio buffer size. For example, a 60 Hz screen and a 60 FPS limiter with a modest buffer will maintain consistent pacing for most games. A limitation appears on variable refresh displays when the OS compositor negates adaptive behavior, forcing fixed intervals despite VRR support. Understanding which layer owns timing prevents chasing ghosts that are not inside DuckStation at all.

Renderer choice influences pipeline latency and queueing, which in turn shapes pacing stability under load. Vulkan often offers steadier frame times on modern GPUs because it manages CPU overhead more predictably, while OpenGL can be simpler but may spike when driver queues flush. Neither is universally best across devices, so test both with the same scene. If you run into sustained spikes during heavy effects, reducing internal resolution or disabling expensive blending can restore margin. A small headroom buffer, roughly 10 to 20 percent under the refresh budget, keeps frames on time when background tasks nudge the CPU. Without that margin, vsync will magnify misses into visible hitches.

Frameworks and decision lenses

Start with a baseline lens that ranks priorities: tearing removal, latency, and stability under load. If tearing distracts you, hardware vsync or compositor sync makes sense, accepting extra input delay around one refresh worth. If latency matters most, leave vsync off and cap FPS just below refresh to reduce tear severity while keeping responsiveness. A middle path uses adaptive or fast sync variants when available, which try to present the latest full frame without blocking the pipeline. The catch is that adaptive strategies can reveal minor tears during dips. For DuckStation, map this lens to your game library and hardware, then test worst cases like transparent textures and busy scenes, not just static menus.

Use a second lens focused on timing ownership to avoid double syncing. Decide whether the OS compositor, GPU driver, or DuckStation should control presentation. As a rule, prefer one owner at a time and disable the others. On Windows with exclusive fullscreen, let the emulator or driver provide vsync and avoid desktop composition timing. On Linux with compositors like KWin or GNOME Shell, test fullscreen unredirect to skip composition during gameplay. If you suspect double sync, watch for rhythmic stutters at fixed intervals despite low GPU use. Eliminating redundant sync often fixes hitching without changing performance budgets.

Examples and short scenarios

On a 60 Hz laptop with iGPU limits, aim for predictable pacing first. Enable vsync in DuckStation, set internal resolution modestly so frame times remain under 16.7 ms, and keep audio buffers moderate to prevent underruns. If you still see periodic hitches, the desktop compositor might be imposing its own sync, so test exclusive fullscreen. A practical follow up is to review recommended presets for graphics and audio to confirm nothing else is adding instability. On a 144 Hz monitor, you can target multiples of refresh for fluid motion. Many users prefer vsync off with an FPS cap slightly below 144 to cut latency while reducing tear severity, or vsync on with a cap that keeps frames under 6.9 ms to avoid misses. If your GPU struggles during heavy effects, consider switching the renderer to improve consistency. In DuckStation, moving to Vulkan often steadies frame delivery on modern hardware. For Steam Deck or handhelds, thermal headroom can collapse unexpectedly, making pacing fragile. Keep vsync on for tear free motion, but lower internal resolution and disable costly effects like hardware accurate blending during demanding scenes. If you are new to the emulator, establishing a clean baseline matters more than chasing one timing tweak. Looking ahead

Begin future testing with repeatable panning scenes that reveal microstutter quickly, such as slow horizontal camera moves or menus with smooth scrolling. Record frame time graphs where possible and avoid judging only by FPS counters, which hide uneven delivery. Adopt a disciplined process: change a single variable, play two minutes of the same scene, and note whether pacing improved or latency worsened. If you switch renderers, recheck your cap and vsync settings because driver behavior may shift. Over time, build a short checklist personalized to your hardware that you can apply to new games without starting from scratch.

Expand your toolkit by learning how other related settings influence timing. Internal resolution scaling changes GPU workload and headroom, while shader effects and blending alter frame time variance under specific scenes. On systems that support it, variable refresh displays can mask minor dips when vsync is off and caps are set near the average frame rate. Use that only when the OS and driver chain actually honors VRR, otherwise it behaves like fixed refresh. Track audio behavior as a health signal, since buffer underruns often precede visible stutter, making them a useful early warning.

Finally, document known tricky titles that expose edge cases, such as games with erratic internal frame timings or heavy transparency. For those, per game overrides may stabilize presentation without penalizing the rest of your library. Keep notes on what combination worked, including refresh, cap value, vsync state, renderer, and any compositor changes. Revisit this list after driver or OS updates because timing characteristics can shift subtly. With a small investment in methodical testing, you will resolve most pacing issues and maintain smoother play sessions across varied hardware and displays.

  • Screen Tearing — Visible split between frames on the display.
  • Variable Refresh Rate — Display tech that changes refresh rate to match frame rate.
