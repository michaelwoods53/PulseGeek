802.11be Release Milestones and Drafts Explained

By Allison Grant • Published January 6, 2026

Engineers track 802.11be milestones to time features, plan dependencies, and forecast risk. This guide explains drafts, ballots, and approvals with pragmatic signals that indicate when designs can harden or must stay flexible. Items were selected for their recurring impact on schedules and the specific decisions teams face as the text evolves.

Key takeaways

  • Draft number jumps signal stability changes that affect engineering commitments.
  • Ballot thresholds and recirculation counts predict schedule risk and churn.
  • Comment resolution mechanics determine whether text is stable or volatile.
  • Sponsor approval gates align with when product roadmaps can solidify.
  • Publication timing differs from certification readiness for Wi‑Fi 7.

1) Draft 0.x: Scope and problem framing

Draft 0.x represents scope forming rather than stability, so treat it as directional guidance rather than a spec. At this point, milestones are about aligning on problem statements and initial Extremely High Throughput mechanisms, not locking MAC or PHY details. For example, a team may prototype Multi Link Operation behaviors with toggles that accept rapid change, while documentation tracks assumptions explicitly. The advantage is speed and broad exploration, which surfaces constraints early. The tradeoff is volatility, because unresolved comments and straw polls can reshape clauses or rename fields. Plan experiments and internal interfaces to be replaceable. Favor narrow abstractions and keep feature flags around critical state machines so discarded branches do not burn calendar time when scope narrows between 0.3 and 0.5.

Section highlights

  • Draft 0.x guides exploration, not implementation commitments or text freezes.
  • Prototype with reversible interfaces to handle rapid scope adjustments.

2) Draft 1.0: WG Letter Ballot entry

Draft 1.0 signals readiness for Working Group Letter Ballot and a shift from exploration to structured review. Milestones at this stage include ballot initiation, comment capture, and initial defect taxonomy that separates editorials from technical changes. A practical example is freezing frame field names while allowing element ordering to evolve, which lets parser code stabilize sooner. The tradeoff is partial stability: significant comments can still reshape PHY procedures or scheduling rules. Engineers should prioritize norms that are unlikely to reverse, such as negotiated capability signaling patterns, and track clause references diligently. Section highlights

  • Draft 1.0 enters structured Working Group ballot with scoped stability.
  • Freeze durable names and capabilities while tracking evolving procedures.

3) Comment resolution and CIDs

Ballot comments become Comment IDs, or CIDs, which drive precise edits, deferrals, or rework. The process matters because CID disposition types forecast churn: accept modifies text, reject preserves it with rationale, and revise in next recirculation queues larger changes. For instance, if several CIDs target HARQ feedback timing, expect ripple effects across scheduling and coexistence clauses. The benefit of transparent CID handling is traceability for design decisions and a shared baseline for implementers. The tradeoff is schedule uncertainty when many technical CIDs stack across related sections. Manage risk with a triage rubric that ranks CIDs by cross clause impact and testability, and keep internal drafts aligned to the latest resolved text to avoid refactoring when the next recirculation lands.

Section highlights

  • CID dispositions forecast stability or churn across related clauses.
  • Triaging by cross impact reduces refactor risk before recirculation.

4) Draft 2.x: Technical convergence

Draft 2.x represents convergence within the Working Group after multiple recirculations, so text is nearer to stable and feature deltas narrow. Teams can usually lock packet formats, IEs, and capability bit positions while leaving room for clarifying notes. A concrete example is finalizing Multi Resource Unit signaling and keeping editor’s notes only for illustrations. The benefit is predictable integration tests and more reliable interop trials. The limitation remains pockets of active CIDs that can adjust corner cases. When mapping clauses to features for deeper analysis, you can navigate the clause structure and MAC PHY mapping to keep work aligned across chapters: navigate the clause structure and MAC PHY mapping. Balance stability with vigilance by gating any silicon hardening behind confirmation that only editorials remain in affected sections.

Section highlights

  • Draft 2.x narrows deltas, enabling stable packet formats and capabilities.
  • Confirm remaining changes are editorial before hardening implementations.

5) Draft 3.0: Sponsor Ballot entry

Draft 3.0 enters Sponsor Ballot where broader IEEE-SA review evaluates technical sufficiency, editorial quality, and compatibility with the base standard. This milestone indicates that Working Group consensus has matured, and issue patterns should focus on polish or targeted clarifications. For example, error handling text might be tightened to resolve ambiguity around retransmission windows without altering field encodings. The benefit is wide scrutiny that increases implementability across vendors. The tradeoff is recirculation cycles that consume schedule margin until approval thresholds are met. Plan for test plan updates and documentation snapshots aligned to each 3.x release, and track any normative note conversions that move guidance into enforceable language, because those can subtly change compliance expectations.

Section highlights

  • Draft 3.0 starts Sponsor Ballot with wider technical and editorial review.
  • Expect recirculations that refine clarity and tighten compliance language.

6) Recirculation thresholds and approvals

Approval thresholds in Sponsor Ballot determine how many recirculations are necessary, so tracking percentage approves and unresolved comments gives a realistic schedule outlook. Mechanically, each recirculation addresses changes since the last ballot and seeks to improve approval ratios while reducing negative votes tied to open CIDs. A practical approach is to build a dashboard that correlates sections changed with approve rates, then forecast the remaining cycles. The benefit is a measurable path to readiness and informed go or hold decisions on downstream releases. The tradeoff is sunk time if new negatives arise from late clarifications, expanding work. Keep a buffer for at least one additional recirculation, and align partner test events after the threshold is consistently met rather than on single ballot optimism.

Section highlights

  • Monitor approval ratios and unresolved CIDs to estimate remaining cycles.
  • Schedule interop after thresholds stabilize across consecutive ballots.

7) Conditional approval and editorial polish

Conditional approval often indicates that only specific fixes remain before forwarding to the next governing body, which places emphasis on editorial hygiene and cross reference accuracy. During this stage, watch for conversions of notes to normative statements and final alignment with annex material. For example, a figure caption might be clarified to match variable names used in text, avoiding mismatches that confuse implementers. The upside is predictable closure, allowing teams to freeze deliverables like register maps and stable headers. The tradeoff is the risk that a small clarification unexpectedly tightens behavior. Mitigate by validating edge cases that were previously permissive and re-running compliance checks against updated normative language, especially in timing and coexistence conditions where footnotes sometimes migrate into requirements.

Section highlights

  • Conditional approval focuses effort on editorial and cross reference fixes.
  • Recheck edge cases when notes become normative requirements.

8) Roll up and publication

After Sponsor approval, the amendment proceeds to final editorial integration and publication, then rolls into a consolidated base release in a later cycle. The milestone to watch is when the amendment text is frozen for publication, which lets teams finalize external documentation and long term support branches. An example is aligning developer guides with stable clause numbers and annex references. The benefit is durable references for field support and certification. The tradeoff is that publication timing does not instantly translate to certification readiness. Plan sequencing with certification bodies and sync clause references using a clause oriented index if available. For broader context on EHT objectives and integration, review this clause aware overview on amendment integration with base 802.11: amendment integration with base 802.11.

Section highlights

  • Publication freezes text and enables durable documentation references.
  • Plan certification separately from publication to avoid schedule surprises.

9) Certification and WFA readiness

Certification programs typically trail the standard, because profiles, test plans, and tooling need mature text and interop data. Treat this milestone as a separate track that validates subsets and profiles rather than the entire amendment at once. For example, early Wi‑Fi 7 certification may emphasize specific MLO modes and channel widths, leaving advanced combinations for later waves. The advantage is earlier market readiness for core features. The limitation is that not all optional capabilities are certifiable at initial launch. Coordinate with partners to match the expected profile set, and lock product marketing claims to what certification bodies can verify in the targeted window. Keep a change log that maps each feature to certification phases to avoid overpromising.

Section highlights

  • Treat certification timing as distinct from standard publication schedules.
  • Align features with available profiles to prevent unsupported claims.

10) Reading draft deltas efficiently

Efficiently reading draft deltas starts with anchoring to clause numbers, figures, and defined terms, then mapping each change to an implementable behavior. A practical method pairs a redline view with a tracking table that lists affected state machines, IEs, and test vectors. For example, a single timing adjustment can cascade through HARQ exchange timers and scheduler priorities. The advantage is precise scoping that curbs unnecessary retesting. The tradeoff is upfront effort to maintain the map. Use structured notes that record reason, dependency, and validation method for each delta. Section highlights

  • Map each redline to concrete behaviors, tests, and dependencies.
  • Maintain a living index to reduce retest and refactor overhead.

11) Timelines and milestone forecasting

Forecasting milestones relies on transparent signals rather than hopeful dates. Track approve percentages, negative vote reasons, and the rate of new technical CIDs to estimate remaining cycles. A practical example is modeling a glide slope that assumes diminishing new negatives per recirculation once major topics stabilize. The benefit is better stakeholder communication and more accurate dependency planning across firmware, drivers, and validation. The tradeoff is sensitivity to late breaking clarifications that can reset the glide slope. To manage uncertainty, plan gates for internal RC candidates that require no unresolved technical CIDs in relevant clauses.   • Use approval trends and CID rates to project remaining cycles.
  • Gate releases on zero unresolved technical CIDs in critical clauses.

Key terms

  • 802.11be Extremely High Throughput — The IEEE spec that defines Wi‑Fi 7 features and limits.
  • Automatic Repeat Request — A protocol that requests retransmission of failed frames.
  • Extremely High Throughput — Capability class in 802.11be targeting very high data rates and efficiency.
  • Hybrid Automatic Repeat Request — Retransmission method combining error correction and repeats to improve reliability.
  • Multi‑Link Operation (MLO) — Using multiple bands or channels at once between a client and AP.

Frequently asked questions

What does a jump from Draft 1.x to 2.0 usually imply?

It typically indicates Working Group convergence where major technical changes slow. Packet formats and capability bits can often stabilize, though some corner cases may still evolve through recirculation.

Is publication the same as being ready for certification programs?

No. Publication freezes text, but certification bodies need time to finalize profiles and test plans. Plan certification as a separate track with phased feature coverage.

How many recirculations should teams budget during Sponsor Ballot?

The count varies with approval thresholds and unresolved comments. Budget for at least one extra cycle beyond the optimistic plan to cover clarifications that introduce new negatives.

When is it safe to harden silicon or firmware interfaces?

Wait until affected clauses show only editorial changes and there are no unresolved technical CIDs. Draft 2.x or later often provides that stability in many areas, but verify per section.

How should engineers track changes between drafts effectively?

Use redlines tied to a mapping table of impacted behaviors, state machines, and tests. Record the reason, dependency, and validation method for each change to keep scope clear.
