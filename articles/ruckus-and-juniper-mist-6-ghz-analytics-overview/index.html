Concepts and definitions

Effective 6 GHz analytics begins with airtime utilization, not raw throughput, because contention determines client experience under 320 MHz. A rule of thumb is to watch busy time per radio with client counts and MCS distribution attached, since a few low-rate stations can dominate airtime. For example, if utilization reads 55 percent but median MCS drops during peaks, the bottleneck is modulation robustness, not bandwidth. The tradeoff is that utilization can mask per-RU inefficiency from puncturing or interference, so graphs need granularity by radio or channel segment. Understanding why airtime tells the story helps size changes to channel width or schedule heavy flows without guessing. Without that lens, operators can chase Mbps gains while latency and jitter worsen.

Latency analytics should focus on percentiles and variance because means hide congestion and scheduling effects with mixed client capabilities. A practical approach is to track p95 and p99 round trips for each SSID alongside retry rate and queue depth, correlating spikes with known events like DFS moves. Consider a scenario where average latency is stable at 12 ms yet p99 jumps to 120 ms during lunch hour, signaling queue pressure from bursty uplink traffic. The limitation is that controller latency often reflects application paths beyond the access layer, so include local echo or test frames when possible. This separation clarifies whether to adjust WMM, shape uplink greed, or investigate upstream routing, which avoids whack-a-mole tuning.

SNR and RSSI require reinterpretation on 320 MHz, since wider bandwidth lowers SNR for the same noise power density. A useful practice is to treat healthy SNR as a range relative to the selected MCS rather than a fixed target, because spatial streams and guard intervals shift thresholds. For instance, a device sustaining MCS 7 with 18 to 20 dB SNR may still experience retries when puncturing narrows usable subchannels. The edge case appears when client radios report optimistic RSSI while the AP sees elevated noise, creating asymmetry that standard averages miss. Understanding how bandwidth affects SNR informs whether to downshift to 160 MHz during high-density periods to stabilize links, even if peak throughput falls.

Looking ahead

The near-term priority is to align analytics with staged policy changes and clear rollback paths. Start by documenting target ranges for airtime, p95 latency, and SNR for each site profile, then wire alerts to those bands with muted noise. When a change like width reduction or minimum rate adjustment ships, capture a bounded before and after window and compare client journeys rather than only aggregate graphs. The risk is attributing wins to the wrong lever, so isolate variables and keep test windows short. With that rigor, dashboards become decision engines instead of passive charts, and teams gain confidence to iterate without destabilizing service during busy periods.

Medium term, treat DFS awareness and channel puncturing as first-class analytics, not postmortems. Build a habit of annotating events like radar hits and maintenance windows to explain utilization and latency shifts, then review those notes during weekly tuning. For mixed fleets, consider adaptive policies that toggle 320 MHz during low density and step down to 160 MHz when client counts or retry rates cross thresholds. The limitation is complexity for operators, which you counter by automating recommendations only after the telemetry proves consistent over time. That balance keeps automation honest and supports gradual adoption without surprising tenants who depend on stable latency.

Finally, invest in visibility that shortens diagnosis when dashboards lack detail. A quick, focused packet capture during an anomaly provides ground truth on retries, MCS drops, and management exchanges. Pair that with exportable time series so you can correlate flows with controller views later. If your environment spans vendors, align metric names and units to prevent misreads and create common runbooks for the team. Frequently asked questions

What metrics matter most for 6 GHz monitoring?

Prioritize airtime utilization per radio, p95 or p99 latency, retry rate, and SNR in context of channel width and puncturing. Together they explain user experience and guide width, power, and QoS decisions.

How do 320 MHz channels affect SNR targets?

Wider bandwidth raises noise across the channel, lowering effective SNR for the same signal. Judge SNR relative to the MCS in use and consider stepping down to 160 MHz when retries rise or tails widen.

Should I tune to throughput or latency first?

Favor latency percentiles and retry reduction first, because users feel delay and jitter more than peak speed. Throughput follows once contention and scheduling are stable for the dominant applications.

When are DFS events the likely root cause?

If you see sudden latency spikes, roaming flaps, or short drops aligned with radar detections or channel changes, treat DFS as a prime suspect. Confirm with event timestamps and client journey traces.

Do I need packet captures for every anomaly?

No. Use brief, targeted captures when dashboards lack resolution or signals conflict. Verify retries, MCS changes, and control exchanges to confirm hypotheses before altering width, power, or QoS.
