Shader Presets and CRT Effects for DuckStation

By Brian Rogers • Published March 4, 2026

Shader presets in DuckStation can recreate CRT effects that flatter PlayStation textures, but they must be tuned to your display and renderer. This guide compares common CRT shaders and explains how masks, scanlines, and bloom interact. Each item pairs a practical rule with an example and a tradeoff so you can decide quickly whether authenticity or clarity matters more for a given game.

Key takeaways

  • Start with a balanced CRT preset, then calibrate mask to panel.
  • Match scanline thickness to integer scale to reduce artifacts.
  • Use bloom and glow lightly to avoid muddying PS1 textures.
  • Tie shader choices to internal resolution and renderer behavior.
  • Save per-game overrides when global settings cause visual issues.

1. Choose a credible CRT base preset

A strong starting point is a well-tested CRT shader that balances mask accuracy, scanline behavior, and performance, because stacking ad hoc passes often creates inconsistent results. For example, a preset modeled after slot masks with configurable phosphor layout tends to preserve PlayStation dithering while reducing harsh pixel edges. Apply it to a 1080p display and you will notice stable scanlines at 2x or 3x internal scale with modest GPU cost. The tradeoff is that heavy presets with many passes can limit headroom for higher internal resolutions or PGXP features. If your renderer struggles, choose a lighter variant that still offers beam shape control and per-channel masks so color fringing remains subtle. The right base preset reduces later tweaks and avoids chasing artifacts introduced by mismatched shader pieces.

Section highlights

  • Begin with a balanced CRT preset to minimize later corrections.
  • Prefer lighter variants if GPU headroom limits resolution scaling.

2. Calibrate mask and scanlines to panel

Mask density and scanline thickness should align with your monitor’s pixel structure and target scale to avoid moiré and banding. On a 1440p panel running 3x internal resolution, a medium slot mask with per-line scanlines at around half intensity often yields crisp text without hiding subpixel detail. Test with high-contrast menus from a PS1 RPG and observe if alternating lines shimmer during camera pans. If shimmer appears, reduce mask strength or adjust scanline phase in the shader parameters until stability returns. The tradeoff is that lighter masks can feel less “CRT-like,” especially on bright scenes. Conversely, heavy masks flatten fine dithering and can exaggerate color separation. Calibrate under the brightness you actually play at so perceived thickness and mask visibility match real usage rather than a photo-friendly profile.

Section highlights

  • Match mask density and scanlines to resolution for stable motion.
  • Tune under real play brightness to avoid misleading thickness.

3. Set gamma and color temperature

Correct gamma and color temperature keep shader output within expected luminance, preventing washout when scanlines and masks darken the image. A simple rule is to start near 2.2 gamma and nudge brightness rather than crushing blacks, then choose a 6500K target unless the game art benefits from warmer tones. Try a survival horror title in DuckStation and raise gamma slightly to recover shadow detail that the scanlines reduce, verifying that fog gradients remain smooth. The tradeoff is that too much gamma compensation eliminates the depth that scanlines add and can reveal banding in skyboxes. If your display has limited contrast, favor milder scanlines and smaller gamma boosts instead of pushing display brightness, which increases glow and reduces mask contrast. Calibrate in a dark scene and a bright scene to ensure balanced results across levels.

Section highlights

  • Use modest gamma tweaks to counter scanline darkening without crush.
  • Validate on dark and bright scenes to avoid banding or haze.

4. Simulate phosphor persistence carefully

Phosphor persistence simulation can add convincing motion continuity, but too much blur smears PS1 dithering and UI edges. Enable a mild temporal response and test with a 60 fps racer where roadside textures stream by. You should perceive softer trailing that hides shimmer while leaving speedometer digits legible. If you stack motion blur with scanline bloom, verify that input lag does not feel compounded, because some persistence passes require multi-frame accumulation. The tradeoff is that low-end GPUs may struggle with history buffers and increase latency. If performance dips, disable temporal accumulation and rely on beam shape plus light bloom to hint at persistence. Reserve stronger persistence for high-refresh displays where presentation remains smooth while your renderer keeps consistent frame pacing under load.

Section highlights

  • Use gentle persistence to reduce shimmer without muddying UI.
  • Drop temporal passes if latency or performance become concerns.

5. Add deconvergence and beam misalignment sparingly

Small amounts of deconvergence recreate analog color fringing that softens hard edges, but overdoing it fatigues the eyes and makes text harder to read. Apply a tiny per-channel offset and test with white text on dark backgrounds in a JRPG menu. If red or blue fringes are visible at normal viewing distance, reduce offsets until the edge looks natural rather than split. The why is simple: PS1 content already leans on dithering and composite-era assumptions, so extra chroma separation multiplies softness. A tradeoff exists with authenticity versus clarity, especially on large 4K panels where subtle misalignment scales visually. If you crave the look but need legibility, keep horizontal offsets smaller than vertical and disable per-pixel randomness so the effect remains consistent across frames and does not introduce shimmer during camera movement.

Section highlights

  • Keep deconvergence tiny to preserve readable text and menus.
  • Favor vertical bias and disable randomness to reduce shimmer.

6. Use curvature and bezel with restraint

Curvature and bezel overlays sell the CRT illusion, yet they consume screen area and can distort HUD elements. Dial in slight barrel distortion and a thin bezel, then load a 2D platformer with HUD at the edges. Ensure score and lives remain fully visible after any overscan emulation the preset applies. The tradeoff is practical: strong curvature looks great in screenshots but reduces effective resolution and introduces scaling artifacts at non-integer zoom. If you play seated far from a TV, a lighter bezel keeps brightness and contrast consistent across the screen. Consider disabling curvature on ultrawide monitors where edge stretch becomes obvious, and instead increase vignette subtly. The goal is a hint of glass and dome while keeping native aspect and important UI readable during long sessions.

Section highlights

  • Apply gentle curvature so HUD and aspect remain stable.
  • Prefer light bezels to preserve brightness and usable screen area.

7. Tune bloom and glow for clarity

Bloom can restore perceived luminance lost to scanlines, but wide kernels wash detail and exaggerate dithering. Start with a narrow radius and low intensity, then test bright spell effects in an action RPG. The highlights should feel lively while character outlines remain crisp. Watch for halos around text or additive HUD elements; if they appear, reduce threshold and compensate with a tiny brightness increase instead. The tradeoff is that stronger bloom often hides mask texture, which some players associate with the CRT look. If you prefer pronounced mask, keep bloom restrained and lean on calibrated gamma. On displays with limited peak brightness, a modest glow helps readability, but keep it scene-limited so dusk environments do not turn gray. The rule is highlight lift, not constant haze.

Section highlights

  • Use small-radius bloom to revive highlights without halos.
  • Favor gamma tweaks when bloom hides mask texture.

8. Pair shaders with internal resolution

Shaders interact with internal resolution scaling, so choose a scale that preserves scanline rhythm and avoids over-sharpening. At 2x or 3x internal resolution, masks and lines map predictably on 1080p and 1440p panels. Try 3x in DuckStation and evaluate text clarity in a tactics game, then toggle to 4x to see if scanlines drift or thin unevenly. If drifting occurs, either change scanline phase or step down the scale. The tradeoff is clarity versus cohesion: higher scale improves geometry edges but can break the cadence that makes CRT effects convincing. For a deeper dive on scaling considerations, compare options in the guide on choosing internal resolutions and understanding tradeoffs. Align shader cadence first, then spend remaining GPU headroom on upscale.

Section highlights

  • Pick scales that keep scanlines and masks mapping predictably.
  • Prioritize cadence, then allocate headroom to higher resolution.

9. Balance sharpness with NTSC simulation

NTSC or composite simulation can blend dithering into smoother gradients, which benefits skyboxes and shadows, but it can also soften UI. Enable a light NTSC pass and test a game with heavy checkerboard dithering in water or fog. If the pattern resolves into cleaner tones while item icons remain crisp, you have the mix right. The tradeoff is that some NTSC shaders introduce chroma bleed that clashes with deconvergence, making edges overly soft. If you already use minor deconvergence, reduce NTSC chroma blur and rely on luma-only blending. For a holistic setup walkthrough, the step-by-step article on installing and configuring DuckStation across platforms explains where to load shader presets and how they interact with renderer selection.

Section highlights

  • Use light NTSC passes to blend dithering without blurring UI.
  • Reduce chroma blur when combined with deconvergence effects.

10. Manage performance with renderer choices

Renderer choice and shader complexity compete for the same GPU budget, so test both together. If Vulkan is stable on your system, its driver overhead and async behavior commonly leave more headroom for heavier CRT presets than OpenGL on the same hardware. Validate with a stress scene like a boss fight with transparency effects turned up. If frame pacing stutters, simplify the shader or switch renderers and retest. The tradeoff is compatibility: some GPUs behave better on OpenGL, and certain shader passes may perform differently across drivers. When you need more guidance on sensible defaults beyond shaders, the write-up on recommended graphics and performance settings helps you choose a baseline before layering effects.

Section highlights

  • Evaluate shader complexity and renderer together under stress scenes.
  • Switch renderers or simplify passes to fix pacing issues.

11. Save presets and per-game overrides

Per-game overrides keep your global look consistent while accommodating titles that react poorly to heavy effects. After tuning a preset for a problematic racer with aggressive transparency, save those parameters to a game-specific profile so other titles retain your preferred mask and scanlines. This approach avoids toggling settings every session. The tradeoff is maintenance: too many overrides become hard to track. Keep a short naming convention and group by visual issue, such as “halo sensitive” or “text heavy.” When a title truly needs unique treatment, follow guidance on applying graphics overrides for tricky games so changes load automatically. Reserve overrides for real conflicts rather than personal mood, and your library will stay coherent without sacrificing authenticity.

Section highlights

  • Use per-game profiles to handle titles sensitive to effects.
  • Keep naming consistent to manage and revisit tuned presets.

Looking ahead

Treat your shader preset as a living profile that evolves with your display, renderer updates, and the mix of games you play. Schedule short checks after driver or DuckStation updates to verify that masks and scanlines still map cleanly at your chosen scale. If you plan to move from 1080p to 4K, prototype a lighter preset now so the transition preserves cadence without doubling GPU cost. Build a small set of three profiles spanning clarity-first, balanced CRT, and authenticity-first so you can switch confidently when a title demands it. For a broader configuration baseline beyond effects, keep notes from your earlier tests aligned with recommendations in the general graphics guide and revisit them when you add new hardware. Iterating deliberately keeps visuals consistent while avoiding endless tinkering.

Section highlights

  • Revalidate presets after updates or hardware changes to maintain cadence.
  • Maintain three profiles to pivot between clarity and authenticity quickly.
