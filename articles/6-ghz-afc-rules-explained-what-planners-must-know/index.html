6 GHz AFC Rules Explained for Network Planners

By Olivia Adams • Published February 23, 2026

Planners need a clear mental model for 6 GHz, AFC, and rules that tie power and channel access to location and time. This explainer centers on how an Automated Frequency Coordination system evaluates your access point request, returns constraints, and influences Wi‑Fi 8 design choices. The focus is not vendor specifics but the mechanisms and decision criteria that make deployments lawful and efficient. You will learn how grants limit EIRP and bandwidth, how to interpret rechecks, and when indoor modes avoid coordination entirely. Short scenarios show tradeoffs between range and capacity, with guidance on when to favor narrower channels over higher transmit power for reliability.

Key takeaways

  • AFC grants constrain channels and EIRP based on location and time.
  • Translate grant limits into channel width and antenna gain choices.
  • Recheck schedules matter when incumbents or weather radar shift risk.
  • Indoor low power can avoid AFC but reduces effective range significantly.
  • Use narrower channels when EIRP or PSD limits throttle wide bandwidths.

Concepts and definitions

AFC, short for Automated Frequency Coordination, is a service that evaluates proposed 6 GHz transmissions from standard power access points and returns allowed channels and EIRP values for a given site. The intent is simple protection of incumbents such as fixed microwave links while enabling Wi‑Fi 8 growth in the band. Practically, you submit coordinates, height, and equipment characteristics, then receive a grant that may restrict bandwidth or require notching around incumbents. The edge case is timing granularity, because grants can expire or tighten as propagation conditions or database updates change. For indoor low power devices, AFC typically does not apply, but indoor rules impose lower EIRP and indoor‑only operation, which trades reach for simplicity.

EIRP, or equivalent isotropically radiated power, combines transmitter output and antenna gain into a single radiated power value that determines range and interference risk. AFC limits EIRP per channel segment based on path‑loss models to incumbents, which means your antenna patterns and mounting height materially impact approval. For example, a high‑gain directional antenna can push EIRP over limits unless you back off conducted power, while a downtilted sector might pass with acceptable margins. The tradeoff is that lowering power can reduce cell edge throughput, so planners often compensate with denser AP placement or narrower channels to lift signal‑to‑noise. Understanding this interaction prevents unintentional noncompliance during optimization.

Grants reference channels and sometimes power spectral density, which is the per‑megahertz power budget that scales with channel width. A 160 MHz request with a fixed PSD cap can be infeasible if the computed EIRP over the entire band would exceed protection thresholds. In such cases, requesting 80 or 40 MHz may unlock an acceptable combination of bandwidth and permissible EIRP, improving reliability even if peak rates decline. Another nuance is frequency notching where the grant permits partial channel use, requiring careful configuration to avoid transmitting on prohibited subchannels. Planners should verify that controller templates can express these constraints, or choose clean channels that the grant fully authorizes.

Frameworks and decision lenses

A workable lens is grant to configuration mapping, which converts AFC outputs into concrete channel plans and power settings. Start by classifying the grant into full channels available, partial notches, or denied ranges, then select the narrowest width that avoids notches while satisfying throughput targets. Next, calculate conducted power by subtracting antenna gain and cable loss from allowed EIRP to produce a safe transmitter setting. The tradeoff appears when client capabilities and environmental noise differ across floors, so per‑area templates may diverge. Document recheck intervals and alarms, because sudden grant tightening could otherwise lead to silent outages or reduced coverage if the system automatically enforces new limits without planner review.

A second lens is mode selection between standard power with AFC and low power indoor operation without coordination. Use standard power when you need outdoor coverage, long links on campuses, or penetration through challenging materials, understanding AFC oversight and installation accuracy are mandatory. Choose low power indoor when deployment simplicity, rapid changes, or dense multi‑tenant environments demand predictable local rules and smaller cells. The limitation is capacity scaling, because indoor constraints reduce EIRP and therefore coverage area, which requires more APs and disciplined frequency reuse. This trade is usually favorable for high‑density offices where spatial reuse improves user experience more than raw transmit power does.

Finally, adopt a risk register that tracks incumbents and environmental factors near each site. Record known fixed service paths, nearby weather radar presence if regionally applicable, and topographical features that alter line of sight. Use conservative safety margins in terrain‑challenged areas, because minor elevation errors can skew path loss estimates that AFC systems depend on. Include an operational policy for grant revalidation before moves or height changes, since even small mounting adjustments can shift RF geometry. For planners operating in multiple regions, keep a matrix of regulatory domains and their differences, then standardize internal templates accordingly while documenting deviations to reduce misconfiguration risk.

Examples and short scenarios

Consider a suburban campus needing outdoor coverage across walkways and courtyards, with a grant that denies 160 MHz and permits two adjacent 80 MHz channels at modest EIRP. The practical choice is to deploy standard power APs on light poles, select a single 80 MHz channel with better clearance, and set conducted power to keep EIRP under the limit after antenna gain. If the area has occasional grant tightening, plan overlapping cells and enable graceful bandwidth fallback to 40 MHz during events. The tradeoff is reduced peak throughput at times, but the outcome is stable coverage with predictable margins. For background, a deep guide to regulations can help plan compliant deployments, such as this piece on EIRP limits and AFC flow.

Now consider a high‑rise office with dense meeting areas and numerous neighboring tenants. Indoor low power is attractive because it avoids AFC registration and simplifies moves and changes, but the lower EIRP means smaller cells and more APs. Choose 40 MHz channels to improve signal quality and reuse, and design for higher AP density to maintain capacity. If a new tenant starts using overlapping channels, your smaller cells and narrower bandwidth reduce contention impact. The tradeoff is hardware count and cabling effort, but the benefit is consistent user experience without coordination delays. A mixed environment shows the nuance. A university wants stadium concourses at standard power for reach and an adjacent library at indoor low power for density. The stadium grant allows only 20 or 40 MHz near certain incumbents, which suggests tighter channelization and more APs at entrances to handle surges. The library, by contrast, can run consistent 40 MHz channels with low EIRP and heavy spatial reuse. Operationally, the team schedules AFC rechecks before major events, then freezes changes during games to avoid unexpected updates. This split approach acknowledges different RF goals and constraints within a single property and documents them for smoother maintenance.

Looking ahead

Planning does not stop at initial activation, because AFC policies and databases evolve as regulators refine protections and add providers. Build processes that treat the grant as a living constraint with explicit revalidation triggers, such as equipment swaps, height changes, or periodic checks. Instrument your controller to alert on grant expirations or tightened limits, and keep rollback plans for channel and power templates. Where regional differences matter, maintain a concise matrix of domains and ensure installers understand local paperwork requirements. Finally, record a rationale for each configuration decision, because future audits or staff changes will benefit from knowing why a narrower channel or lower EIRP was chosen at deployment time.

Tooling also matures, so revisit planning assumptions when vendors add features like automated notch handling or more granular PSD settings. If your environment changes, such as new construction altering line of sight, model path differences and request updated grants proactively. Engage with operations to observe client experience under real loads, because airtime, retries, and rates offer better insight than peak throughput alone. Consider peer reviews for complex sites where exposure to incumbents is higher, like hilltops or tall rooftops. When possible, stage deployments in small batches, validate behavior against the grant, and widen rollout once metrics confirm that constraints translate cleanly into safe performance.

For deeper context on the regulatory landscape and domain differences, consult neutral resources rather than relying on vendor brochures. If you work globally, tracking how FCC, Ofcom, or EU authorities adjust allocations and terminology will prevent configuration drift between sites. Use internal wikis to distill those changes into practical checklists that capture data inputs, like precise coordinates and antenna patterns, which AFC systems require. As your team gains experience, standardize templates that prefer clean channels over wide ones when PSD or EIRP caps squeeze headroom. This discipline keeps designs resilient despite updates and reduces risk when grants shift over time.

Frequently asked questions

What does AFC control in 6 GHz deployments?

AFC controls which channels and EIRP levels a standard power access point may use at a given location, based on protecting incumbents. It can also require rechecks or expire grants, which affects ongoing configuration.

Do indoor low power devices require AFC?

Typically no. Indoor low power devices operate under separate limits and indoor-only constraints, so they avoid AFC registration. The tradeoff is reduced EIRP and smaller cells that require more access points.

How should I handle partial channel grants?

Prefer clean channels that the grant fully authorizes. If not possible, configure narrower widths that exclude restricted subchannels. Ensure your controller or AP supports notching before attempting partial use.

Why do AFC grants change over time?

Grants can change due to database updates, environmental factors, or policy adjustments. Regular revalidation and monitoring reduce surprises, and documented templates help apply new limits with minimal disruption.

What inputs matter most for AFC requests?

Accurate coordinates, mounting height, antenna gain and pattern, and requested channel details matter. Small errors in location or height can affect path loss estimates and narrow the grant unnecessarily.
