Best Audio Settings in DuckStation: SPU Latency & Sync

By Joshua Bennett • Published January 18, 2026

Clean audio in DuckStation comes from balancing SPU latency, sync behavior, and output buffer size rather than toggling one magic switch. This guide curates practical audio settings that reduce crackle, avoid desync, and keep timing stable. Each item explains why it matters, offers a clear example, and describes tradeoffs so you can choose the right approach for your setup. If you need a broader path that spans input and storage issues too, see our complete guide to fix controllers, audio crackle, and saves in DuckStation, which shows how adjacent systems affect sound quality.

Key takeaways

  • Start with moderate SPU latency, then tune buffer size for stability.
  • Use synchronous audio for accuracy, time stretch for smoother hiccups.
  • Match output backend and sample rate to your platform’s strengths.
  • Cap stretch range and enable pitch correction to avoid warble.
  • Reverb tweaks can reduce muddiness without masking real timing faults.

1) Set SPU latency for stable timing

Begin with a moderate SPU latency to stabilize timing without hiding real performance issues. A practical baseline is a midrange setting that avoids extremes, since overly low latency risks crackle during short workload spikes while excessively high values add delay that feels unresponsive. For instance, if brief stutters produce pops, nudging latency upward in small increments often smooths playback while keeping inputs snappy. The tradeoff is that latency cannot fix sustained CPU slowdowns, so pushing it too high may only mask deeper problems. Treat the SPU slider as a shock absorber for micro jitter, not a cure for frame drops. Make one change at a time and play a busy scene for sixty to ninety seconds to let your ears tell you whether stability improved.

Use repeatable passages to measure the effect of SPU adjustments rather than guessing. A menu with constant music and periodic cursor sounds exposes dropouts quickly, because the tonal floor is steady while transient clicks stand out. If pops occur only during heavy scenes, raise latency one step and retest the same sequence, noting whether artifacts shift from frequent to rare. The limitation is that some titles react differently to timing, so keep a second test track from another game to avoid overfitting to one scenario. If higher latency fails to improve crackle, revert to your baseline and look to sync mode or output buffer size, which may be the real bottleneck.

Section highlights

  • Use moderate SPU latency as a shock absorber for micro jitter.
  • Test repeatable scenes and adjust in small, measurable steps.

2) Choose the right audio sync mode

Choosing synchronous audio aligns sound precisely to emulation time, which best preserves pitch and event timing when your system maintains full speed. For example, if your machine holds the target frame rate consistently, synchronous output keeps footsteps, menu bleeps, and cutscene dialogue locked to video. The tradeoff is that strict sync exposes slowness without smoothing, so you may hear rapid pops or short gaps during hiccups. Time stretch can hide small dips by lengthening or compressing audio to fill frames, but it slightly alters pitch unless correction is enabled. A rule of thumb is to prefer synchronous on stable hardware and enable stretch only when occasional dips are unavoidable. If you use stretch, set its allowed range conservatively so it smooths blips without rubber-banding.

When deciding between synchronous and stretch, measure your worst case rather than your average frame time. Games with heavy effects or high geometry load may spike periodically, causing audible artifacts under strict sync. In that case, time stretch with a narrow range can bridge the gap while keeping the soundtrack natural. The downside is that overzealous stretching can smear transients and add a subtle seasick feel when ranges are wide. Keep pitch correction on so notes stay true, then evaluate using a looped music section. For a deeper walkthrough across timing paths, consult the comprehensive DuckStation troubleshooting guide with reproducible steps for validating stability end to end.

Section highlights

  • Prefer synchronous audio on stable hardware to preserve exact timing.
  • Use time stretch narrowly when dips occur, with pitch correction.

3) Size the output buffer to prevent dropouts

Output buffer size determines how much audio is queued ahead, which can absorb brief spikes but increases delay. If you hear occasional crackle during busy scenes, increase the buffer slightly to build headroom without making menu sounds feel sluggish. For instance, doubling a too-small buffer often eliminates periodic pops from short CPU bursts while adding only a few milliseconds of lag. The tradeoff is that very large buffers hide timing issues and create a disconnect between actions and sound. Test with rapid UI navigation where delay is obvious. If the interface feels sluggish after an increase, step back to the smallest size that plays cleanly through your worst-case scene. Buffer tuning works best after setting SPU latency and sync mode, since those govern timing flow.

Cross-check buffer changes against your platform’s audio backend because different drivers schedule work differently. On some systems, the default backend handles modest buffers reliably while alternative backends require larger minimums to avoid underruns. A concrete example is switching from a shared OS mixer to a lower-latency exclusive path, which often supports smaller buffers with fewer dropouts. The limitation is that exclusive modes can block other apps’ sounds and may fail on certain devices. Keep a note of each buffer step you test alongside the backend used, then lock the smallest clean value that survives a two-minute stress scene. If pops persist even at reasonable sizes, investigate time stretch limits or sample rate matching to remove hidden resample penalties.

Section highlights

  • Increase buffer slightly to absorb spikes without excessive delay.
  • Validate on worst-case scenes and note backend-specific behavior.

4) Match sample rate and resampler quality

Matching the emulator’s output sample rate to your device’s native rate reduces costly resampling and lowers the chance of pops. If your OS reports a 48 kHz default, set DuckStation to 48 kHz so the mixer can pass audio through cleanly. This alignment keeps processing simple and avoids small timing drift that can accumulate into clicks. For example, forcing 44.1 kHz into a 48 kHz pipeline adds conversion overhead and can amplify artifacts when the CPU is busy. The tradeoff is that some DACs sound slightly better at one rate due to hardware filters, so let your ears decide after aligning to the system’s preferred rate. If you desire higher quality, increase resampler precision carefully because steep filters cost CPU and may reintroduce dropout risk under load.

Use a predictable test track to compare resampler quality settings fairly. A sustained synth pad with clear high-frequency content reveals dullness when using low-precision resampling. If raising precision audibly improves detail without triggering crackle during stress scenes, keep the higher setting. Otherwise, return to a lighter option and reclaim headroom for hard moments in gameplay. Another practical check is to watch OS-level meters for rate conversion, ensuring the stream remains at your chosen rate through the entire path. Section highlights

  • Match output rate to system default to minimize conversions and clicks.
  • Raise resampler precision only if benefits outweigh CPU cost.

5) Limit time stretch range and fix pitch

Time stretch is most helpful when its range is narrow and pitch correction is enabled, because small adjustments hide dips without sounding rubbery. Set a modest maximum stretch down and up so the engine corrects only brief slowdowns rather than chasing every fluctuation. For example, allowing just a small percent of stretch can remove choppy dialogue in heavy cutscenes while preserving musical character. The tradeoff is that too little range does not cover longer slowdowns, producing recurring gaps as the buffer runs dry. If that happens, first confirm your SPU latency and output buffer are not undersized, then widen the stretch range slightly. Keep pitch correction on to maintain note accuracy when speed modulates, and validate with looped vocals where pitch drift is easiest to detect.

Use a two-step evaluation to avoid overfitting the stretch range to one scene. First, tune the range during a known stress point until pops disappear and the soundtrack feels natural. Second, replay a quiet ambient area, listening for pumping or unnatural chorus that indicates too much modulation. If you hear those side effects, trim the range by a small margin and retest. This method balances stability against transparency. Note that pitch correction carries a minor CPU cost, which can compound if your system is already near its limit. If enabling correction triggers new artifacts under load, revisit renderer and sync decisions that reduce overall latency, then return to pitch tools.   • Keep stretch ranges small and enable pitch correction for transparency.
  • Retest in calm scenes to catch pumping or chorus side effects.

6) Dial reverb to avoid muddiness

Moderating reverb can make clicks and timing faults easier to hear during tuning. Some PS1 mixes use generous echo that masks transients, which complicates diagnosis. Temporarily reducing reverb helps you detect whether a pop originates from timing or from effect tails colliding. For instance, trimming reverb level while adjusting SPU latency reveals whether crackle disappears with timing fixes. The tradeoff is that lowering reverb changes the aesthetic of classic titles, so you should restore your preferred level after stabilization. Use reverb reduction as a diagnostic lens, not a permanent crutch. If a game sounds thin with lower reverb, adjust only during tests. Once your chosen latency and buffer remove artifacts, re-enable the original ambience and confirm the soundtrack remains stable through busy battles.

When reverb is heavy, short bursts like menu clicks may smear into a haze that hides small dropouts. In that scenario, toggle reverb down while performing repeatable UI actions to see if clicks persist. If artifacts remain with reverb minimized, timing is the likely culprit and you should revisit buffer size or sync mode. The limitation is that some titles bake reverb into their samples, making software controls less effective. In those cases, prioritize system-level stability and accept the inherent texture. Pair this test with a stable benchmark scene so you do not chase phantom improvements. After you lock settings, restore the effect and listen to fast percussion where smearing would reappear if timing were still marginal.

7) Pick the best output backend for your platform

Different audio backends handle scheduling and latency differently, so pick one that matches your operating system’s strengths. On Windows, exclusive modes can reduce overhead and allow smaller buffers at the cost of blocking other app sounds. On Linux, selecting a low-latency server can improve stability if configured correctly. On macOS, the native path tends to provide consistent timing with minimal fuss. Try each available backend with the same SPU latency and buffer size, then pick the one that plays your stress scene cleanest. The tradeoff is that some devices or mixers behave inconsistently under alternative drivers, requiring extra setup. Keep notes on the smallest clean buffer for each backend. If none are stable, revisit sample rate matching and stretch range before escalating configuration complexity.

Backend choice also interacts with vsync and renderer timing, because the whole pipeline determines how steadily audio is fed. If you switch to a driver that wakes the CPU more often with smaller chunks, you may need to rebalance SPU latency to keep queues healthy. Use a logging mindset and change one variable at a time. Play a two-minute loop with heavy action and verify both dialogue and effects remain free of crackle. Frequently asked questions

What should I try first to stop audio crackle?

Start with a moderate SPU latency and a slightly larger output buffer. If pops persist, prefer synchronous audio on stable hardware or enable a narrow time stretch with pitch correction for small dips.

Is higher resampler quality always better?

No. Higher precision can improve clarity but costs CPU. Match the output sample rate to the system first, then increase resampler quality only if it audibly helps without reintroducing dropouts.

When should I switch audio backends?

Switch backends if you cannot achieve clean playback with reasonable buffers. Test each driver using the same SPU latency and buffer size, then pick the one that remains stable through your stress scene.

Does time stretch affect game speed?

Time stretch adjusts audio speed to bridge short dips but does not change emulation speed. Keep the stretch range narrow and enable pitch correction to maintain natural sound while smoothing small hiccups.

Why does audio desync from cutscenes sometimes?

Desync often appears when the system cannot maintain consistent timing. Use synchronous audio if the machine is stable or enable a narrow time stretch. Verify SPU latency, buffer size, and sample rate alignment.
