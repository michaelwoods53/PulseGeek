Frame Delay on Miyoo Mini: How to Use Safely

By Austin Price • Published January 8, 2026

You will configure frame delay on Miyoo Mini to reduce input lag while keeping vsync steady and audio clean. This guide assumes RetroArch on stock firmware, default display refresh, and no overclock. We will set conservative limits, test stability, and roll back when edges appear. Expect to tweak frame delay in small steps, verify with a consistent scene, and cross check audio buffering. By the end, you will know when the setting helps, when it hurts, and how to maintain safe margins. If you also need pacing fundamentals, see our related pieces on frame pacing and audio synchronization to support your final settings.

Key takeaways

  • Increase frame delay slowly and test a repeatable, busy scene.
  • Keep vsync enabled to avoid tearing and pacing instability.
  • Stop raising delay at the first audio crackle or dropped frames.
  • Core load spikes reduce safe headroom, so retest per system.
  • Document stable delay per core for quick, risk free reuse.

Plan the work

Start with a clear goal and constraints to guide every frame delay change. The aim is lower input latency without sacrificing vsync stability or audio integrity. Plan to evaluate in a demanding scene that pushes CPU usage, like a busy platformer level or an action screen with many sprites. A repeatable section avoids false positives from easy menus. Define a hard stop: the first sign of crackle, dropped frames, or uneven pacing means backing off one notch. This boundary protects against over-tuning that saves milliseconds but degrades feel. Frame delay works by scheduling emulation closer to the next refresh, which raises risk when cores spike. Planning ahead makes the difference between targeted improvement and a spiral of hidden regressions.

Choose a baseline configuration that removes confounding variables before touching delay values. Enable vsync in RetroArch and disable frameskip so you can observe actual pacing instead of masked stutters. Keep shaders light or off and avoid heavy overlays, which can shift CPU and GPU load during tests. If rewind or autosave are enabled, note that they add overhead and can reduce the safe margin for delay. Testing with these features off yields a generous ceiling, but real play may reintroduce load. To manage this, record your stable value under test conditions and subtract one or two steps for daily use. This approach accounts for ambient tasks and inconsistent scenes.

Establish checkpoints that include audio and visual validations. For audio, listen for faint crackle when scenes get dense, then pause and unpause to catch buffer recovery behavior. For video, watch for micro stutters when scrolling backgrounds or rapidly moving sprites. A clean scroll across a parallax background is sensitive to pacing jitter and exposes issues early. Note temperature and battery level because thermal throttling or low power can mimic heavy load. Finally, document core specific results per system, since a lightweight 8-bit core may tolerate higher delay than a more demanding 16-bit emulator. These checkpoints ensure consistent decisions and faster retesting after firmware updates.

Prepare environment

Begin by locking in synchronized output so frame delay adjustments reflect true timing. In RetroArch, keep video vsync enabled and turn off frameskip to prevent masked drops. Use integer scale or simple scaling and avoid GPU heavy shaders while testing. This removes variable rendering cost that would otherwise fluctuate and obscure the impact of delay. If audio resampler options are available, pick a balanced setting rather than the most expensive filter. The goal is a steady baseline where one variable changes at a time. By setting these defaults, any stutter or crackle that appears after raising delay is attributable to the new scheduling rather than unrelated features.

Next, check audio buffering and device latency, because sound pipelines often reveal strain first. Set a moderate audio buffer size that has behaved well for your library. If your buffer is already razor thin, frame delay can push the system beyond safe margins. Conversely, an overly large buffer can mask problems until they burst as rare pops. A reasonable middle ground makes failures appear at predictable thresholds. Test by looping a scene with rapid sound effects, such as quick jumps or shots, then raise delay in small steps. If sounds begin to fuzz or desync when action spikes, back off immediately and retest stability at a lower value.

Finally, create a per core profile strategy, since load varies widely by emulator. Lightweight cores for 8-bit libraries can often run higher delay than heavier 16-bit cores, while certain systems with dynamic recompilers can spike unpredictably. Note differences in frame time headroom by watching frame time graphs if available, or by counting how often you approach the refresh deadline. If your firmware exposes frame time metrics, use them cautiously and look for trends rather than fixating on single numbers. This preparation allows a tailored approach that respects each core’s behavior and prevents applying risky settings globally across dissimilar systems.

Execute steps

Use a conservative ladder to raise frame delay and lock in stable points. Start from the default value and increase in small increments while monitoring a demanding scene. Each step should be followed by at least thirty seconds of motion and active sound effects. If you notice any audio crackle, a partial tear, or micro stutter during scrolls, revert to the previous stable step and record it as your maximum for that core. Because delay tightens the processing window, background spikes or overlay activity can suddenly tip the system. Keeping a notebook of stable values and the test scene used helps you reproduce results quickly after firmware changes.

  1. Enable vsync: keep tearing out of the equation while testing delay.
  2. Set a repeatable scene: choose heavy motion with consistent sound cues.
  3. Raise delay one step: increase minimally, then observe motion and audio.
  4. Stress the scene: trigger effects and scrolling for at least thirty seconds.
  5. Watch for artifacts: note crackle, judder, or any intermittent frame drops.
  6. Lock the last stable step: back off one notch at the first symptom.

Validate results

Confirm that the chosen frame delay actually improves responsiveness without hidden costs. A practical check is to measure a consistent input reaction, like jump timing in a platformer, and compare feel before and after. While subjective, repeating the same action across several runs reduces bias. If available, frame time overlays help confirm deadlines are met consistently. Stability matters more than a tiny theoretical gain. If reactions feel snappier yet scrolling remains smooth and sound clean, the setting passes. If benefits are ambiguous, prefer the lower value. That conservative choice survives system load changes and avoids surprises in later stages or different games.

Test across multiple cores because safe headroom is not uniform. An 8-bit core with simple audio mixing might tolerate higher delay compared with a heavier 16-bit core that spikes during effects. Run the same validation scene per core and record both the highest clean delay and a daily use value that is one notch lower. This creates a margin for overlays or occasional features you may enable later. Also check menu transitions and save states because file I/O can momentarily raise CPU usage. Consistent results here increase confidence that the setting will hold over real sessions.

Finally, assess interactions with pacing features that target smoothness. Keep vsync active and verify that frame pacing charts, if available, show minimal variance. If you rely on audio sync, confirm that no drift or resampler strain appears during long play. Clean audio is a stringent signal that the system is not occasionally missing deadlines. If latency measurements are important to you, repeat them with the new delay but avoid chasing minimal wins that trade reliability. Validation finishes when you can play a busy scene for several minutes with steady scrolls and no audible artifacts while perceiving a modest but real reduction in delay.

Troubleshoot and optimize

When symptoms appear, isolate the cause before changing multiple settings. If audio crackles during spikes, reduce frame delay one notch and retest. Persistent micro stutter with vsync enabled can indicate an overloaded scene or heavy overlay rather than the delay itself. Disable overlays and shaders temporarily and confirm improvement. If tearing appears, ensure vsync remains on and that any frameskip option is disabled. Only after stabilizing should you try raising delay again. A measured loop of change, stress, and rollback preserves your floor for latency while protecting pacing. This process also builds intuition about each core’s behavior under pressure.

Optimize the rest of the performance stack to widen your safe margin. Keep shaders lightweight and avoid overlays that update every frame. Frequently asked questions

Is frame delay safe to use on every core?

No. Heavier cores have less headroom and can stutter sooner. Test each core separately and back off one step from the highest clean value for daily play.

Why do I hear audio crackle only during explosions?

Effects create short CPU spikes that shrink timing margins. Your delay is marginal in those moments. Lower it one notch and retest the same scene.

Should I disable vsync to reduce latency further?

No. Turning off vsync invites tearing and uneven pacing. Keep it on and pursue latency with modest delay increases and light overlays.

Do shaders or overlays affect safe delay levels?

Yes. Visual filters and HUDs add per frame cost that reduces headroom. Disable them while tuning and subtract one step for everyday use if you keep them on.

Can I raise audio buffer size to hide crackle?

You can, but it trades latency for stability. Prefer lowering frame delay first. If crackle persists at low delay, a moderate buffer increase is reasonable.
