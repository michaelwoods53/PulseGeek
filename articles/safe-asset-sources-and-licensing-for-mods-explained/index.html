Safe Asset Sources and Licensing for Mods

By Sarah Bennett • Published January 16, 2026

Mod authors often ask where safe asset sources come from and how licensing applies across formats like PNG, DDS, or OGG. This guide explains practical definitions, decision lenses, and documentation patterns that keep projects respectful and maintainable. It focuses on assets you can legally use and adapt, why permissions differ by medium, and how to record provenance so changes and redistributions stay clear over time.

Key takeaways

  • Source assets from permissive libraries, original work, or rights you hold.
  • Check license permissions, attribution, share alike, and commercial clauses carefully.
  • Document provenance with SPDX identifiers and verifiable links to creators.
  • Prefer remix friendly terms when distributing modpacks or public builds.
  • Keep a manifest recording edits, formats, and any third party constraints.

Concepts and definitions

Safe asset sourcing starts with clear categories of origin, because license obligations follow the source and not your intent. Original work you create grants you default copyright and flexibility, while third party assets require explicit permission that travels with derivatives. Public domain and CC0 allow broad reuse with minimal obligations, but attribution styles like CC BY demand visible credit in mod pages or manifests. Noncommercial terms can restrict patreon packs or ad supported downloads, so context matters. Game EULAs often prohibit redistribution of native files, even if edited, which limits direct extraction for public release. A good rule is to treat every imported file as carrying a paper trail, and to record when and where you obtained it. Doing so protects you when policies change or creators update terms.

Licenses express permissions and conditions, and understanding their scope avoids accidental overreach. Permissive terms like MIT or BSD typically govern code, while Creative Commons variants speak to media such as textures or audio. Attribution clauses ensure creators are credited where the audience will reasonably see it, which for mods often means a README and published description. Share alike requirements propagate the same license to derivatives, which can complicate mixing with assets under incompatible terms. No derivatives blocks editing beyond verbatim distribution, making it poor fit for reskins or remixes. When an asset combines multiple layers, such as a texture with embedded third party brushes, the most restrictive term often controls. When uncertain, ask for permission or choose a different source to avoid conflict.

Provenance captures the chain of custody for an asset and explains how it evolved through your mod. A simple manifest that lists the original source, license, SPDX identifier, and edits creates a durable record future maintainers can trust. Examples include noting that a PNG converted to DDS added mipmaps and alpha pre-multiplication, which may affect compatibility. Provenance also distinguishes private reference materials you cannot ship from distributable files, preventing accidental inclusion. Tradeoffs appear when detailed records add overhead, but the cost is small compared with takedowns or community disputes. A clean record also accelerates security checks, because reviewers can verify hashes and origins quickly. Treat provenance as part of quality, similar to naming conventions and versioning.

Frameworks and decision lenses

A practical sourcing lens begins with three questions that gradually narrow risk. First, is the asset yours, in the public domain, or clearly licensed for remixing, such as CC BY or CC BY SA. Second, does its license permit redistribution inside modpacks and public mirrors, which is common for permissive terms but not for NC or ND variants. Third, can you meet the attribution or share alike obligations in your documentation without conflicting with other bundled assets. This triage works across textures, models, and audio, and it helps you reject gray sources early. When signals conflict, choose another option or replace the asset with original work to keep distribution simple. That clarity saves time when platforms request compliance evidence.

File formats shape what counts as a derivative, so pick routes that match intended edits. Converting a PNG to DDS typically creates a derivative because compression and mipmaps change the artifact, so attribution or share alike rules still apply. Transcoding OGG to WAV or trimming silence modifies the work, and license obligations continue. Conversely, generating new normal maps from your own high poly model is original work, even if you reference open tutorials. If your project involves platform specific re-packs, verify whether the license permits embedding assets in installers. A limitation is that some creators forbid redistribution through mod loaders while permitting standalone patches, reflecting different expectations about exposure. This nuance favors assets under clear remix friendly terms to avoid confusion.

Pitfalls, limitations, and edge cases

One frequent pitfall is assuming credit alone cures license conflicts, which fails when terms explicitly forbid redistribution or derivatives. For example, many texture marketplaces allow personal use but block repackaging into public mods, even with prominent attribution. Another mistake is mixing incompatible share alike and all rights reserved content in a single distributable package, which creates obligations you cannot meet. A safer pattern is to ship open assets together and require users to fetch restricted files themselves through documented steps. The tradeoff is slightly more friction for the player, but it protects your project and respects creators. When storefront or publisher guidelines mention mod permissions, follow those policies before broader assumptions about fair use that rarely apply to asset bundles.

Rip based sources from game files trigger special risks because EULAs often prohibit distribution of original assets, even if you transform or compress them. Public showcases sometimes tolerate minor excerpts for commentary, but packaging game textures or audio for download is usually disallowed. If you study original materials to match proportions or palettes and then handcraft replacements, your result can be original and distributable. Keep your workflow non destructive and document references to separate research from shipped content. An edge case appears with mods that depend on proprietary shaders or codecs embedded in assets, where redistribution may violate platform rules. In those cases, replacing the dependency or providing an optional patcher avoids shipping restricted components directly.

Attribution placement can also go wrong when it hides credits where players never see them, undermining the spirit of CC BY or similar terms. A practical standard is to include credits in the README and the mod listing, and to note exact asset names where applicable. If the creator requests a specific wording, store it in your manifest so translators and maintainers remain consistent. Another limitation emerges with AI generated assets trained on uncertain datasets, which may carry unclear rights. When provenance is ambiguous, avoid distribution or seek sources with traceable licenses rather than debating gray areas. If you must replace a questionable asset quickly, consider interim placeholders from libraries that explicitly allow modification and redistribution.

Looking ahead

The next step is to establish team rules that standardize asset intake, because predictability reduces mistakes as projects evolve. Start with a short checklist that confirms license type, attribution text, and whether redistribution is permitted in modpacks. Embed this gate in your repository templates so every folder includes a manifest and README fields for credits. As you grow, appoint a reviewer who verifies SPDX tags and confirms links to original sources remain accessible. This process may feel formal if you mod solo, but it scales well when collaborators join. Over time, you will spend less energy on ad hoc decisions and more on creative work, because the rules handle common cases.

Deepening knowledge across formats helps you reason about quality and obligations together, not as separate tracks. When choosing between PNG and DDS for textures, consider how compression settings intersect with license requirements to redistribute derivatives. For audio, determine whether your changes count as transformative edits that carry forward obligations, and document those details alongside sample rate and normalization. If you plan a release on multiple platforms, check their mod guidelines for attribution placement, which may differ between storefronts. To keep learning, review broader packaging practices that reinforce good provenance and compatibility, including manifests and hashes. Incorporating these habits early prevents unpleasant surprises during submissions or community reviews.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use assets extracted from a game I own in my mod?

Typically no. Most EULAs forbid redistributing original game assets, even if transformed. You can study them as reference and create your own replacements, then distribute those originals you authored.

Does attribution alone make an asset legal to redistribute?

No. Attribution is only one license condition. If the license disallows derivatives or redistribution, credit will not grant permission. Verify terms that explicitly permit repackaging and edits.

What is the safest license for texture assets in shared modpacks?

Permissive or remix friendly media licenses such as CC BY or CC BY SA are common choices. They allow redistribution with obligations you can meet through clear credits and consistent manifests.

How should I store attribution and provenance for future maintainers?

Keep a simple manifest per asset folder with SPDX license ID, creator name, source link, and edit notes. Mirror credits in the README and the published mod description so players can see them.

Are marketplace assets safe to include if I purchased them?

Not by default. Many marketplaces restrict redistribution. Read the item terms carefully and confirm whether modpack or public distribution is permitted. When terms are unclear, request written permission.
