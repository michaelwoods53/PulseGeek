Which Video Scaler Is Best on Miyoo RetroArch?

By Logan Ward • Published January 16, 2026

The short answer is that integer scaling is usually the best video scaler for Miyoo RetroArch when you want crisp pixels, minimal blur, and predictable latency. It preserves original pixel edges by multiplying the source image cleanly, which avoids shimmering on movement. When the screen ratio or target resolution mismatches the original system, a limited, lightweight shader can fill the remaining space with tasteful smoothing without adding lag. In this guide, we will compare integer and shader-based scaling, note when each applies, and provide steps to implement and verify settings. Short answer and nuance on video scalers

Integer scaling is the default recommendation because it multiplies the original resolution into whole-number steps that keep pixel edges intact and frame pacing steady. On Miyoo displays, that simple rule of thumb typically yields the cleanest result on sprite art and low-resolution consoles. For example, a Game Boy or NES source maps cleanly with nearest-neighbor at 2x, 3x, or 4x without ringing or blur. The limitation is leftover space when the handheld’s panel does not divide evenly, which can create borders. That tradeoff matters less than blur for most users, though purists may accept small pillars to preserve clarity. The predictable computation also reduces GPU load, which helps avoid micro stutter under tight performance budgets.

Shader-based scaling can complement integer scaling when the aspect ratio or resolution leaves a small remainder that looks awkward. A lightweight pass such as a fast sharpening or mild anti-ringing filter can smooth fractional edges only where needed. The idea is to run integer scaling to the largest whole multiple, then let a shader fill the gaps while keeping most pixels untouched. This hybrid keeps motion clean since the majority of the image follows exact multiples. The tradeoff is shader cost and potential latency if you choose complex filters. On Miyoo hardware, avoid multi-pass effects or heavy convolution and favor simple, single-pass options that keep frame time predictable.

Stretch-to-fit scaling looks tempting when you dislike borders, but it typically introduces uneven pixel sizes that shimmer during scrolling. The mechanism is subpixel resampling that creates moiré and crawling edges on diagonal lines, which becomes distracting in platformers. If you must fill the screen, limit the interpolation to a high-quality but fast method and pair it with a small sharpening step to recover perceived crispness. Even then, expect compromises. The better alternative for most titles is integer scaling with small borders or the hybrid method mentioned earlier. Matching expectations upfront saves time spent chasing artifacts that are baked into fractional resizing math.

When it applies vs when it does not for scalers

Integer scaling applies best to pixel-art consoles where native resolutions are low and consistent across titles. Systems like NES, SNES, and Game Boy families benefit from exact pixel multiplication that preserves artwork intent and controller feel. If the game scrolls horizontally with fine patterns, such as checkerboards or text, integer output prevents crawling and preserves readability. It does not apply cleanly when the panel’s aspect ratio differs widely, which can produce wide borders. In those cases, the cost of borders is often smaller than motion artifacts. The decision hinges on your tolerance for unused space versus blur and shimmer, which usually favors the cleaner integer outcome for retro content.

Shader scaling applies when the leftover border after integer scaling is small enough that a gentle fractional step completes the frame without visible artifacts. The technique is common on handhelds where panel resolution slightly exceeds an even multiple of the source. For example, if 3x integer scaling leaves a narrow edge, a single-pass scaler can interpolate that margin while the central image remains untouched. It does not apply when the remainder is large or when the shader significantly affects performance. If a shader introduces timing jitter or audio pops, disable it and accept borders. Stability and responsiveness usually outweigh marginal gains at the edges.

Full stretch scaling applies rarely and only when viewer preference demands a borderless image, such as for casual play or demonstration. The approach can hide letterboxing but risks shimmering on scrolling backgrounds and diagonal edges. It becomes least harmful on content with large flat areas and minimal fine detail where interpolation artifacts are harder to notice. It does not apply if you are sensitive to input feel or if the device already operates near its performance limits. When used, pair it with vertical sync tuned correctly to avoid tearing, and verify that input latency remains acceptable in quick reflex scenes. If problems appear, revert to integer modes immediately.

How to implement or validate scaling

The fastest path is to set integer scaling, lock aspect ratio, and validate with motion patterns. Start by enabling integer scale and a core-accurate aspect, then confirm that the image centers with expected borders. Use a game with smooth scrolling, like a platformer, and watch fine patterns for shimmer while moving horizontally. If you see crawling, double check that nearest-neighbor is active and that no filter is enabled. When the remainder is small and you want a cleaner edge, add a single-pass shader that focuses on anti-ringing or minor sharpening. Keep overlays and heavy post-processing off during testing to isolate scaler behavior and preserve consistent frame timing.

If you prefer saving different rules per system, create per-core overrides so each console family loads its own scaler defaults. This keeps handheld content distinct from 16-bit consoles and avoids accidental reuse of a shader where it is not needed. After setting a default, use a test suite or a built-in grid pattern to verify that lines remain straight and text stays legible. Then play a few minutes of a demanding scene to ensure no stutter appears during effects. If micro hitching or audio ticks appear, remove the shader and retest. You can revisit advanced performance tuning with broader adjustments when scaling is proven stable.

Frequently asked questions

Is integer scaling always better on Miyoo?

Not always, but it is the default for pixel-art systems. Use integer scaling for crisp edges and consistent motion. If small borders bother you, add one light shader to fill the remainder. Avoid heavy filters that add cost or blur.

Do shaders increase input lag noticeably?

Simple single-pass shaders usually have negligible impact, while complex multi-pass effects can add frame time. On Miyoo, stick to lightweight shaders when needed and test with a fast action scene to verify that input still feels responsive.

Why does my screen shimmer during scrolling?

Shimmer often comes from fractional scaling or smoothing filters that create uneven pixel sizes. Enable integer scaling and nearest-neighbor, then retest with a scrolling grid. If shimmer persists, check synchronization settings and ensure VSync is active.

Should I force full screen to remove borders?

Only if you accept softer edges and potential motion artifacts. Full stretch removes borders but introduces blur and possible shimmer. If you value clarity, keep integer scaling and accept small borders or use a gentle shader to fill minor gaps.

Do I need different scalers per core?

Often yes. Save per-core overrides so handheld systems and 16-bit consoles use defaults that fit their native resolutions. This avoids accidentally applying a shader where it is not needed and keeps results consistent across your library.
