What Is a Physics Solver Iteration Count?

By Ethan Palmer • Published January 2, 2026

Solver iteration count is the number of passes a physics solver performs to satisfy constraints like contacts and joints each step. Higher iterations usually reduce jitter, penetration, and sponginess by letting the solver refine errors repeatedly in the physics domain. Lower counts run faster but can leave visible artifacts under load. This article explains the mechanics behind iteration counts, when to change them, how to implement and verify tuning, and what alternatives exist when passes are not the real bottleneck. We will connect abstract rules to concrete play results, moving from definitions to practical steps with small examples and tradeoffs you can measure.

Key takeaways

  • More solver iterations reduce jitter and penetration but cost CPU time.
  • Velocity iterations help friction and bounce feel consistent under load.
  • Tune per rig or layer before raising global iteration settings.
  • Validate with stacked boxes, sliding blocks, and stress contact tests.
  • If jitter persists, check mass ratios, timestep, and constraint limits.

Short answer and nuance

At its core, a physics solver iteration count is how many times the constraint solver revisits contacts and joints within a single step to reduce error. More iterations push solutions closer to constraint goals, so stacks sink less and joints feel tighter, typically visible as cleaner contacts and reduced jitter. A practical rule is to start with engine defaults, then raise by small increments if you see penetration or slipping at rest. The tradeoff is clear: each pass consumes CPU, and returns diminish as counts climb. Systems with simple interactions may not benefit beyond a modest increase, while heavy ragdolls or tall stacks often need extra passes to maintain believable stability under changing forces.

Two iteration families commonly appear: positional iterations that resolve penetration and constraint drift, and velocity iterations that shape friction, restitution, and stable motion. Engines expose these as separate numbers or combined passes, each influencing different aspects of contact quality. For instance, boosting positional iterations can reduce interpenetration in stacked crates, while upping velocity iterations better preserves bounce and sliding behavior during impacts. The balance matters because overemphasizing one can mask issues in the other. If friction feels weak despite correct materials, additional velocity iterations may help more than purely positional work, though frame-time budgets may push you to split the difference and profile the result carefully.

Importantly, iterations address solver accuracy, not all sources of instability. If your timestep is variable with large spikes, extra passes cannot fully compensate for inconsistent integration and accumulated error. Likewise, poor mass ratios or unrealistic joint limits can produce instability that iterations only partially smooth. This is why many teams pair iteration tuning with a fixed or semi-fixed timestep and reasonable mass scaling. The guiding heuristic is to treat iterations as refinement, not a cure-all: use them to polish contacts once basic simulation hygiene is in place. Otherwise you risk paying computational cost while the underlying cause continues to produce jitter and constraint violations.

When it applies vs when it does not

Iterations help most with stacked bodies, dense contact networks, and articulated rigs where constraints pull in different directions. In these scenarios, additional passes reduce residual error after each sweep, so piles settle without sinking and joint chains stop quivering at rest. A good example is a warehouse scene where tall stacks used to creep over time. Raising positional passes by a small amount often removes the drift. The tradeoff shows up in scenes with many active contacts, where even a two-step increase can push CPU past budget. To stay practical, favor local overrides for troublesome rigs before touching global defaults, and keep a profiler session focused on physics and main thread costs.

Iterations help less when the core issue is timestep instability or unrealistic parameter choices. If frame rate swings widely, the solver integrates motion unevenly and iteration refinements chase moving targets. In that case, a fixed or semi-fixed timestep and clamped substeps can yield larger gains. Similarly, extreme mass ratios or aggressive joint limits cause impulses that iterations alone cannot tame. Think of a tiny object welded to a huge body, or a hinge driven beyond reasonable angular ranges. For these, corrective strategies include mass scaling, motor strength limits, and constraint damping. Iteration increases can still polish results afterward but should not lead the intervention.

Iterations are also a poor substitute for correct collision detection settings. Continuous collision detection prevents tunneling in fast movers by addressing missed contacts before the solver even starts. If bullets or fast vehicles pass through thin walls, more passes will not recover contacts that were never created. Instead, enable continuous modes selectively for high-speed actors, then revisit iteration counts for contact quality. How to implement or validate

Implementation usually means adjusting two numbers: position iterations and velocity iterations, either globally or per-asset. Begin with a controlled test scene that includes a crate stack, a sliding block, and a ragdoll or joint chain. Increase positional passes until stacks stop creeping under moderate load, then raise velocity iterations to stabilize friction and restitution. A safe approach is to change one dimension at a time in small increments, profiling after each step to watch CPU budgets. If changes help only partially, audit collision layers and contact offsets, since poor contact generation wastes solver effort. When done, replay deterministic sequences to confirm improvements persist across seeds and minor timing noise.

Validation hinges on targeted tests that surface typical failure modes. For stacks, drop several cubes with varying masses and observe resting penetration and lateral creep over thirty seconds. For friction, slide a block down a gentle ramp and measure travel distance, then increase velocity iterations and compare. For joints, swing a pendulum or drive a hinge with a motor and watch overshoot decay. Track results with simple metrics like maximum penetration depth and settled tilt angle rather than impressions. Alternatives and related questions

Before pushing iteration counts higher, consider timestep control, contact offsets, and material parameters that shape the solver’s job. A fixed or semi-fixed update with clamped substeps often stabilizes integration enough that default iterations look better without extra cost. Correct contact offsets prevent deep penetrations that are expensive to resolve, and well-chosen friction and restitution reduce oscillations. Another lever is per-rig overrides, where a demanding ragdoll gets more passes than the world baseline. This targeted approach protects frame budgets while addressing the true hot spots. If instability persists, inspect mass ratios and constraint limits, choosing conservative ranges that avoid explosive impulses in the first place.

Adjacent choices can change whether more solver passes are even necessary. Continuous collision detection for fast movers captures contacts early, avoiding missed manifolds that no number of iterations can reconstruct later. Constraint damping, solver warm starting, and sleep thresholds also influence stability and perceived jitterness. When picking tactics, ask whether you are compensating for missing contacts or refining existing ones. If it is the former, improve detection. If it is the latter, refine the solve. Finally, remember that iteration count interacts with art and design constraints. Sometimes a slightly softer simulation reads better on screen, and higher passes make motion feel unnaturally stiff. Other times, designers prefer sharp, grippy friction that needs extra velocity iterations to land consistently during fast action. Treat tuning as a conversation with aesthetics and hardware limits. Build a small matrix of goals, like stack stability, slide distance, and joint responsiveness, then map settings that hit acceptable ranges. Revisit the plan as scenes change, keeping a record of baselines and diffs. That discipline makes future adjustments fast and reduces guesswork under deadline pressure.

Frequently asked questions

How many solver iterations should I use by default?

Start with engine defaults and raise in small steps only if you observe penetration, sliding at rest, or unstable joints. Many projects find modest increases sufficient, but always profile because each pass adds CPU cost.

What is the difference between position and velocity iterations?

Position iterations mainly reduce penetration and constraint drift, improving stack stability. Velocity iterations affect friction and restitution responses, shaping how sliding and bounce behave. Both interact, so tune one at a time and recheck results.

Will more iterations fix tunneling at high speed?

No. Tunneling is a collision detection issue. Use continuous collision detection for fast movers so contacts are created in time for the solver. Extra passes cannot recover contacts that were never generated.

Should I change global iterations or override specific rigs?

Prefer per-rig or per-layer overrides for demanding assets like ragdolls or tall stacks. Adjusting global values affects everything and can waste CPU in simple scenes. Target hot spots first, then reassess the baseline if needed.

Looking ahead

Use iterations as a precise dial rather than a blunt fix. Establish clean baselines with stable timesteps, reasonable mass ratios, and sensible contact settings, then layer in extra passes only where tests prove value. Keep a small suite of scenes that stress stacks, friction, and joints, and log outcomes whenever you change counts. Revisit settings as assets and gameplay evolve, since different levels surface different constraints.
