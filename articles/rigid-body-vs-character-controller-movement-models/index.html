Rigid Body vs Character Controller: Movement Models

By Ethan Palmer • Published December 31, 2025

Choosing between rigid body motion and a character controller sets the feel, stability, and constraints of your movement models. This comparison focuses on how each approach handles grounding, slopes, collisions, and input shaping under common engine features. We evaluate practical tradeoffs that show up in shipped projects and prototypes, so you can align mechanics and performance with your design goals without overfitting to one toolkit.

Key takeaways

  • Rigid bodies excel at emergent interactions but need careful damping and limits.
  • Character controllers give consistent steps, slopes, and ladders with fewer surprises.
  • Mix approaches by using kinematic actors for players and dynamic props.
  • Prefer rigid bodies when gameplay demands believable momentum transfer.
  • Choose controllers when netsync and deterministic replays are priorities.

Evaluation criteria and how to weigh them

Start with player intent fidelity, which measures how inputs map to perceived motion under varied conditions. A rigid body integrates forces and impulses, so intent passes through mass, friction, and solver steps that can add expressive momentum but also overshoot on tight ledges. A character controller typically resolves velocity with kinematic sweeps, clamping slopes and steps for predictable displacement. If your design relies on wall kicks, ladder snaps, or precise edge stops, tighter intent fidelity often favors a controller. If the experience celebrates inertia, sliding, and believable pushes, the rigid body delivers. The weighting depends on whether misses feel earned through physics or frustrating because they contradict clear inputs.

Next evaluate environmental stability across uneven ground, moving platforms, and changing collision states. Rigid bodies naturally conserve momentum and may jitter when contacting sharp edges or stacked geometry if solver iterations are low. Controllers usually implement ground detection and projection that smooths micro steps and floating point seams, which improves readability in platformers. However, controllers can tunnel if sweeps miss thin features or if frame stutters desync ground checks. Budget small margins like skin width and floor snap distance to mitigate issues. When worlds are complex with many dynamic bodies, plan solver tuning and culling. For a broader foundation, see how engines coordinate systems in this guide on how engines turn inputs into responsive worlds.

Finally consider integration cost, extensibility, and networking requirements. Rigid bodies tie into physics materials, joints, and constraints, enabling emergent puzzles and believable collisions but requiring careful collision filtering. Controllers centralize logic with explicit states like grounded, sliding, or climbing, making animation coupling and netcode reconciliation simpler. Deterministic replays often prefer controllers due to fewer nondeterministic solver branches, while client prediction for physics needs authoritative corrections. Weight these factors by team strengths and live-ops tolerance for edge-case bugs. If physics depth is a priority, see a practical guide to collisions, joints, and solver stability to anticipate tuning time and constraints.

Deep dives per attribute with tradeoffs and concrete examples

Grounding depends on how motion resolves against walkable surfaces. Controllers often compute a ground normal via ray or shape casts, then project intended velocity onto the plane to prevent unwanted vertical drift. This makes stair travel and shallow slopes consistent, especially when step height and max angle are tuned. Rigid bodies rely on contact manifolds and friction, which can slip under heavy loads or low solver iterations. You can mitigate with higher static friction, slight linear damping, and stable timesteps. For a focused comparison of detection phases, see when to tune broadphase versus narrowphase collision so your choice does not inherit avoidable tunneling or missed contacts in tight spaces.

Networking and determinism hinge on predictability. Controllers advance with explicit velocities, so client prediction mirrors server results with fewer floating differences. That helps rollback models and replays that must match frame by frame. Rigid bodies introduce nondeterminism from contact ordering and solver iteration counts, which complicates reconciliation. You can reduce drift by fixing timesteps and keeping mass ratios within safe ranges, but authoritative correction still needs clear thresholds for snap versus blend. For timing choices that stabilize simulations, review when to prefer a fixed timestep to limit jitter and desync, especially where animations and camera states depend on consistent updates.

Interaction with dynamic props showcases the biggest contrast. With a rigid body avatar, pushing crates, riding debris, and exchanging momentum feel natural because impulses propagate through the solver. With a controller, props remain inert unless you apply forces manually, which can appear artificial without careful cueing. Hybrid designs keep the avatar kinematic but inject impulses into contacted bodies based on collision normals and relative velocity. This reinforces world believability without inheriting full dynamic instability. When moving platforms enter the scene, prefer explicit platform velocity transfer in a controller, or constrain a dynamic body with joints when you want motion to inherit oscillation and sway naturally.

Fit by scenario with short, explicit recommendations

For precision platformers and first person shooters, prefer a character controller that treats steps, slopes, and ladders as parameters. Tight input fidelity and predictable dismounts from edges outweigh the loss of emergent pushes. Ground snapping and slope clamping deliver clean silhouettes for animation and camera logic. If movement spans varied surfaces with many thin edges, add a brief tolerance like skin width to prevent snagging. When traversal includes wall running or vaulting, extend the controller state machine rather than leaning on friction alone. To reason about sensing for jumps and ledge finds, compare rays and volumes in this piece on choosing rays or shapecasts for navigation.

For physics driven sandboxes, vehicle levels, or heavy prop interaction, use a rigid body avatar with tuned materials and damping. This setup rewards momentum management and dynamic puzzles like swinging cranes or collapsing stacks. Keep mass ratios bounded and prefer continuous collision for faster actors to avoid tunneling through thin geometry. If moving platforms are frequent, constrain relative motion with joints or transfer platform velocity explicitly to reduce sliding. For platform logic that stays stable with elevators and lifts, study kinematic setups for transport systems and decide whether the avatar or the platform should own authority in netcode.

For hybrid designs, keep the player kinematic and author impulses into nearby rigid bodies on impact events. This preserves precise inputs while props still move believably. Use friction and restitution on props to sell contact while clamping player speed to avoid knockback that breaks readability. In fast scenes or high latency conditions, restrict physics to deterministic or server authoritative objects and simulate local presentation for the rest. Where collision speed risks tunneling, weigh techniques in continuous versus discrete collision detection to balance performance against accuracy for your specific velocity ranges.

Looking ahead

The fastest path to clarity is a graybox that isolates slopes, stairs, moving platforms, and edge cases like thin rails. Instrument intent fidelity by logging requested versus actual displacement per frame, and flag deltas above a small threshold to catch surprise drifts. With a rigid body, sweep through diverse materials to sense how friction alters landings, then iterate damping and solver iterations in small increments. With a controller, chart how step height and skin width trade clean traversal against snag risk. The exercise anchors debates in numbers and clips instead of opinions and diagrams.

Lock in decision gates before content scales. Define the maximum tolerable variance in jump height and run speed across frame rates, the largest acceptable slide on slopes, and the number of missed ground snaps per thousand steps. Use those gates as automated checks in CI by replaying deterministic inputs across levels. If rigid bodies cannot meet stability gates without losing intended expressiveness, move interaction duties to props while keeping the avatar kinematic. If controllers feel sterile, inject micro forces on landings or camera bob tied to surface roughness.

Plan extensions based on roadmap features. If you expect grapples, ladders, and wall runs, a controller state machine grows cleanly. If you foresee vehicles, debris storms, or breakable supports, lean into rigid body motion and reserve budget for solver iterations. In both paths, keep a single source of truth for ground state and platform velocity transfer to avoid divergent logic. For additional systems context, compare collision phases or solver tuning using targeted reads rather than broad rewrites, and save experiments as toggles so playtesters can A/B the feel rapidly.

Frequently asked questions

Can I switch from a rigid body to a controller mid project?

Yes, but plan for animation, input, and networking rewrites. The avatar’s state machine, grounding logic, and collision layers change, so set aside time for retuning slopes, steps, and camera reactions.

Do controllers work with dynamic objects like crates and debris?

They work, but props will not react unless you apply impulses manually. Inject forces on contact events or let the server author pushes while the client shows predicted motion to keep consistency.

Why does my rigid body slide on gentle slopes?

Sliding usually results from low static friction, insufficient solver iterations, or high mass ratios. Raise friction, limit extreme masses, and consider a fixed timestep to reduce contact jitter and drift.

Is continuous collision detection required for fast players?

Use it when velocities risk tunneling through thin geometry. Enable continuous modes selectively for the player or high speed movers, and keep shapes simple to control the performance cost.

Which approach is better for rollback netcode?

Character controllers are usually easier to predict and rewind because motion is explicit. Physics driven motion can work, but reconciliation logic must handle nondeterministic contacts and corrective snaps.
