How to Set Alerts for 6 GHz DFS Events

By Rachel Morgan • Published February 16, 2026

Goal: build dependable alerts when 6 GHz DFS events occur, without flooding operators. You will plan routing, thresholds, and suppression rules that respect radar detection behavior and client roaming needs. Assumptions include controller access, a syslog or webhook destination, and at least basic rights in your alert platform. By the end, alerts for DFS actions such as channel move or CAC start will reach the right people with clear context. This guide balances alerts, DFS signals, and 6 GHz specifics so teams see what matters and ignore transient noise from non action events. We avoid vendor lock by describing mechanisms that map to most controllers and SIEMs, and give examples you can validate quickly in a lab or a maintenance window.

Key takeaways

  • Alert on actionable DFS states, not every informational log line.
  • Use per-AP and band filters so non 6 GHz noise stays out.
  • Require brief persistence before firing to avoid transient triggers.
  • Include channel, AP, and client impact fields in notifications.
  • Test webhooks with synthetic DFS payloads before enabling alerts.
  • Tune suppression so repeated CAC messages do not dominate alerts.

Plan the work

Start by defining which DFS events are truly actionable on 6 GHz and which are informational. Actionable items generally include channel availability check started, CAC failed, radar detected, and channel move completed. Informational items like periodic status reports rarely warrant paging. A useful rule of thumb is to alert only when client service could degrade for more than a brief window, such as a mandatory channel switch. That distinction reduces noise and keeps responders focused. As an example, a channel move that takes longer than your roaming tolerance should notify on-call, while a single radar detection with immediate recovery can route to a lower priority queue for review.

Next, decide who needs which alert and why. Network operations may want paging for sustained CAC failures, while architects prefer daily summaries of DFS patterns across 6 GHz cells. Map each event type to an action, such as investigate AP neighbor plan or verify MLO path diversity. Consider mixed fleets and ensure the taxonomy works across vendors so multi controller teams receive consistent signals. When routing, prefer channels that match urgency. For example, page for repeated band resets in a defined time window, and send email for isolated, non recurring detections where clients did not disconnect.

Finally, anchor your alert logic to measurable context. Include AP name, band, channel width, and whether 320 MHz puncturing or narrower fallback is in effect. That detail explains impact at a glance and speeds triage. If you analyze wide lanes, reviewing an overview of 320 MHz channels efficiency and puncturing in real deployments helps set expectations for usable airtime during changes. Add regional DFS constraints and building materials to your notes so responders understand why some areas see more events. Tradeoffs appear when you tighten thresholds too much, so document assumptions and keep a small buffer for transient fluctuations.

Prepare environment

Confirm your controller or APs emit structured events that identify 6 GHz DFS conditions. Look for fields like band, channel, radar detected, CAC status, and AP identifiers. If logs are unstructured, plan a parser in your SIEM to extract those attributes reliably. Establish a transport path using syslog over TLS or webhooks with authentication where possible. Before creating alerts, verify time synchronization so event ordering remains accurate across sources. If you plan to visualize trends, consider building a small dashboard that tracks rate of DFS detections per AP and per channel. Select notification channels based on urgency and operator availability. Paging should be rare and reserved for persistent failures such as repeated CAC timeouts. Chat messages work for non urgent awareness, while email suits daily summaries. Many teams mirror high severity alerts into incident tools to track acknowledgment and resolution. Evaluate each channel’s rate limits and formatting constraints. For instance, some chat systems truncate long JSON payloads, so include a concise summary along with a link to the incident record. Execute steps

Implement alert rules in small increments so you can verify behavior and noise. Start with a filter that matches 6 GHz band events only, then layer event types such as radar detected, CAC failed, and channel move. Where supported, add a persistence window such as require the condition for 90 seconds before firing. This avoids flapping alerts during brief scans. Normalize AP names and include channel width in the payload so responders see whether 320 MHz, 160 MHz, or narrower widths were active. Provide a clear title like DFS radar detected on 6 GHz with AP name and channel. Route high severity items to paging and others to chat or email for awareness.

Before enabling global routing, test with a synthetic payload. Most webhook receivers accept application/json posts, and you can simulate realistic fields. This confirms auth, formatting, and triage notes render correctly in the destination. Observe whether long strings wrap poorly or if timestamps parse correctly. Validate results

Validation starts with event integrity. Confirm that a single radar detection in logs maps to exactly one alert in your destination. Check timestamps and ensure they are in UTC or consistently converted so sorting works. Measure the time from event generation to alert receipt, aiming for a predictable window such as under one minute during steady load. Next, look for missing context fields. If AP name or channel width is absent, fix parsing or payload templates. Validate that non 6 GHz events do not match your rules by triggering known 5 GHz changes and confirming no alerts fire. This prevents cross band noise from eroding trust in the system.

Move to impact validation by observing client behavior during a controlled channel move. Track association counts, retries, and throughput for a small test group before and after the event. The goal is to verify that your alert correlates with measurable service changes without exaggerating transient effects. Where possible, look at multi link operation and see whether an alternate link masked disruption. If that occurs, keep the alert at awareness level rather than paging. For ongoing visibility into capacity during channel transitions, consult resources that explain interpreting throughput and airtime dynamics on wide channels to set reasonable expectations.

Finally, test suppression and cooldown behavior. Trigger repeated synthetic events to ensure aggregation works and that one incident remains open until resolution criteria are met. Document edge cases such as an AP reboot that resets counters and might create unexpected sequences. Review alert volumes for a week and compare against historical DFS frequency to spot gaps or over sensitivity. Adjust thresholds or routing if operators missed important incidents or felt overloaded. This iterative loop hardens your alerting so it stays useful as RF conditions change seasonally or building usage evolves.

Troubleshoot and optimize

When noise appears, examine matching logic first. Many false positives stem from broad text filters that capture unrelated messages. Replace raw string matches with structured fields or anchored patterns that include band equals 6 GHz and explicit DFS states. If your controller outputs batch summaries, ensure your parser does not treat them as unique events. For genuinely chatty environments, increase the persistence window or require two sightings before firing. The tradeoff is slower detection, which is acceptable for awareness grade items but not for sustained CAC failures. Adjust by event type to preserve responsiveness where it matters most.

If alerts are missing, confirm upstream logging is reliable. Check that APs report to the controller during channel transitions and that timestamps do not drift. Inspect queue backlogs in your collector and raise resource limits if ingestion spikes during busy hours. For webhook paths, verify certificate trust and TLS settings to avoid intermittent delivery issues. Some platforms silently drop payloads that exceed limits, so shorten summaries and move details into structured fields. When coverage and capacity are tight, consider narrowing 320 MHz widths or enabling puncturing rules to reduce the likelihood of disruptive moves while you investigate root causes.

Improve operator experience by tightening content and adding visuals where helpful. Include a short playbook in the alert that lists immediate checks such as neighbor channels, noise floor trends, and client disassociation counts. Provide a link to your internal runbook on DFS responses and a tag that auto routes to the current on-call rotation. Periodically review event volumes and update thresholds based on seasonality or new building occupants. If your platform supports analytics, compare DFS patterns over months to predict hotspots. Frequently asked questions

Which 6 GHz DFS events should page on-call?

Page on persistent CAC failures, repeated radar detections within a short window, or channel moves that exceed your roaming tolerance. Route single detections with quick recovery to chat or email.

How do I prevent false positives from non 6 GHz logs?

Filter on structured fields that include band equals 6 GHz and explicit DFS states. Avoid broad text searches and add a short persistence window before firing to eliminate transient matches.

What context should every DFS alert include?

Include AP name, band, channel, channel width, timestamp, and a brief summary of the action taken. Optional fields like client disconnect count and region help prioritize response.

How can I test alert routing safely?

Use a staging webhook or incident queue and post a synthetic DFS event with curl. Verify a 2xx response, correct formatting, and that the message lands in the expected channel.
