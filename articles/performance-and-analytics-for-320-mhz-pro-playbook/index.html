Foundations for 320 MHz throughput analytics

Good analytics starts with precise terms, because wide channel behavior can mask issues if definitions drift. Throughput should be reported alongside airtime utilization and effective latency percentiles, since 320 MHz lanes amplify contention patterns. A rule of thumb is tracking P50 and P95 latency with fixed packet sizes, then tying those values to retries and MCS selections over the same sampling window. For example, a rising P95 with steady P50 often signals queuing or collision bursts, not pure RF problems. The tradeoff is granularity, because overly short windows create flicker while long windows blur transient spikes. Most teams settle on 30 to 60 second windows for dashboards and use shorter slices only in forensic captures.

Utilization requires consistent math, because 320 MHz bandwidth tempts tools to mix PHY capacity assumptions with airtime accounting. Prefer channel utilization derived from carrier sense duty cycle and transmit occupancy rather than synthetic bits per second against theoretical maxima. As an example, a dashboard that calculates utilization from busy time plus transmit time reflects contention more faithfully during multi-link operation. The limitation is hardware variance, since some radios round busy time coarsely, which hides microbursts. Cross checking with retry rates and block acknowledgement efficiency helps validate the reading. If airtime rises and retries stay low, congestion likely originates from large flows or queue depth rather than a noisy floor.

SNR and RSSI must be interpreted with caution on ultra wide channels, because noise power scales with bandwidth. A client showing moderate SNR on 320 MHz may still achieve high MCS if interference is low and scheduling is efficient. A practical technique is plotting SNR against MCS and retry percentage to spot where rate adaptation is bumping ceilings. Edge cases include mixed fleet devices that downshift to narrower channel support, which skews averages if not segmented. For those situations, consider using targeted guidance on how to interpret SNR and RSSI on ultra wide channels, ensuring your assumptions match the client’s PHY behavior and the AP’s channelization plan.

Pitfalls and edge cases to watch

Wide lanes tempt optimistic assumptions, but 320 MHz channels are not magic bandwidth. A common mistake is reading bits per second without checking airtime and retries, which hides how a single bulky flow can crowd others. Another trap is ignoring puncturing, where excluded subchannels distort SNR and effective capacity in subtle ways. To calibrate expectations, study guidance that explains wide lane efficiency and puncturing guidance so the analytics reflect true availability. Edge cases include clients that only partially support the width, leading to quiet underperformance. Segment results by client capability when possible, and validate that your test pairs both use the intended channelization without fallback.

Latency tests can mislead if sampling windows shift during DFS or roaming events, because percentile calculations span heterogeneous conditions. When a DFS event forces channel changes, reset your baseline and annotate the dashboard to avoid mixing apples with oranges. Frequently asked questions

How long should a throughput test run on 320 MHz?

Run for two to five minutes with fixed packet sizes and stream counts. This balances rate adaptation stabilization with operational practicality while keeping results comparable across days and sites.

What latency metrics best describe user experience?

Track both P50 and P95 latency using a consistent payload size. Rising P95 with steady P50 often indicates queue pressure or contention bursts rather than a pure RF issue.

How do I compare analytics across different controllers?

Normalize sampling windows and align fields such as busy time, retries, MCS, and latency. Export raw counters when possible and apply the same baselines to every platform during evaluations.

Do wider channels always increase throughput?

No. Wider lanes can increase contention and noise, especially with puncturing or mixed client capabilities. Validate with airtime, retries, and latency before attributing gains to channel width.

When should I reset baselines during monitoring?

Reset after DFS events, roaming changes, or major policy updates. Mixing data across those transitions blurs signals and can produce misleading comparisons in your dashboards.
