Post-Processing Stack for Games: Effects and Costs

By Ethan Palmer • Published January 12, 2026

Post-processing effects can transform a serviceable frame into a look that reads immediately, yet every filter adds cost and risk. Understanding the stack as a programmable sequence helps you reason about effects and their cumulative costs. We will map common components such as bloom, tone mapping, color grading, and motion blur to the GPU and bandwidth realities that decide frame time. By treating each effect as a step with inputs, thresholds, and resolution dependencies, you can predict failure modes before they surprise players.

Key takeaways

  • Order effects to avoid reprocessing pixels and compounding bandwidth costs.
  • Target half or quarter resolution for bloom and similar blur passes.
  • Prefer LUT-based grading for predictable cost and stable color results.
  • Cap motion blur samples based on target frame time budget and camera speed.
  • Measure with per-effect toggles to see each effect’s true impact.

Concepts and definitions

A post-processing stack is a sequence of full-screen passes that modify the rendered image after lighting, usually in a single or few composited textures. The order matters because many effects read and write the entire frame, so bandwidth dominates cost. Bloom takes bright pixels, creates a blurred contribution, and combines it back, often at half or quarter resolution to limit taps. Tone mapping converts high dynamic range values to display-referred space using a curve, where filmic operators compress highlights gracefully. Color grading uses a lookup table, or LUT, to remap colors consistently with fixed cost. Motion blur samples along velocity vectors to simulate shutter time, trading clarity for perceived smoothness when frame time spikes.

Stack order should reduce unnecessary work by placing early filters that cut energy or resolution before broad blurs. A common pattern applies exposure and tone mapping near the end, reserving early stages for bloom thresholding and downsampling. For example, thresholding bright areas before blur reduces samples and preserves edges, while applying bloom after tone mapping can flatten highlights and feel smeary. Color grading via a 3D LUT is best applied when values are display-referred, otherwise the table can clip or shift hues unexpectedly. Motion blur placement depends on your engine’s velocity buffer timing. If applied too early, it can smear temporary render targets or punch-through UI that should remain crisp.

Each effect maps to specific resources, which suggests where cost emerges. Bloom and depth of field are tap heavy and scale with resolution, so they benefit from smaller render targets and aggressive mip use. Tone mapping and LUT evaluation are arithmetic light and bandwidth bound, making them relatively cheap if kept in a single pass. Film grain and vignette are per-pixel and can be combined to minimize extra bandwidth. Ambient occlusion variants range widely in cost depending on sample count and reconstruction, which is why they are best controlled separately from purely stylistic grades. Understanding these mechanics turns trial-and-error into predictable choices aligned to a target frame budget.

Section highlights

  • Sequence effects to reduce bandwidth and limit unnecessary full-resolution work.
  • Place grading post tone mapping for consistent, predictable color transformations.

Frameworks and decision lenses

A useful lens is to budget by resolution sensitivity, since many post effects scale with pixel count more than triangle count. Establish a per-frame budget for post-processing, for example a small fraction of your target frame time, then allocate it across highest impact filters. If your target is 16.6 ms at 1080p, reserve around 2 to 3 ms for the stack and prove each millisecond with toggles. Effects that can operate at reduced resolution, like bloom or some depth of field variants, should downsample early to stabilize cost. Reserve full-resolution passes for tone mapping, LUT, and minimal composite blends. This keeps frame time variance low when window size or dynamic resolution shifts.

Another lens is readability versus style. If your game relies on sharp text and UI integrated into the scene, aggressive film grain and vignettes can harm legibility at smaller screen sizes. Use LUT-based grading to set mood while preserving local contrast for gameplay-critical silhouettes. Motion blur can unify fast camera cuts but risks muddying enemy tells. A rule of thumb is to tie blur intensity to camera velocity rather than input deltas, then clamp sample counts to protect small targets. Bloom should respect a luminance threshold and a soft knee to avoid washing midtones. These guardrails prevent style from contradicting clarity during tense moments.

Finally, choose effect variants that align with platform memory and bandwidth. On lower-end GPUs, prefer single-pass composite pipelines where tone mapping, vignette, and grain share a final pass. On higher-end targets, split passes when precision or ordering benefits justify it. If your engine supports volume blending, predefine profiles for indoor, outdoor, and cinematic scenes, then switch effects rather than toggling parameters constantly. When testing, measure scene classes, not single viewpoints. Examples and short scenarios

Consider a third-person city chase where headlights and neon must feel bright but not blind. Start with a bloom threshold around bright highlights, downsample to quarter resolution, and apply a wide Gaussian for a soft halo. Place tone mapping after lighting is complete, then evaluate a LUT tuned to preserve midtone contrast for character silhouettes. Cap motion blur samples in the range that fits your budget and tie intensity to camera velocity. In testing, toggle each effect to isolate visible change and cost. As a contrasting scenario, picture a stylized sidescroller with flat lighting and punchy color. Heavy bloom would dilute its graphic shapes, so rely on LUT-based grading and a subtle vignette combined in a final pass. Keep motion blur off to preserve input precision during tight jumps, and instead add a tiny amount of film grain to break banding in gradients. If performance dips on handheld devices, reduce vignette strength and grain intensity first, since both are cheap but repeat per pixel. Looking ahead

The next step is to formalize measurement so your stack choices scale across levels and platforms. Create a shortlist of scenes that represent indoor, outdoor, and VFX-heavy moments, then profile them at target resolutions. Lock a post-processing budget and treat it as a first-class constraint during visual reviews. You can also derive “safe mode” profiles that preserve grading while disabling expensive blurs on weaker hardware. Codify order and parameters as presets so creative experiments happen within guardrails. For instance, define a tone mapper and LUT combination as non-negotiable, then allow variations in bloom threshold and intensity within a narrow range. This reduces regressions when multiple artists push looks. If your project uses volumes or zones, annotate them with performance notes such as expected milliseconds at 1080p and 4K. Tie these notes to automated checks in your performance dashboards. Finally, map the relationship between lighting choices and post. When lights change, update the LUT and thresholds together rather than independently to avoid iterative thrash.

As you broaden the toolkit, keep a reference of why each effect exists and when to say no. A clear “do not use” policy for certain scenes can save hours of debate and testing. When you add new features like screen-space reflections or temporal upscalers, re-evaluate stack order and bandwidth pressure. Frequently asked questions

Should tone mapping come before or after color grading?

Apply tone mapping before LUT-based color grading in most pipelines. This ensures the LUT operates on display-referred values, preventing hue shifts or clipping that can occur when grading high dynamic range values directly.

How do I keep bloom from washing out midtones?

Use a luminance threshold with a soft knee and downsampled blur. This isolates bright sources, reduces taps, and prevents low-contrast areas from accumulating glow that flattens detail or hides gameplay-critical silhouettes.

Is motion blur helpful for low frame rates?

It can mask judder slightly, but sample counts increase cost and can hurt readability. Tie intensity to camera velocity, clamp samples tightly, and prefer fixing the underlying performance issues when possible.

What post effects are safest on low-end GPUs?

LUT-based color grading and tone mapping are predictable and lightweight. Simple vignette and subtle grain combined in a single pass are also safe. Avoid heavy depth of field or high-sample ambient occlusion when budgets are tight.

Do post-processing effects impact UI clarity?

Yes, full-screen effects can blur or shift colors in UI. Exclude UI layers from post or composite them after the stack. Keep grain and vignette subtle, and verify small text at typical viewing distances.
