6 GHz Spectrum Availability by Country: Global List

By Olivia Adams • Published February 28, 2026

Planners comparing 6 GHz spectrum across borders need a reliable, country aware framework rather than a static list. This guide organizes availability patterns by region and shows how to confirm the exact status with each regulator for a given country. Items highlight how spectrum access, automated coordination, and power limits shape WiFi 8 design choices. The selections prioritize published rules, repeatable verification steps, and deployment implications that hold up under audit.

Key takeaways

  • Verify country rules directly with the regulator and official gazettes.
  • Map availability to LPI, standard power with AFC, or not permitted.
  • Design channels and power after reading device capabilities by band.
  • Expect regional differences in 6 GHz that affect roaming and capacity.
  • Document assumptions and keep change logs when rules or grants update.

1) North America patterns that shape design choices

In the United States and Canada, planners often see both low power indoor access and standard power coordinated through automated systems, which meaningfully expands 6 GHz spectrum options. A practical approach is to first classify your venue types, then align indoor areas to low power indoor while evaluating outdoor or large arenas for standard power after registration. For example, a campus might keep lecture halls on indoor limits while seeking coordinated grants for stadium coverage to support dense seating. The tradeoff is operational complexity. Coordinated access adds registration steps, grant interpretation, and change management when grants update. That overhead is justified for big outdoor or high ceiling spaces with heavy demand, but smaller offices usually stay with indoor constraints for simplicity and predictable power spectral density compliance.

North American channel planning typically splits wide channels for high capacity indoor areas and narrower channels near coordinated zones to avoid self interference and respect granted limits. In a mixed estate, a hospital could run 80 MHz channels in clinical floors where indoor limits apply, then shift to 40 MHz in a rooftop mesh that depends on granted frequencies. An evidence based safeguard is to read regulator notices and vendor advisories before activating new revisions. For deeper regulatory context like EIRP limits and regional differences that affect plan choices, review this practical guide to plan compliant deployments with EIRP and AFC nuances. The cost of this diligence is time, yet it prevents misconfigurations that later trigger rollback.

  • Check regulator notices and vendor advisories before expanding channels.

2) Europe and UK emphasize lower band indoor access

Across much of Europe and in the United Kingdom, policy commonly favors indoor low power in the lower portion of 6 GHz, which supports office and venue upgrades without coordination. A rule of thumb is to design with conservative power spectral density and focus on building internal isolation that keeps signals indoors. A large museum could deploy access points within galleries while avoiding atriums that leak signal outdoors. This pattern simplifies activation, yet the tradeoff is reduced outdoor flexibility and potentially fewer practical channels during peak events. To understand how tuning and coordination interplay for robust WiFi 8, see this grounded overview of lanes, power options, and client realities. That background helps teams select widths that fit device support while preserving headroom for roaming zones and voice traffic.

Planners should test roaming across stairwells and courtyards where indoor focused limits may stress coverage continuity. For example, a university with connected buildings can keep study rooms on wide channels then carve narrow channels in passages where attenuation changes quickly. The benefit is clean spectrum indoors and predictable duty cycles. The limitation is that certain outdoor courtyards or heritage facades cannot rely on higher power without coordination or extra approvals. Teams often complement 6 GHz with tuned 5 GHz layers for transitional spaces. A balanced approach validates indoor only assumptions during site surveys and documents exceptions such as glass atriums. That documentation becomes critical when stakeholders ask why outdoor event coverage still uses legacy bands in specific corners even as 6 GHz performs well indoors.

Asia Pacific presents a patchwork, with some regulators enabling portions of the band and others signaling staged reviews. The operational takeaway is to treat each country as a unique plan and track official notices rather than assuming regional uniformity. A multinational retailer might find indoor access enabled in one market while another allows only limited testing. The tradeoff is inventory complexity because device firmware and domain settings must follow the local rule set. Because client support varies by region and product line, APAC deployments benefit from pre staging tests that verify domain selection, beacon content, and channel availability masks. A practical scenario is a logistics hub validating that handheld clients recognize planned channels after a country code change during import. The benefit is early detection of channel gaps that might force a narrower plan. The downside is extra lab time and coordination with procurement to ensure SKU alignment. When standard power options are considered, it is prudent to review local guidance and, if applicable, follow any registration steps before outdoor activation. Teams that document device capabilities and country settings reduce risk when firmware updates alter available channels or power constraints across specific sites in the region.

Several Latin American regulators have advanced rulemaking that supports indoor use and in some cases broader access, yet specifics vary country by country. A practical method is to align channel plans with the most conservative market in your footprint and expand only after official confirmation. For instance, a chain of retail stores could standardize on mid width channels that work across multiple countries, then widen where local rules and clients permit. The benefit is smoother logistics and fewer configuration variants. The limitation is possible underuse of available spectrum in markets that offer more flexibility. A major university could implement indoor layers now and plan outdoor expansions only after clear guidance or coordination pathways are established. The upside is immediate capacity gains where rules are firm. The downside is a staggered rollout that requires a communications plan for staff and users. Start by identifying the authority, then search for published decisions regarding indoor limits, standard power, and coordination requirements. A hotel group planning upgrades could log regulator citations, dates, and any conditions on power or channels in a shared register. The benefit is traceability when auditors or partners review your assumptions. The tradeoff is time spent reading legal text and watching for updates. The benefit is rapid, consistent rollouts that still respect local rules. The limitation is the need to maintain templates as regulations evolve. Looking ahead

The safest way to keep a living country list accurate is to schedule periodic reviews tied to regulator calendars and vendor release notes. A quarterly review that reads official gazettes, checks controller domain files, and updates internal templates will catch meaningful changes before they disrupt rollouts. The benefit is proactive adjustments that avoid emergency reconfigurations during peak seasons. The cost is sustained attention and a clear owner who curates citations. Assigning that responsibility to a central team prevents drift. This steady cadence also builds confidence with security and legal stakeholders who look for documented diligence in regulated environments and large public venues.

Visibility across teams matters because spectrum decisions ripple into device procurement, survey methods, and support playbooks. A practical approach is to publish a short internal bulletin that summarizes new approvals or constraints by country, then link to the underlying citations. That way, field teams can act without parsing regulatory text while auditors still have the source trail. The upside is faster execution with fewer misinterpretations. The limitation is bulletin fatigue if it becomes too frequent or verbose. Keep it concise and adopt a standard template. This habit aligns daily work with compliance and turns complex rule sets into actionable steps for planners and technicians.

Finally, decisions about channel width and power should be revisited when client populations shift or when new coordination features roll out from providers. A venue that adds real time video or XR training may need narrower channels in parts of the estate to reduce contention and meet latency targets. The advantage is a network aligned to current workloads rather than historic assumptions. The tradeoff is occasional redesign effort and retraining of on site teams. Incorporate these changes into your decision log and template library so future projects inherit the learning. Over time, your country list becomes more than a catalog. It evolves into a repeatable, compliant process that scales across regions.

Key terms

  • 320 MHz Channels — Very wide channels that allow high peak data rates.
  • Automated Frequency Coordination (AFC) — A system that manages 6 GHz outdoor use to avoid interference with incumbents.
  • Client Station — A Wi‑Fi device that connects to an AP.
  • Low Power Indoor — A 6 GHz device class limited to indoor use with capped EIRP.
  • Power Spectral Density — Power distributed across frequency over channel bandwidth.
  • Regulatory Domain — Region-specific RF rules for power, channels, and DFS.

Frequently asked questions

Where can I find a definitive country list for 6 GHz?

No single list stays authoritative for every country. Use each national regulator’s decisions and official gazettes as primary sources and maintain your own register with citations and dates.

How often should I recheck a country’s 6 GHz status?

Quarterly reviews work well for most teams. Recheck sooner when a regulator publishes new notices, when vendors release domain updates, or before major site launches.

What is the fastest way to verify allowed channels?

Confirm the regulator’s published mask, then compare it with your controller’s regulatory domain file and a sample access point’s channel availability after domain selection.

Do I need coordination for outdoor 6 GHz?

It depends on the country and power class. Many markets require automated coordination for higher power or outdoor service. Always confirm current requirements with the regulator.

How should I handle clients that do not support planned channels?

Create a transitional plan. Use compatible widths and reuse patterns, keep a 5 GHz layer where needed, and document exceptions until client fleets are upgraded.
