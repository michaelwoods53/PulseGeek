Collision Detection: Broadphase vs Narrowphase

By Ethan Palmer • Published December 21, 2025

Collision detection often hinges on choosing how much work to do early versus late. Broadphase techniques quickly sort many bodies to rule out non-interactions, while narrowphase computes precise contacts for likely pairs. We will compare these stages through the lenses of performance, accuracy, and stability, then show how to tune decisions for different genres and object counts. Along the way, we will link the concepts to engine building blocks and simulation loops, so you can integrate collision cleanly with rendering and game logic.

Key takeaways

  • Broadphase reduces pair tests drastically, but misses fine contact detail.
  • Narrowphase finds accurate contacts, with higher per-pair computation cost.
  • Hybrid tuning balances false positives and missed collisions for stability.
  • Dense scenes favor spatial partitioning; sparse fields tolerate simple grids.
  • Use continuous tests only where tunneling risk justifies overhead.

Evaluation criteria and how to weigh them

Start with a clear objective: minimize total frame time while preserving believable contact behavior. Evaluate broadphase by how aggressively it prunes candidate pairs across the full scene, and judge narrowphase by accuracy of contact points, normals, and penetration depth. For example, in a 5,000-body arena, reducing potential pairs by 95 percent often matters more than micro-optimizing triangle tests. The tradeoff appears when broadphase creates too many false positives, which overloads narrowphase anyway. Calibrate weights based on genre: precision-heavy puzzles lean toward narrowphase fidelity, while shooters prioritize pruning speed to keep input latency low.

Quantify cost using stable baselines rather than raw microbenchmarks. Measure the number of pairs leaving broadphase, the average contact manifold size per pair in narrowphase, and the percentage of frames where solver iterations must increase to maintain stability. For instance, if narrowphase contact generation triggers extra solver passes in crowded scenes, those passes become the bottleneck. An edge case occurs with many small fast movers where continuous collision is necessary. In that case, you should count swept tests explicitly and allocate budget to them, or you will underpredict frame spikes during bursts of motion.

Balance accuracy against perceptual thresholds, not theoretical exactness. Players rarely notice a 1 millimeter contact error, but they instantly detect jittering stacks or tunneling bullets. A good rule is to set broadphase so that average overlap volume for false positives remains small, which makes narrowphase quick to refute. Conversely, if false positives become large overlapping volumes, narrowphase must resolve complex geometry unnecessarily. Watch degenerate cases like thin meshes or concave shapes. Use simplified proxy shapes during broadphase to avoid pathological overlaps, then refine with triangle or GJK-based narrowphase only when necessary.

Summarize the stages before diving deeper. Broadphase is a coarse culling pass using grids, trees, or sweep structures to discard most non-colliding pairs quickly. Narrowphase is the precise stage using algorithms like GJK with EPA or SAT to compute contact points and normals. The key attributes worth comparing are computational cost per pair, scalability with object count, accuracy of contact generation, stability implications for the solver, and tuning complexity. Remember that the stages interact. A weak culling strategy may overflow narrowphase, which then propagates instability into the solver and increases iteration counts. Conversely, an overly aggressive broadphase that occasionally misses fast interactions forces you to compensate with continuous checks in narrowphase. Deep dives per attribute with tradeoffs and concrete examples

Computational cost depends on how you update and query spatial structures. Uniform grids work well for evenly distributed objects and predictable motion, because cell hashing is constant time and broadphase can prune quickly. However, grids struggle with large size disparities, where big objects touch many cells. Dynamic AABB trees adapt to distribution and handle size differences better, though insertions and refits add overhead. Sweep and prune excels when velocities are mostly aligned along axes and objects move gradually. A hybrid approach often wins: tree for static geometry plus sweep for moving bodies. If distributions shift during gameplay, rebuild frequency becomes a critical tuning knob.

Accuracy in narrowphase is about choosing the right shape representation and algorithm. For convex shapes, GJK with EPA produces stable penetration depth and normals, while SAT can be competitive for polytopes with few faces. For concave meshes, use convex decomposition or bounded triangle tests to avoid expensive manifold churn. As a practical example, represent character capsules and crates as convex proxies for most interactions, then fall back to mesh triangle tests only for resting contact on terrain. Edge cases include thin obstacles where contact normals flip; here, smoothing thresholds and contact caching reduce jitter and keep the solver predictable over frames.

Stability links collision to the solver through manifold quality and contact persistence. A narrowphase that produces consistent contact points across frames helps warm starting and reduces solver iterations. Conversely, noisy contact features can cause stacking jitter and energy gain. To manage this, clamp restitution for low-speed contacts, enable speculative contacts where available, and constrain friction directions to stable bases. Broadphase contributes by keeping pair churn low, which improves contact caching efficacy. For moving platforms or elevators, kinematic bodies with carefully updated bounds avoid missed pairs. For more scenarios on motion sampling, see guidance on weighing fixed and variable timesteps to limit jitter caused by inconsistent integration.

Fit by scenario with short, explicit recommendations

High-speed projectiles demand selective continuous tests in narrowphase, backed by a broadphase that supports fast swept queries. Use convex proxies for bullets and target hulls, then compute time of impact only for pairs flagged as risky by relative velocity thresholds. This avoids running continuous checks on slow movers. The tradeoff is false negatives in broadphase when bounds are too small; expand temporal bounds slightly to hedge. For accuracy under stress, enable conservative advancement caps to prevent long loops. For more sense-versus-precision choices, weigh approaches for sensing and aiming in the discussion of rays versus volumes.

Dense stacking scenes benefit from a dynamic tree broadphase with infrequent rebuilds and careful fattening of AABBs to reduce pair churn. In narrowphase, ensure contact reduction merges near-coincident points and that friction cones remain consistent across frames. Consider continuous checks only for fast intruders entering the pile. A limitation appears with concave decorations on props, which can cause spurious contact flips; prefer convex decomposition for resting contact. For developers refining contact feel, adjust friction and restitution following guidance on physics materials so the solver receives realistic limits.

Large open worlds with sparse interactions often run well on a simple grid or two-level culling: region streaming first, then local broadphase. Narrowphase can stay minimal with convex proxies, activating mesh detail only near the player. The risk is sudden crowding during events which saturates narrowphase. Mitigate by monitoring pairs per region and elevating tree structures in hotspots. When accuracy must spike temporarily, like during a cinematic crash, budget extra time to narrowphase and reduce AI updates in that frame. If projectiles tunnel across long frames, consider selective continuous collision for critical actors rather than enabling it globally.

Frequently asked questions

Do I always need both broadphase and narrowphase?

Yes for real-time physics at scale. Broadphase limits the candidate pairs to a manageable set, and narrowphase provides the precise contacts the solver requires. Small prototypes with few bodies can sometimes skip broadphase, but performance degrades quickly.

When should I enable continuous collision detection?

Enable it selectively for fast or small objects that might tunnel through thin geometry. Use velocity thresholds or object tags to limit usage. Global continuous checks add noticeable cost without benefit for slow movers and large static objects.

Which broadphase structure is best for dynamic scenes?

Dynamic AABB trees adapt well to changing distributions and varying sizes. Sweep and prune works when motion is mostly axis-aligned and incremental. Uniform grids suit evenly distributed, similarly sized objects. Profile with your actual motion patterns before deciding.

How do I diagnose narrowphase bottlenecks?

Measure pairs entering narrowphase, manifold counts, and solver iteration spikes. Inspect contact normals for jitter and check how often convex proxies fall back to mesh triangles. Reducing false positives upstream often saves more time than micro-optimizing shapes.

Why does stacking jitter even with accurate contacts?

Jitter often comes from inconsistent contact caching, excessive restitution at low speeds, or friction directions drifting frame to frame. Stabilize by merging near-duplicate contacts, clamping low-speed bounce, and using persistent manifolds where supported.

Looking ahead

  • Profile with scene-specific motion patterns before locking broadphase choices.
  • Budget continuous checks narrowly and prefer convex proxies by default.
  • Track pairs, manifolds, and solver iterations to catch regressions early.
