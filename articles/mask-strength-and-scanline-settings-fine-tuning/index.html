Mask Strength and Scanline Settings: Fine Tuning

By Logan Pierce • Published January 27, 2026

Our goal is to fine tune mask strength and scanline settings so CRT shaders look believable without crushing brightness or adding moiré. You need an emulator that supports multi pass shaders, a display in SDR at a known gamma, and time to test at 240p and 480p. We assume RetroArch or similar with shader presets and on screen display. The method favors small, measured adjustments over guesswork, using consistent patterns and a reversible workflow. Expect tradeoffs between luminance, detail, and stability, especially at modern 4K and 1440p pixel structures.

Key takeaways

  • Set mask strength by display brightness headroom and visible moiré risk.
  • Match scanline thickness to source resolution and viewing distance.
  • Validate at 240p and 480i to expose pattern stability differences.
  • Use small increments and A B compares to avoid over tuning.
  • Prefer presets that separate mask and scanline parameters for control.

Plan the work

Start by defining targets for mask strength, scanline depth, and brightness so adjustments track toward a goal rather than a vibe. A practical target is keeping mid grays around their baseline luminance while adding a visible subpixel mask at close inspection distance. For a 4K panel, that often means a modest mask weight that does not exceed the display’s brightness headroom. The tradeoff is that heavier masks darken the image and can trigger moiré against your panel’s subpixel layout. Setting a clear success metric, like legible HUD text without shimmer during scrolling, gives you a reliable check that guides later choices.

Establish references to judge progress, because relative comparisons beat memory. Use a static 240p grid pattern, a 1 pixel checkerboard, and a gameplay scene with slow panning backgrounds. These reveal moiré and mask stability fast. If your emulator supports per core overrides, duplicate a neutral preset so you can branch safely. The risk with ad hoc edits is losing the baseline, which makes regressions hard to catch. Snapshotting the original preset and noting current mask and scanline values in a changelog protects your calibration loop and keeps reversibility straightforward when experiments go sideways.

Decide which resolutions you must satisfy, since 240p and 480i behave differently with the same mask and scanline settings. For example, a mask tuned at 4K for 240p may look too coarse with 480p, where line pairing interacts with scanline weighting. A good rule is to tune for the dominant content first, then test the secondary mode for acceptable compromise. If you care about multiple systems, consider separate presets. The cost is slightly more management, but it avoids one size fits none results. Planning this split early reduces later confusion when a fine looking 240p profile misbehaves on interlaced menus.

Prepare environment

Stabilize the display before touching mask or scanlines, because wrong system settings will hide the effect or exaggerate artifacts. Disable any TV sharpness enhancements and variable contrast. Set SDR mode with a known gamma, commonly 2.2, and cap peak brightness to a level you can sustain for long sessions. The reason is simple: heavy masks cost luminance, so you need predictable headroom. On OLED, watch for near black crush that disguises scanline weighting. On LCD, ensure local dimming is not oscillating with high frequency patterns. These controls keep your shader output faithfully mapped to the panel.

Lock emulator output resolution and scaling behavior, since mask pitch and scanline cadence depend on the final render size. For a 4K display, integer scaling for 240p content minimizes resampling artifacts that interfere with mask perception. If you cannot achieve clean integers, prioritize consistent scaling rather than maximum fill. The tradeoff is small letterboxing that pays back with stability. When choosing the GPU backend, match what your shaders expect. Slang shaders prefer Vulkan in many stacks, while GLSL aligns with OpenGL. If unsure, keep a single backend through tuning to prevent confounding changes in sampling precision.

Create a controlled preset workflow inside your emulator, with override layers that separate system, core, and content. This lets you experiment without touching global behavior. In RetroArch, for instance, you can save a copy of your shader preset and adjust only a few parameters. Keep changes small, typically in steps of 0.02 to 0.05 for weights, then A B compare. Execute steps

Work in a repeatable loop that isolates mask strength first, then scanlines, because mask changes alter perceived brightness and can hide line weighting. Begin from a neutral preset with mask disabled and scanline weight minimal, then adjust mask until you see a clear but stable phosphor structure at close viewing distance. Only after the mask is set should you deepen scanlines to restore perceived contrast in flat areas. This ordering controls luminance drift and preserves midtone integrity. As you iterate, move between a static 1 pixel grid and gameplay. Static patterns expose moiré and uneven cadence, while motion reveals shimmer and temporal instability. If mask strength above roughly 0.45 starts striping on diagonals, step back and confirm your scaling mode is integer where possible. If scanlines above 0.28 crush small fonts, lower weight and consider a gamma trim rather than chasing black level in the TV. The guiding principle is to let the mask create texture and the scanline pass restore contrast, not the other way around. Separate roles keep fine detail intact while maintaining stable midtones.

Validate results

Validation hinges on stability and brightness rather than vibe, so check a few simple criteria. First, with a 240p grid, ensure vertical and horizontal lines do not exhibit traveling interference when you slowly pan. Second, measure mid gray with a pattern if you have a meter, or use a consistent photo exposure as a proxy. If your mask strength dropped average picture level too far, compensate with a small gamma adjust in the shader rather than TV contrast. The reason is to keep the TV in a linear, predictable state while you fine tune within the rendering pipeline where changes are reversible and scoped.

Next, test 480i or 480p content to catch interactions between line pairing and scanline weighting. Interlaced material often exhibits flicker in thin HUD elements when scanline depth is too aggressive. A practical rule is to target slightly lighter scanlines for higher resolutions or interlaced sources, since increased line density already reduces perceived brightness. If switching between 240p and 480p, maintain separate presets or use per content overrides. This avoids the pitfall where a great 240p profile appears muddy on menus rendered at higher resolution. Cross checking prevents overfitting to a single scenario.

Finally, compare different backends only after locking parameters, because backend changes modify sampling precision and rounding. If a Vulkan preset looks subtly different from an OpenGL run, confirm that both share identical scaling and that texture filtering is nearest for the content stage. Differences that survive those checks suggest true shader path variance. At that point, choose the variant with fewer artifacts rather than the one that appears brightest. Perceived brightness can be misleading when masks interact with panel subpixels. Favor the output that keeps geometry stable and text legible under slow scrolls and diagonal motion.

Troubleshoot and optimize

If moiré appears at normal seating distance, reduce mask strength and test a different mask pattern rather than only lowering scanline depth. Some masks use triad layouts that interact poorly with your panel’s subpixels. Switching to a slot or grille variant can stabilize the image without losing intended texture. Another lever is slight defocus or a micro blur pass before the mask. The tradeoff is a small hit to razor sharp edges, but a gain in temporal stability during camera pans. Adjust sparingly and always recheck the grid pattern to confirm that shimmer has decreased.

When small fonts look hollow or broken, your scanline weight may be too high relative to the mask. Lower scanline depth a few increments, then restore perceived contrast with gamma rather than mask. This preserves the mask texture while rescuing thin glyphs. If interlaced flicker persists, consider a lighter scanline preset for 480i sources that also reduces mask strength slightly. You can then keep a stronger combination for 240p games. Organizing these presets with clear names and content coverage prevents confusion and shortens decision time when switching between systems or cores.

Performance constraints sometimes force compromise, since heavier shader stacks cost GPU cycles. If frame pacing becomes uneven, profile individual passes and disable optional effects like bezel reflections before changing mask or scanline fundamentals. Many shaders expose quality tiers that retain core behavior with fewer samples. Choose the lowest tier that preserves stable geometry, then revisit mask strength to recover lost presence. Quick start checklist

  1. Normalize display mode: set SDR, disable sharpening, and pick a stable gamma.
  2. Lock scaling behavior: prefer integer scaling for 240p and fixed output resolution.
  3. Clone the preset: create a safe copy and note current parameter values.
  4. Sweep mask strength: step 0.25 to 0.45 and stop before moiré appears.
  5. Deepen scanlines: raise weight until contrast returns without crushing text.
  6. Validate across sources: test 240p and 480i with grids and slow scrolls.

Frequently asked questions

Why does increasing mask strength reduce overall brightness?

Masks simulate unlit phosphor gaps which block subpixels and lower average picture level. Stronger masks create more dark area, so you need either gamma compensation in the shader or a modest brightness increase to maintain mid gray.

How do I avoid moiré on a 1440p monitor?

Use integer scaling where possible and back off mask strength until shimmer disappears at normal viewing distance. Try a different mask pattern with a cadence that lands beyond the panel’s subpixel structure, or add a very slight pre blur before the mask pass.

Should scanline depth be the same for 240p and 480i?

Usually not. Interlaced and higher resolution sources already reduce perceived brightness and can flicker with heavy scanlines. Use a lighter scanline weight for 480i or 480p, then validate with text legibility and slow camera pans.

Do I tune brightness on the TV or inside the shader?

Prefer small gamma or brightness trims in the shader to keep the TV in a linear, predictable state. TV level changes can engage processing or alter tone mapping which complicates comparisons and makes small differences harder to evaluate.

What backend should I choose for Slang based presets?

Slang presets typically target Vulkan. Pick one backend for the entire tuning session to avoid precision changes while you calibrate. After parameters lock, you can compare Vulkan and OpenGL and keep the variant with fewer visible artifacts.

Looking ahead

Carry your tuned mask strength and scanline settings into a labeled preset family that matches systems and resolutions you play most. Revisit them after a few sessions to confirm comfort and stability at real viewing distances, then document final values and reasoning. This practice tightens future adjustments and gives you a repeatable baseline when hardware or backends change.
