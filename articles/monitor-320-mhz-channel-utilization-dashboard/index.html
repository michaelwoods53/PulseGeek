Monitor 320 MHz Channel Utilization Dashboard

By Rachel Morgan • Published February 19, 2026

You will monitor 320 MHz channel utilization by building a focused dashboard that separates airtime from throughput and latency. This guide assumes access to controller telemetry or AP stats and a workspace like Grafana, plus basic API or SNMP familiarity. The outcome is a repeatable process that produces stable panels, minimal alert noise, and quick drill paths. We emphasize utilization and dashboard reliability over one time tests, using simple baselines and clear labels so teams can compare sites and time ranges confidently. If your platform supports multi link operation and puncturing, the approach still holds, because the calculations rely on observed airtime share, retries, and noise floor rather than vendor specific interpretations.

Key takeaways

  • Separate airtime utilization from throughput to avoid misleading interpretations.
  • Baseline idle periods first, then tighten thresholds with history.
  • Tag panels by band, width, and puncturing to keep comparisons honest.
  • Alert on sustained utilization windows, not single spiky samples.
  • Validate dashboard math with spot tests and packet timings.

Plan the work

Start by defining utilization as airtime share rather than raw throughput, because 320 MHz links can appear fast while consuming little channel time. A practical rule is to track busy time percentage, retries, and noise floor together for context. For example, an AP reporting 35 percent busy with stable retries usually indicates healthy scheduling, whereas 35 percent with rising retries hints at contention or interference. Treat client mix and multi link operation as variables that change airtime patterns across radios. The core decision is to pick metrics that reflect medium access, not just data volume, which makes the dashboard resilient to rate shifts and modulation changes over distance and obstacles.

Next, scope the dashboard to distinct panels that answer different questions without overlap. One panel should present channel busy percentage for 6 GHz at 320 MHz width, another should visualize latency distribution, and a third should cover retries and MCS spread. A compact table listing AP, BSSID, and width clarifies where wide channels are enabled. This separation avoids conflating speed with health and keeps operators from chasing throughput dips that do not affect voice or control traffic. Decide on time ranges that match operations, such as 1 hour for incident response and 24 hours for planning, so your comparisons remain consistent.

Choose data sources with care, because controller counters and AP local stats can diverge by sampling windows. Prefer server side metrics with documented definitions and align collection intervals, such as 15 seconds for real time pages and 60 seconds for trends. Where platform specifics are unclear, annotate panel legends with definitions like busy time or medium utilization to prevent misreads. When considering platform depth, an overview of wide channel behavior helps. Prepare environment

Verify that access points and controllers expose 6 GHz counters for busy time, noise, retries, and channel width state. Some platforms aggregate counters across radios, which hides 320 MHz details, so confirm per radio visibility before proceeding. Document authentication for APIs and test a single request in a terminal to confirm you can reach utilization endpoints. If your fleet mixes vendors, standardize metric names as labels during ingestion to normalize dashboards. This prevents queries from breaking when you add new AP models. When possible, tag data with site, RF profile, and puncturing flags, which allows filtered comparisons when interference or policy forces narrower segments.

Establish storage and visualization layers that can support short and long time windows without downsampling the very spikes you want to catch. A time series database like Prometheus or an analytics engine offered by your controller can both work if scraping intervals and retention match your goals. For example, collecting every 15 seconds with 30 days retention captures busy hour signatures while keeping costs tractable. Choose a visualization tool that can annotate events such as DFS moves and firmware upgrades. Those notes help explain step changes that are not related to client behavior and avoid false optimizations.

Draft alert criteria before you build panels so thresholds align with how you operate. A helpful structure is two tiers: a warning for sustained busy time above a chosen level, and a critical when retry rate and latency rise together. Sustained means a window like 5 minutes to avoid reacting to a single burst. Borrow the ideas that fit your environment, then keep definitions consistent in your own labels.

Validate results

Prove the numbers by running simple load tests while watching panels. The quickest check is to generate a predictable downstream flow and confirm busy percentage rises while retries remain moderate. If busy surges but throughput stays flat, investigate puncturing or client modulation constraints. Conversely, if retries spike during moderate busy time, look for co channel interference or a misaligned radio profile. Record before and after snapshots over the same window to build a baseline library. This habit helps future analysts recognize normal peaks and prevents aggressive threshold tuning that would hide useful signals.

Correlate utilization with latency, since airtime alone does not capture queueing. Track p95 latency during busy windows and verify alert sensitivity aligns with user experience reports. A practical pattern is to alert when busy and p95 latency exceed chosen levels for several minutes. If your platform supports test clients, schedule periodic pings or small TCP transfers to generate consistent timings. These probes are not perfect, but they validate directionally that your panels reflect reality. If measurements diverge, audit sampling intervals and confirm that panel units and axes match the definitions you documented earlier.

Troubleshoot and optimize

When dashboards feel noisy, first confirm whether spikes are true or sampling artifacts. Compare controller counters against AP local views for a short window to detect aggregation lag. If differences persist, reduce scrape interval temporarily and check time alignment. Next, look for DFS events that force channel changes and create gaps or step changes in busy time. Annotating those shifts keeps incident reviews grounded in RF realities. If you operate in mixed widths due to puncturing, ensure filters show only 320 MHz radios in panels intended for wide channels. Blended views often hide the cause of alerts, leading to over tuning and missed problems.

Optimize thresholds iteratively by analyzing weekly distributions instead of chasing daily outliers. Export busy time and latency percentiles to a notebook and compute typical ranges for your sites. This supports a tiered alerting approach where quiet sites can use tighter thresholds and dense sites use wider bands. Revisit labeling as you add multi link operation, since traffic may spread across links and change per radio counters. If your controller offers AI hints, treat them as suggestions and reconcile them with your baselines. Focus on reducing false positives first, then refine sensitivity in steps rather than large jumps.

Investigate sustained utilization with root cause notes that attach to timeline bookmarks. For instance, correlate retry surges with a new neighbor deployment or a power change on your APs. Packet captures can clarify contention, but use them sparingly and target short windows during suspected issues to avoid heavy analysis. For practical capture approaches on wide channels, you can use techniques described in guides that discuss methods for capturing 6 GHz frames and filters. Keep remediation lightweight at first, such as adjusting transmit power or disabling problematic channel reuse patterns, then measure impact with the same panels to verify improvement.

Frequently asked questions

Why does busy time show high while throughput looks normal?

Busy time measures airtime use, not data moved. Normal throughput with high busy suggests contention, small frames, or management traffic. Check retries and latency to decide if users are impacted before tuning thresholds.

How long should the sustained window be for alerts?

Pick a window that filters transients while catching user impact. Five minutes is a practical start. Shorten for real time incident response or lengthen for scheduled jobs that temporarily spike airtime without complaints.

What if my controller lacks a 320 MHz specific counter?

Use per radio busy time and include width as a label from configuration. If width is unavailable, add a static tag from inventory. Clarify definitions in panel legends so viewers understand whether values reflect wide channels.

Do I need packet captures to validate utilization?

No, start with synthetic pings or small TCP flows and correlate retries and latency with busy time changes. Use captures only when symptoms persist or counters conflict. Limit captures to short windows to avoid heavy analysis.
