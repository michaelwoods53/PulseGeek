Remote Play Over Internet with Tailscale or ZeroTier

By Jacob Reed • Published January 10, 2026

Goal: stream from a gaming PC to Steam Deck across the internet using Tailscale or ZeroTier without exposing ports. Assumptions: one Windows or Linux host with hardware video encode support and a Steam Deck on current SteamOS. Prerequisites: admin rights on the host, ability to install clients on both ends, and a stable network where you can test packet loss and latency. The path pairs a mesh VPN with a streaming app like Moonlight, Sunshine, or Steam Remote Play. We will plan the network, prepare devices, execute the setup, validate with pings and bitrates, and then tune. Remote play over internet benefits from mesh routing and relays when NAT is strict, but overhead can add a few milliseconds and requires careful codec settings.

Key takeaways

  • Tailscale or ZeroTier provides private addressing that avoids risky port exposure.
  • Validate with ping, bitrate caps, and resolution before testing real gameplay.
  • Prefer hardware encoders and consistent frame pacing to limit perceived latency.
  • Use relays as a fallback when symmetric NAT blocks direct peer connectivity.
  • Start with H.264, then try HEVC or AV1 only if both ends support it.

Plan the work

Start by choosing a mesh approach that matches constraints on internet connectivity and control. Tailscale uses WireGuard-based tunnels and can route over direct peering or relays when NAT is strict, while ZeroTier builds a virtual Ethernet overlay with intelligent path selection. If you need simple client installs and easy device sharing, Tailscale is often faster to deploy. If you want granular virtual network control, ZeroTier’s network rules can help. On both options, the streaming layer remains separate, such as Sunshine with Moonlight or Steam Remote Play. As an example, a laptop on hotel Wi Fi often benefits from Tailscale relays to traverse symmetric NAT, though relay adds a small latency penalty. Define early what acceptable round trip time and stutter levels are so compromises are explicit.

Define success criteria in measurable terms to avoid endless tweaking without feedback. A practical baseline is idle ping under 60 ms between Deck and host, sustained stream bitrate that does not force drops below 70 percent of configured value, and less than 1 percent packet loss during gameplay bursts. These are guardrails rather than hard laws and will vary with genre expectations. A turn based game can tolerate higher latency than a shooter that demands quick inputs. Consider time of day effects or Wi Fi interference in apartments, since both can change performance without changing your setup. Write down thresholds so you can decide when to stop tuning and start playing, which keeps changes purposeful.

Pick a streaming stack that aligns with your GPU’s encoder options and the Deck’s decoder. Many NVIDIA cards handle H.264 and HEVC well, while AV1 encode is newer and only present on certain GPUs. On the Deck side, H.264 decode is broadly compatible and robust under fluctuating bandwidth. Sunshine plus Moonlight gives fine control over bitrate and codec, while Steam Remote Play is tightly integrated and usually simpler. Moonlight will not work without an NVIDIA GPU on the host, so avoid that path if you run AMD only. Prepare environment

Install the mesh client on both the host PC and Steam Deck, then confirm they see each other with stable private IPs. On the Deck, the easiest path is installing Tailscale or ZeroTier from Discover in Desktop Mode, which persists across reboots. On Windows or Linux hosts, use vendor installers to ensure service daemons start automatically so the PC is reachable when unattended. After installation, log in and note each device’s private address from the app UI or CLI for later use. As an example, a Tailscale address often looks like 100.x.y.z and is unique to your account. Record these addresses and keep the clients running on startup. If you need SSH for remote fixes, enable it intentionally and verify it is locked to the private network only.

Before you configure the streaming layer, verify basic reachability and path stability. Use continuous ping from the Deck to the host’s mesh address for at least one minute to detect intermittent spikes that indicate Wi Fi congestion. If average latency is steady but jitter is high, consider moving the Deck closer to the router or using 5 GHz with a channel that avoids neighboring networks. If ping fails completely under certain conditions, your path may be traversing a relay or blocked by a firewall rule. In that case, allow the mesh client through device firewalls and retry until the Deck and PC can communicate continuously. Eliminating basic connectivity issues early saves time later when tuning codecs.

Once reachability is stable, prepare Sunshine or Steam Remote Play on the host and pair the Deck. Sunshine exposes the host to Moonlight clients and uses NVIDIA’s NVENC or AMD’s AMF for encoding when supported. Steam Remote Play is simpler to start and is often installed already with Steam. Add one test title with a consistent frame rate pattern, such as a simple 3D scene or a fixed benchmark mode, so measurements are comparable across changes. While you can jump directly into a demanding game, controlled testing helps isolate blame when stutter appears. Keep drivers up to date and ensure the GPU’s power state will not downclock aggressively during encoding, which can cause sudden bitrate drops without obvious warnings.

Execute steps

With the mesh online and the streaming app installed, apply configuration changes in a deterministic order. First, confirm both devices show as connected in Tailscale or ZeroTier, then lock the Deck to a 5 GHz network with a known channel to reduce interference. Next, set Sunshine or Steam Remote Play to H.264 at a conservative bitrate like 15 to 25 Mbps at 60 FPS to establish a stable baseline. Later, you can raise bitrate or switch to HEVC if both ends support it. Finally, ensure the host’s power plan prevents aggressive sleep states and the network adapter is not allowed to power down. This sequence limits variables during testing and avoids conflating improved Wi Fi with codec changes that would hide the cause of gains.

Apply resolution and frame rate choices that match internet variability and Deck decoding limits. A 1280x800 stream at 60 FPS is a practical starting point that reduces strain while preserving responsiveness. If you want 1080p, try 45 to 60 FPS with bitrate ceilings that avoid starving the encoder during quick motion. An example approach is stepping from 12 Mbps to 18 Mbps while watching a real time network graph to catch sudden congestion. Higher resolutions load encoders more and raise the cost of packet loss, so scale conservatively. Latency sensitive titles often feel better with consistent frame pacing at 60 FPS and a lower bitrate than with fluctuating 90 FPS targets that cannot hold under internet jitter.

Set security and access to prevent surprises when away from home. In Tailscale, disable key expiry only when you have safe account controls with multifactor authentication. In ZeroTier, approve members explicitly and restrict the network to private routes only. Do not forward ports on your router for streaming apps when a mesh VPN gives direct reachability. That avoids exposing services to the open internet and reduces attack surface. If you need Wake-on-LAN for the host, test it on the LAN first and then consider a relay method or a supported mesh feature to issue magic packets. Always confirm remote reboots bring services back automatically, because an unresponsive encoder cannot be fixed without physical access.

Validate results

Validate the path in layers so you can pinpoint blame. Start with ping from the Deck to the host’s Tailscale or ZeroTier address and watch min, avg, and max values for two minutes. Spikes above 30 ms over average hint at contention. Then test a small file transfer over the mesh to confirm throughput matches expectations and does not drop erratically. Finally, run the streaming app and watch its statistics overlay or log. If Sunshine reports encoder wait times rising during motion, your bitrate is too high for conditions. If Steam Remote Play shows network-limited flags frequently, reduce resolution or FPS. The goal is consistent delivery rather than chasing peak numbers that collapse under real movement.

Use visual signal checks to spot decoder pressure or missed frames on the Deck. Stair stepping artifacts and macroblocking during fast scenes often point to insufficient bitrate headroom or Wi Fi collisions. If black frames appear during scene cuts, test whether the mesh path is using a relay by checking client status. Relay usage is not inherently bad, but it adds hops that can magnify jitter. Consider moving closer to the router or switching channels before increasing bitrate. Many people raise bitrate to hide artifacts when the real cause is unstable wireless. A structured approach fixes the right layer first, which often yields smoother input latency than any single codec change would achieve.

Compare your outcome to a clean local baseline to isolate internet-specific penalties. If you see large gaps relative to LAN performance, revisit the network path and encoding tradeoffs. A focused guide to end-to-end LAN remote play setup for codecs and bitrate tuning can reveal whether the internet path or host workload is the main limiter. If LAN tests are perfect but WAN stutters, the bottleneck is outside the PC and likely in Wi Fi or relay distance. If both are poor, the host encoder or game settings might be the culprit. Use this differential to avoid chasing the wrong fix when only one layer changed.

Troubleshoot and optimize

If streams disconnect or refuse to start, validate identity and routing before touching codecs. In Tailscale, expired keys or ACL changes can silently prevent traffic; reauthenticate and confirm device approvals. In ZeroTier, ensure the member is authorized and the network controller shows a direct path, not blocked. Firewalls that block the mesh service often cause intermittent failures that look like app bugs. Temporarily disable third party firewalls to test, then add precise allow rules rather than leaving them off. When stuck behind symmetric NAT with no direct path, accept relay routing and lower temporal settings to reduce sensitivity. Starting with 45 FPS instead of 60 can stabilize delivery when hop count increases.

When video tears or exhibits heavy artifacting, examine encoder load and bandwidth ceilings. On GPUs without AV1 encode, HEVC may save bits over H.264, but both ends must support it. If decode on the Deck struggles, step down resolution before touching framerate to lower per frame complexity. Another tactic is enabling VBR with a reasonable maximum so bursts can push higher without permanent increases. Be cautious with overly aggressive B frame counts that raise latency. Aim for predictability over absolute clarity. People often chase higher bitrates and create congestion that then tanks frame pacing. Conservative targets yield better input feel across variable internet conditions and leave headroom for sudden motion.

If input delay is the main complaint, optimize the shortest path and remove buffering you do not need. Prefer wired Ethernet for the host, disable background downloads, and set the router’s QoS to prioritize the Deck’s MAC or IP. Some routers misbehave with smart queueing defaults that cap throughput too low for video plus inputs; test with QoS off and then add just enough shaping to prevent bufferbloat. Monitor packet loss during heavy scenes and pause overlays or monitoring tools that steal GPU time. Input latency is often a sum of small waits across layers, so removing even a few milliseconds per layer can transform feel without any one dramatic change. Always retest with the same scene to keep results comparable.

Key terms

  • Access Control List — Rules that permit or deny traffic based on criteria.
  • Frame Rate Cap — A set limit on frames per second to control performance and power.
  • Frames Per Second — The number of images shown each second in a game.
  • Internet Service Provider — A company that provides internet access to customers.
  • Network Address Translation — The process of mapping private addresses to public ones.
  • Network Latency — The time it takes for data to travel between your device and the game server.
  • Quality of Service — Network rules that prioritize or shape traffic to manage latency.
  • Steam Remote Play — Valve’s system for streaming PC games to other devices over a network.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to open router ports when using Tailscale or ZeroTier?

No. Both tools create private tunnels and handle NAT traversal automatically. Avoid manual port forwarding for streaming apps when a mesh VPN provides reachability. This reduces exposure and simplifies maintenance across different networks.

Why is latency higher over the internet than on my LAN?

Wide area paths add distance, hops, and potentially relays when NAT blocks direct connections. Wireless contention and ISP shaping also introduce jitter. Aim for stable delivery with conservative bitrate and frame pacing rather than pure peak throughput.

Should I use HEVC or AV1 for remote play to Steam Deck?

Start with H.264 for broad compatibility and predictable latency. Try HEVC only if both host encode and Deck decode are reliable. Use AV1 only when both ends support hardware encode and decode, and your path can handle the added complexity.

What bitrate should I start with for 60 FPS streaming?

Begin near 15 to 25 Mbps at 60 FPS and 1280x800 or 1080p. Increase slowly while watching for dropped frames and encoder wait. If network jitter rises, reduce bitrate or frame rate to maintain consistent delivery across variable conditions.

How can I tell if my connection is using a relay?

Check the mesh client status on both devices for peer path details. If it shows a relay or fallback path, you will often see slightly higher and steadier latency. Direct paths usually have lower round trip times and more variable jitter from Wi Fi.

Looking ahead

Once the baseline works reliably, you can refine without risking stability. Consider trying HEVC for scenes with fine detail if both ends support it, or AV1 when hardware encode and decode are confirmed. Explore per game profiles that adjust resolution and bitrate for genre needs so shooters prioritize latency while slower titles enjoy sharper image quality. If you travel often, document a portable Wi Fi strategy that avoids crowded channels and brings a small Ethernet adapter for the host. Finally, schedule periodic checks for client updates and review mesh access lists quarterly to keep security tight. Incremental, documented changes preserve a smooth remote play experience instead of starting from scratch each time conditions change.
