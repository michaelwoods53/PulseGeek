Audio Desync in Cutscenes on Miyoo: Sync It Back

By Logan Ward • Published January 3, 2026

Cutscenes reveal sync issues fast, because audio and video cues are tightly choreographed on Miyoo hardware. If you hear audio leading or lagging dialogue, the root is often buffering, time-stretch, or VSync interactions rather than a single bad setting. This guide targets audio desync in cutscenes with a practical sequence that starts safe, then narrows causes without breaking performance. We assume RetroArch on Miyoo Mini or Mini Plus with stock OS or Onion-based builds and typical cores for PS1, GBA, SNES, and Genesis. You will change a few options, verify with repeatable tests, and only then proceed to deeper adjustments if needed.

Key takeaways

  • Stabilize audio by matching VSync, audio sync, and buffering first.
  • Use time-stretch only when frame rate slightly misses real time.
  • Validate with repeated cutscene cues, not general gameplay moments.
  • Avoid frameskip for story scenes because it breaks A/V alignment.
  • Apply overrides per core to isolate one set of changes at a time.

Quick start checklist

  1. Enable audio sync: turn on sync to exact content rate in RetroArch.
  2. Match VSync to display: enable VSync and disable hard GPU sync on Miyoo.
  3. Set safe buffer: use a moderate audio buffer and avoid extreme latency cuts.
  4. Disable frameskip: keep consistent frame pacing during story and FMVs.
  5. Turn on time-stretch: use slight stretch only for near-real-time cores.
  6. Test a known scene: replay the same cutscene cue to confirm alignment.

Plan the work

Start by framing the symptom clearly, because “audio desync in cutscenes” can stem from timing, buffering, or display mismatch and each requires a different approach. Pick one reproducible scene such as an opening FMV or a dialogue sequence with lip flaps and repeated sound effects. Note whether audio leads or lags and whether drift grows over time or appears as sudden jumps. If drift increases steadily, look at mismatched rates and time-stretch. If it jumps, suspect buffering or frameskip. Plan to make changes per core, not globally, so you measure impact without collateral side effects on other systems.

Define the environment to control variables. Miyoo models differ in CPU limits and audio drivers, so write down the device variant, firmware or Onion version, and the RetroArch core in use. For PlayStation titles, PCSX ReARMed has different timing behavior compared to Beetle PSX, and their settings should not be mixed in testing. Record the display refresh configuration if accessible, and whether shaders or overlays are active. Shaders can add a small GPU cost that nudges frame pacing, which then ripples into audio. You will adjust components in order of impact and risk, starting with sync flags before touching advanced latency cuts.

Establish your verification method before changing anything. Choose one cutscene and identify three fixed cues like a door slam, a title card flash, and a spoken syllable that aligns to a mouth shape. Replay that segment at least three times and note whether audio consistently leads or trails those visuals by a similar offset. A stable, repeatable offset suggests a rate mismatch that time-stretch or VSync alignment can correct. A varying offset points to unstable frame delivery or buffer underruns that require different fixes. This baseline keeps the process honest and prevents over-tuning from anecdotal impressions.

Prepare environment

Create a stable baseline before tuning, or every change will look noisy. Disable frameskip in RetroArch, because skipping frames may keep gameplay fluid but it breaks audio and video alignment in dialogue and FMV scenes. Set video VSync to on and leave hard GPU sync off on Miyoo to avoid extra timing cost. Restore any extreme latency shortcuts to moderate values, since overly aggressive reductions often cause crackles and drift. Ensure the device has sufficient battery and normal temperature, as throttling can alter timing under load. With a known baseline, each subsequent adjustment can be measured against your fixed cutscene cues confidently.

Confirm the audio driver and rate are consistent. Most Miyoo builds run an ALSA-based driver under RetroArch with a standard sample rate such as 44100 Hz. Keep the audio rate at the core’s expected default to avoid resampling drift during long scenes. Set the audio buffer to a moderate range, large enough to absorb tiny delivery hiccups but not so large that it masks pacing issues. If you previously minimized the buffer to reduce delay in action games, increase it slightly for testing. This reduces false positives where temporary underruns resemble desync but are actually buffer starvation artifacts.

Disable nonessential overlays and heavy shaders for the initial pass. Visual filters that add multiple passes can push frame time over the budget, leading to micro stutter that compounds in voiced scenes. If you prefer a specific scanline or smoothing effect, reintroduce it later only after achieving stable sync in the bare configuration. Likewise, avoid mixing global and core overrides yet. Keep changes at the core level during preparation, so that one system’s demands do not pollute conditions for another. A clean, simple rendering path reveals whether timing misalignment is genuinely a rate issue or a performance bottleneck.

Execute steps

Make targeted changes in a controlled sequence so you can attribute improvements correctly. First, enable audio sync within RetroArch to match playback to content timing, which prevents gradual drift in most story scenes. Second, ensure video VSync is active to align frame delivery to the display refresh and avoid jitter that pushes audio out of step. Third, use time-stretch only if your core misses full speed by a small margin, because it can cushion tiny deficits without obvious pitch artifacts. Testing after each change narrows the cause while keeping the system responsive and stable.

Map each action to a predictable result. Turning on audio sync should immediately reduce steady, linear drift that accumulates as the cutscene plays. Enabling VSync should smooth frame pacing and prevent brief bursts of frames that make lines sound early or late relative to mouth shapes. When applying time-stretch, keep adjustments conservative, as large values can sound processed and may still fail if performance dips are severe. If a single change improves two cues but harms a third, roll back and consider the interaction between buffering and VSync rather than stacking more toggles without evidence.

Validate results

Verification separates perception from measurement. Rewatch your chosen cutscene and use the same three cues you documented, such as a mouth closure, a flash frame, and a sound effect like a door. If the offset is now consistently within a small window that you cannot detect without focusing, the settings are acceptable. If drift remains but is smaller, you can keep the successful changes and iterate on time-stretch or buffer size in small steps. If alignment worsened or fluctuates more, revert the last tweak and consider that component a non-solution for your device and core combination.

Use slow motion or frame advance only as a tie breaker. Human perception of sync is tolerant within a narrow band, and chasing perfect frame parity can introduce other issues like added latency or stutter. The goal is stable, perceptually matched audio and video across the full cutscene. Also validate several games that share the same core but only change one variable at a time. If one title’s converted FMV is oddly encoded, it may behave differently from in-engine dialogue, and that is useful to know before generalizing your settings as a new default.

Document final settings so you can reproduce success quickly. Save a per-core override once satisfied, and include notes on the exact cutscene used for testing. This makes it easy to investigate regressions after updates or when enabling a shader later. Troubleshoot and optimize

If audio still drifts, classify the failure mode carefully. A constant lead or lag that stays uniform points to rate mismatch. Increase or decrease time-stretch slightly and retest. A growing offset that resets after scene cuts hints at intermittent performance dips or buffering. In that case, increase audio latency a notch and ensure threaded video remains on to smooth delivery. Disable any dynamic frameskip options in cores like PCSX ReARMed. Frameskip may keep gameplay smooth but will break dialogue synchronization by dropping the very frames that align voice to lip motion during stories.

Frequently asked questions

Should I use frameskip to keep cutscenes smooth?

No. Frameskip drops visual frames that dialogue sync relies on. It can hide slowdowns but will break alignment between lip movement and spoken lines during story scenes and FMVs.

Will time-stretch change pitch in dialogue?

With small adjustments, time-stretch maintains pitch while correcting minor speed deficits. Large values can sound processed and may still fail if performance drops are significant.

Why does only one game drift while others are fine?

Some titles use unusual FMV frame rates or heavier decoders. Create a per-game override with a slightly larger buffer or modest time-stretch while keeping other games on standard values.

Do shaders affect audio and video sync?

Yes. Heavy shaders increase render time and can disturb frame pacing. Disable them while diagnosing and reintroduce only after achieving stable alignment in your test cutscene.

How do I tell if the problem is tearing instead of desync?

Look for horizontal breaks or uneven motion without a steady audio lead or lag. If visuals split or stutter while audio is steady, prioritize fixing VSync and refresh alignment.
