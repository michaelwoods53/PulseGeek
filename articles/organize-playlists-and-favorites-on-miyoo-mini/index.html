Organize Playlists and Favorites on Miyoo Mini

By Jeremy Crawford • Published February 26, 2026

Organize playlists and favorites on Miyoo Mini by using simple structures that survive resets and SD swaps. This guide prioritizes reversible steps, clear naming, and repeatable routines, so your favorites stay predictable across updates. We chose methods that work with stock firmware or popular community builds without requiring risky tools. You will see how to star top games, group seasonal picks, and keep multi-disc titles neat while avoiding duplicate entries. Where helpful, we link to formatting and error guides to keep your library reliable over time.

Key takeaways

  • Favor simple folder groups and stable names for portable playlists.
  • Use favorites sparingly and re-evaluate lists every few weeks.
  • Keep multi-disc games unified with one entry and clear labels.
  • Seasonal or theme folders reduce scrolling and decision friction.
  • Back up lists before firmware or SD changes to preserve favorites.

1. Curate a core favorites list

A lean favorites list works better than an overflowing one because it acts as a fast launchpad. Start by starring only 10 to 20 evergreen titles that you return to every month, like a portable version of a rotation shelf. For example, keep a short list spanning genres and consoles, such as a platformer, a racer, a puzzler, and an RPG you replay often. This compact set shortens scrolling and keeps your choices visible on small screens. The tradeoff is that you must un-star items as your tastes shift. Without a cleanup rhythm, the favorites list can become noisy and lose its speed advantage, so put reminders in your weekly routine to prune entries.

Set a simple rule to control growth, such as one in one out when you star a new game. If a title sits unplayed for two weeks, remove the star and return it to its regular folder. A short favorites list doubles as a quick reliability check because frequently launched entries surface bad dumps or missing BIOS earlier in your cycle. If a favorite fails to boot, fix it before it spreads confusion. 2. Create theme folders for playlists

When the frontend lacks advanced list features, theme folders act like portable playlists that survive across firmware updates. Make top level categories such as Short Sessions, Long Plays, or Weekend Co-op, then place copies or shortcuts of ROMs inside. As an example, put pick-up-and-play arcade titles in Short Sessions and a single JRPG in Long Plays. This approach mirrors how many users organize music into curated sets. The benefit is resilience because folders work regardless of frontend changes. The tradeoff is duplication, which can consume space if you copy ROMs. To reduce footprint, keep theme folders small and rely on symbolic naming rather than cloning entire libraries.

Label theme folders with a prefix so they group together, such as 00-Short-Sessions or 01-Long-Plays, which ensures predictable sorting on most frontends. Inside the folder, add a small README text file that lists the curation rule, for example 30 minute sessions only, so you remember why each game belongs. If you later migrate to a larger card, you can move theme folders intact without breaking your structure. 3. Manage multi-disc games as single entries

Combining multi-disc games into one entry prevents cluttered menus and confusion during play. The simplest pattern is a parent folder named for the game with disc files tagged clearly, for example Final Fantasy IX Disc 1, Disc 2, and so on. When the emulator supports a list file like M3U, use one M3U per title so the launcher shows a single selectable game while enabling disc swaps in the pause menu. This reduces duplicate entries across playlists and favorites. The tradeoff comes from varying emulator support and naming sensitivity. If your frontend cannot read M3U, stick to the folder pattern and keep a text note describing the correct disc order near the ROMs.

Good naming is the safety net. Use short alphanumeric names with a consistent separator like hyphens, and include region tags only if meaningful to your setup. For example, FFIX-Disc-1-USA is readable and remains stable across tools. Avoid special symbols that may cause parsing problems on some devices. If a multi-disc favorite breaks, it often traces back to inconsistent filenames or a missing BIOS file. To avoid that pitfall, verify your emulator expects specific BIOS locations and ensure they match your layout before adding the title to favorites. 4. Use recency and session length playlists

Playlists based on session length and recency reduce decision fatigue when time is tight. Build two small lists: 15-Minute Plays and Ongoing Stories. Move arcade shooters and puzzlers into the short list and keep narrative-heavy games in the long list. Pair that with the frontend’s Recently Played view to rotate fresh items in and stale ones out. For example, after three recent launches, upgrade a game to favorites or move it to a theme folder. The tradeoff is churn. You must maintain these lists weekly, or they will drift from your actual habits. Still, the payoff is faster launches and a greater chance of finishing long games.

Define a migration rule so the lists self-regulate. For instance, when a title appears in Recently Played three times in seven days, promote it to Ongoing Stories, and demote the least played entry. Use a short naming prefix like 02-15m and 03-Long to keep sorting predictable and keep both folders near the top. This rotation mirrors how personal kanban boards maintain focus by limiting work in progress. 5. Apply safe ROM naming for stable sorting

Safe ROM naming keeps favorites and playlists stable across tools and updates. Use a consistent format like Series GameName Region Year, avoiding special characters that might break sorting or scraping. For example, MegaMan2 USA 1988 sorts correctly while remaining readable. Good names reduce the chance that your frontend misinterprets entries, which helps playlists retain membership after re-indexing. The tradeoff is a small upfront investment to rename files. Automating with a batch rename tool mitigates the effort, but you should still spot check a sample before bulk updates to avoid accidental collisions or lost metadata caused by aggressive patterns.

Adopt a minimal character set and stick to hyphens or spaces for separators. If you must add tags, keep them short and ordered, such as [RevA] or [T-En], and place them at the end. This approach helps checksum tools and keeps filenames within common filesystem limits. Maintain a mapping file that lists any exceptions, like Japanese titles you prefer to keep in native script. Clear structure makes it easier to rebuild playlists after a rescan.

6. Star in-progress games and rotate weekly

Starring only active playthroughs creates a focused queue that you can clear. Limit the in-progress set to four or fewer starred items. For example, keep a platformer, a racing title, a quick puzzle, and one long-form RPG. Any new game must replace a completed or abandoned one. This constraint prevents stalled backlogs from flooding favorites and speeds resume sessions. The tradeoff is resisting the urge to star new finds. To manage that, keep a Not Yet list in a theme folder. That holding area captures curiosity without contaminating your active favorites. Revisit it weekly and swap one item in if a slot opens.

Use save management to support the rotation. Back up saves before un-starring long projects to avoid accidental loss or confusion during reorganizations. If you are unsure where your handheld stores saves, review a resource that explains save locations and backups so you can move or copy files safely. Periodic backups also help when switching SD cards or testing firmware updates. If an update scrambles shortcuts, you can restore the starred set quickly using your notes and the Not Yet list. This lightweight routine lowers friction and keeps your most played titles within two taps from the launcher.

7. Build seasonal or event collections

Seasonal collections keep browsing fresh and make curation enjoyable. Create quarterly sets like Spring Arcade Picks, Summer Co-op Nights, or Autumn RPGs. Move a small number of games into each, aiming for variety and a clear theme. For instance, gather five couch-friendly titles for a weekend meetup and remove them when the event is over. This method keeps your main library clean while offering a ready-to-play stack for specific moods. The tradeoff is occasional duplication. To avoid confusion, put a simple index text file in each seasonal folder listing the chosen games and their original locations, so you can re-sync or rebuild quickly after a cleanup.

Schedule a five minute review at the start of each season to swap entries and retire stale picks. If a game turns into a long play, promote it to your core favorites and document the move in the index file. Seasonal lists also help surface broken assets early because you launch them during focused sessions. If you find errors, consult a concise troubleshooting guide that outlines proven steps for common SD problems and fix them before the next event. This approach keeps your favorite sets dependable and reduces the odds of last minute frustration during gatherings.

8. Backup and recover your curated lists

Backups protect playlists and favorites from accidental loss during SD changes or firmware updates. The simplest approach is to export configuration files, copy themed folders, and save a small text log of starred items. Keep the backup on a computer or a second SD card stored separately. As a real example, before trying a system update, copy your theme folders and notes, then take an image of the card so you can restore everything if the index changes. The tradeoff is extra storage and a few added steps. The benefit is a fast, confident rollback that saves hours of manual re-curation after an unexpected reset.

Create a predictable backup cadence tied to your maintenance routine, such as after every eight hours of play or after major folder edits. Verify the integrity of important ROMs with checksums before you snapshot the card, so you do not preserve corrupt files. Frequently asked questions

Do I need special tools to make playlists on Miyoo Mini?

No. Simple folders, favorites, and readable filenames work well. Some emulators support list files for multi-disc games, but the folder approach remains portable across updates and different firmware variants.

How many favorites should I keep at once?

Keep about 10 to 20 at most. That range balances speed with variety. Use one in one out to prevent growth and prune weekly so the list remains a quick launchpad rather than a second library.

What is the safest way to handle multi-disc games?

Group discs under one game folder with clear names, or use a single list file when supported. This keeps one entry in menus and reduces confusion during disc swaps or rescans.

Will renaming ROMs break saves?

It can if your emulator links saves to filenames. Back up saves, test renames on a few titles, and confirm save detection before bulk changes to avoid accidental loss or duplicate files.

How often should I back up curated lists?

Back up after meaningful edits or roughly every eight hours of play. Snapshot before firmware updates or SD migrations to preserve folder structure, notes, and starred items.
