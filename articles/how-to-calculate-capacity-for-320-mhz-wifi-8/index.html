How to Calculate Capacity for 320 MHz WiFi 8

By Rachel Morgan • Published March 4, 2026

Goal oriented capacity work begins by translating a 320 MHz WiFi 8 channel’s theoretical throughput into realistic service levels. We assume a modern 6 GHz deployment with standard WPA3 and typical enterprise clients. You will calculate capacity using measured conditions, then validate and iterate. The plan references key WiFi 8 concepts like MCS selection and multi link behavior without over relying on lab perfect numbers. Bring admin access to your WLAN tools, a spectrum view if available, and at least one capable WiFi 7 or WiFi 8 client for testing. By steadily mapping PHY rate to usable throughput and airtime, you can justify design choices and detect bottlenecks before users feel them.

Key takeaways

  • Model capacity from inputs first, then confirm with measured throughput.
  • Use airtime efficiency to bridge PHY rate and delivered goodput.
  • Account for client mix, MCS spread, and power asymmetry.
  • Validate with both single stream peak tests and multi user loads.
  • Recalculate after changes to channel width, RU use, or MLO.

Plan the work

Start by declaring a precise capacity target that ties to user experience rather than a single headline PHY rate. For example, aim for 400 Mbps sustained per premium client at P95 latency under 30 ms with twenty active devices in range. This frames success and prevents overestimating what a 320 MHz channel can deliver when management frames, retries, and control overhead appear. Write down constraints like regulatory power, channel plan, and spatial layout. The tradeoff is that tighter targets reduce wiggle room for interference or client diversity, but they produce clearer go or no go decisions and more actionable tuning later.

Next, list the inputs you can measure or bound, since those determine whether the 320 MHz width helps. Capture noise floor at the AP, typical RSSI at key seats, client radio tiers, and observed MCS distribution during busy times. If you lack history, gather a one hour trace to estimate baseline airtime use and retry rate. Wide channels magnify interference sensitivity, so a noisier floor or hidden nodes can erase expected gains. Writing assumptions forces you to model best case and likely case, then communicate risk when results depend on fragile conditions.

Finally, choose a modeling granularity that aligns with your tooling and tolerance for error. A per client approach multiplies estimated goodput by active devices and factors in spatial reuse. A simpler aggregate model treats the channel as a shared pipe with an efficiency factor applied to the PHY ceiling. The detailed route offers better forecasting for mixed device capabilities, though it takes more data. The aggregate route is faster for a first pass, but it can hide bottlenecks like low rate clients consuming disproportionate airtime. Decide once, document it, and keep the same method through validation.

  • Document assumptions about noise, client tiers, and expected airtime load.

Prepare environment

Preparation begins with verifying that a clean 320 MHz option actually exists in your regulatory domain and building layout. Confirm channel groupings, permissible power, and any automated frequency control. If your environment regularly sees adjacent occupancy, the gains from 320 MHz may require preamble puncturing or a fallback to 160 MHz in certain areas. Capture a spectrum snapshot during peak hours to check for persistent interferers that would depress MCS or force retries. The limitation here is that short captures can miss bursts, so complement a quick scan with WLAN controller counters that aggregate across time.

With spectrum confirmed, assemble test clients that represent your production tiers, including at least one multi antenna device and one legacy handset. Record each device’s maximum spatial streams and supported MCS. Then validate that your access point firmware exposes counters like MCS distributions, airtime fairness, and contention time. This instrumentation lets you translate laboratory PHY rates into MAC layer throughput with real framing overhead. A tradeoff exists between capturing exhaustive metrics and staying practical, so prioritize signal quality, MCS hit rate, and retries. These few indicators explain most of the swing between expected and delivered capacity.

To anchor expectations, compare channel width choices and guard intervals using a compact table. This helps you communicate why 320 MHz is not universally better than narrower lanes. The table highlights two knobs you will tune during validation. Remember that shorter guard intervals are sensitive to delay spread, so test where walls and reflections are complex. In echo heavy rooms, a longer interval may sustain higher MCS and therefore higher net throughput than raw symbol speed suggests.

Execute steps

Execution converts your inputs into a defensible capacity estimate through a repeatable checklist. Start by selecting a representative MCS for strong clients at target RSSI, then apply a realistic MAC efficiency factor to bridge the gap between PHY and goodput. Many teams use a range rather than a single value to reflect scheduling and aggregation behavior. Next, estimate airtime share per client during the busy hour by dividing offered load by per client goodput, which reveals whether the channel saturates. The advantage of this flow is transparency about where assumptions drive outcomes, though it depends on the quality of your MCS and efficiency picks.

  1. Set service targets: define throughput, latency, and concurrency expectations for key user groups.
  2. Choose modeling scope: decide per client or aggregate method based on available metrics.
  3. Select representative MCS: derive likely MCS from RSSI, SNR, and observed distributions.
  4. Apply efficiency factor: convert PHY rate to expected goodput under realistic overhead.
  5. Estimate airtime use: divide offered load by goodput to compute airtime share per device.
  6. Sum capacity: aggregate across active clients and compare to the channel’s airtime budget.
  7. Stress with variance: rerun with lower MCS and higher retries to bracket worst case.

For teams that prefer a quick calculator, a short script can enforce consistent math. The snippet multiplies a chosen PHY rate by an efficiency factor, then projects multi user demand against an airtime budget. It does not guess protocol constants, so you avoid false precision. Use controller counters to feed the variables, then rerun with optimistic and pessimistic inputs. The result is a capacity number expressed in Mbps and a utilization estimate. If utilization exceeds 70 to 80 percent during the busy window, plan for contention spikes and either reduce cell size or right size channel width.

As you compute, add references for design choices that influence efficiency. For wide channels, puncturing can preserve usable bandwidth when a sub channel is busy. If most clients are dual band and support multi link operation, you may reduce burstiness and smooth airtime even when 320 MHz experiences partial occupancy. Validate results

Validation compares your estimate to measured throughput and airtime during controlled tests. Begin with a near ideal single client test at strong RSSI to check your PHY to MAC efficiency assumption. Use a wired endpoint to remove LAN constraints and run at least three trials for repeatability. If measured goodput falls far below your modeled range, investigate aggregation, RTS thresholds, and retries. The limitation is that a pristine single client result can hide fairness and scheduler issues, so treat it as a ceiling validation rather than proof of multi user behavior.

Follow with a multi client, mixed tier test that reflects your busy hour concurrency and offered loads. Track the distribution of MCS values and airtime shares per client. If a few low rate devices dominate airtime, recalculate capacity with a heavier tail in the MCS distribution. This explains why a high ceiling on a 320 MHz channel can still under deliver. When results diverge, change one variable at a time and annotate. The goal is to understand which assumption shifts fix the gap rather than blindly tweaking knobs.

Finally, inspect stability by running a fifteen minute soak with realistic application patterns like short bursts and pauses. Watch for transient spikes in contention time or queue depth that point to burst synchronization issues. If multi link operation is in use, check whether flows pin to a single link during interference and whether that matches your model. These references keep the validation grounded and help you decide if a narrower channel would actually improve delivered experience.

Troubleshoot and optimize

Troubleshooting starts with mapping shortfalls to either signal quality, contention, or device limits. If MCS collapses at normal seating positions, tune transmit power and placement to reduce cell size rather than adding power indiscriminately. Consider moving from 320 MHz to 160 MHz where the noise floor is stubbornly high, then retest efficiency. The tradeoff is a lower ceiling but potentially better average goodput when retries and interference drop. Always change one variable at a time so you can attribute gains to the right lever and keep your capacity model honest.

When contention dominates, focus on airtime fairness and scheduler settings that influence aggregation, RTS behavior, and OFDMA trigger density. If your platform supports preamble puncturing, enable it to salvage usable spectrum slices during partial occupancy. Review retry rates and tweak thresholds conservatively, then rerun mixed client tests. Where multi link operation is available, ensure it is stable under preferred band conditions instead of flapping. These optimizations aim to raise efficiency rather than chase peak PHY numbers that do not translate to user throughput.

Finally, formalize a recalculation cadence tied to facility changes and firmware updates. Re run the model after adding high density spaces, changing channel plans, or enabling new features. Keep a short changelog in your design document so future estimates inherit validated efficiency factors. Stable documentation lowers the time to accurate answers and reduces debate about whose numbers are right.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my 320 MHz test show lower throughput than expected?

Measured throughput reflects MAC layer efficiency, retries, and scheduling. Wide channels are sensitive to interference and may deliver less than PHY suggests if noise is high or low rate clients consume airtime.

How should I pick a PHY to MAC efficiency factor?

Derive it from controlled single client tests at strong RSSI. Use a range for planning, then tighten the number after mixed client validation that includes real aggregation and retry behavior.

When is 160 MHz a better choice than 320 MHz?

Choose 160 MHz when the noise floor is elevated or partial occupancy is frequent. Narrower bandwidth reduces interference sensitivity and can raise delivered goodput through fewer retries.

Do I need multi link operation to reach my targets?

No, but MLO can stabilize throughput by spreading traffic across links. Treat it as a smoothing tool and still validate capacity on a single wide channel path.

How long should validation tests run?

Use short bursts for ceiling checks, then a fifteen minute soak for stability. Longer runs reveal contention spikes, queue buildup, and scheduler edges not visible in quick tests.

Looking ahead

The most durable capacity plans evolve with your measurements and design history. Keep your estimator, efficiency factors, and validation scripts in version control so each site inherits proven defaults. As client mixes shift and features like preamble puncturing or scheduler updates land, rerun the model and adjust the airtime thresholds that trigger redesign. If future surveys show narrow windows of clean spectrum, prepare a dual width plan that uses 320 MHz in quiet zones and 160 MHz elsewhere. This cadence keeps your WiFi 8 service reliable while making room for growth without guesswork.
