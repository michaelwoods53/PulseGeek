Capacity Planning Calculator for 320 MHz

By Rachel Morgan • Published February 3, 2026

Capacity planning for 320 MHz in WiFi 8 needs a calculator that turns realistic inputs into airtime and throughput estimates. This guide shows how to structure planning, verify assumptions, and refine outputs so the model maps to field behavior. We assume enterprise WLANs using 6 GHz with managed controllers, standard QoS, and mixed clients. The method works whether you script the calculator or build a spreadsheet, and it keeps Planning, Calculator logic, and 320 MHz considerations traceable. You will define required service levels, turn PHY data rates into MAC throughput, and model concurrency. Finally, you will validate against measurements and adjust. The focus stays on predictable outcomes rather than perfect precision, because production WiFi varies with traffic patterns, client capabilities, and coexistence features like puncturing and multi-link operation.

Key takeaways

  • Define service targets, client mix, and concurrency before any capacity math.
  • Convert PHY rates to MAC throughput using realistic efficiency ranges and overheads.
  • Model airtime per flow with QoS, retries, and duty cycle constraints.
  • Size AP counts by required Mbps per user and busy hour concurrency.
  • Validate estimates with surveys, counters, and packet captures before rollout.

Plan the work

Start by stating service targets, because capacity math is meaningless without a throughput and latency goal per user. Define minimum Mbps per active client, tolerable one way delay, and packet loss budgets that match your applications. For example, a collaboration space might need 5 to 8 Mbps during calls with sub 50 ms latency, while a lab might need higher dataset bursts but tolerate jitter. Capture busy hour concurrency as a percent of total clients, often 10 to 35 percent depending on site type. The tradeoff is overprovisioning cost versus degraded experience during peaks. Write down non negotiables like roaming coverage and voice quality, since they drive airtime headroom. This clarity tells your calculator what success must look like before numbers start moving.

List environmental constraints that bound 320 MHz use, since spectrum availability and power rules change the math. Identify regulatory domain, 6 GHz indoor or outdoor status, and whether Automated Frequency Coordination applies, because these affect EIRP and channel access. Note building materials that attenuate 6 GHz more strongly than 5 GHz, such as low E glass or dense concrete. Decide if 320 MHz will be fixed or dynamically punctured to avoid incumbents or local interferers. The limitation here is simple models can ignore walls or AFC behavior, so your calculator must let you derate throughput for edge rooms or outdoor links. Document these constraints up front so later adjustments feel principled, not ad hoc.

Inventory the client mix and capabilities since they dominate airtime demand. Count how many devices support 320 MHz, spatial streams, and typical MCS levels under real RSSI. A rule of thumb is to group clients into performance tiers like high, medium, and legacy, each with its own average PHY rate and retry profile. Include multi link operation if both APs and clients support it, but treat it as an optional gain rather than guaranteed. The tradeoff is complexity in modeling per tier versus accuracy. A three tier approach keeps the calculator manageable while capturing most variability. Recording these distributions lets the model weight throughput by actual populations instead of assuming all clients are equal.

Prepare environment

Choose your calculator medium and data sources so the workflow stays repeatable. A spreadsheet is fast for what-if analysis, while a short script improves reuse and version control. Whichever you select, plan separate tabs or modules for inputs, derived rates, and outputs. Pull PHY rate tables from chipset documentation where possible, or create a simplified mapping by RSSI bands to typical MCS. The tradeoff is precision versus maintenance burden, so bias toward a compact table you can defend. Decide how you will store client distributions and concurrency assumptions to avoid accidental changes. This structure prevents logic from mixing with inputs and keeps audits simple when stakeholders ask how the numbers arose.

Define conversion from PHY to MAC throughput using explicit efficiency factors, because raw data rates overstate real capacity. Include preamble, interframe spacing, contention, QoS scheduling, and block ACK overheads. A pragmatic range for MAC efficiency can be 35 to 70 percent depending on retries and aggregation. Use separate factors for downlink and uplink, since contention and queueing differ. Add a derate for management and control traffic, typically a small but persistent slice of airtime. The limitation is that efficiency varies with environment and client behavior, so keep these factors editable and visible. This turns an argument about numbers into a discussion about assumptions you can test.

Map channels and features that influence 320 MHz behavior, like puncturing patterns and primary channel placement. Decide whether you will model a single 320 MHz lane or allow dynamic bandwidth that narrows under interference. If spectrum is constrained, represent time spent at 160 or 80 MHz as a duty cycle variable so the calculator averages realistic operation. This context guides which features your environment supports and which your model should soften.

Execute steps

Implement the calculator in a sequence that locks down assumptions before arithmetic. Start with inputs for client tiers, concurrency, PHY rates, and efficiency factors, then compute weighted MAC throughput per tier. Next, derive airtime per flow given average frame size and QoS class to account for scheduling and contention. Finally, allocate total airtime across active clients and convert to Mbps delivered per user. The main tradeoff is modeling complexity versus usability, so build a tidy set of inputs that can be tuned without editing formulas. If your environment uses RRM or policy features that shape airtime, factor them in as optional toggles. For example, request number of APs in the area, expected busy hour clients, and the percent of time the AP can use 320 MHz versus falling back to 160 or 80 MHz due to puncturing or interference. Ask for retry rates per tier to calculate effective MAC efficiency rather than relying on a static number. The tradeoff is making the sheet feel longer, but it prevents hidden defaults from biasing estimates. When the inputs are explicit, you can compare sites and scenarios without guessing which parameter changed between versions.

Validate results

Validate by comparing calculator outputs against measurements so assumptions converge toward reality. Start with controller or AP counters for retries, MCS distributions, and airtime utilization during the busy hour. If your model assumed 55 percent MAC efficiency but the site shows heavy retries, adjust downward and rerun. Use small packet captures to verify aggregation, QoS markings, and block ACK behavior for high demand clients. The tradeoff is measurement noise during transient peaks, so gather multiple samples and prefer medians. A consistent gap in either direction indicates a factor that needs tuning, such as duty cycle at full 320 MHz or management overhead in dense areas.

Run micro surveys in representative locations to capture path loss and real RSSI bands that drive MCS. A quick walk test with a supported client and passive survey tool reveals which rooms sit at lower MCS than expected. Translate these observations into tier share changes inside the calculator. For instance, if the high tier shrinks in edge rooms, lower its population share or PHY rate accordingly. This adjustment mirrors the actual distribution of client experiences. The limitation is that single device surveys might not represent the full client fleet, so repeat with a second device class when possible to avoid confirmation bias.

Exercise QoS paths with targeted traffic to validate scheduling and airtime reservations. Generate a voice call, a video stream, and a bulk transfer to see whether prioritization operates as modeled. Track per user throughput and latency while noting total airtime usage. If voice flows do not maintain priority under load, your calculator should allocate more airtime to high priority queues or lower throughput expectations for best effort. The why is simple: capacity planning must reflect scheduler behavior, not just physical rate potential. Close the loop by logging the final factors so future sites can benefit from the refined template.

Troubleshoot and optimize

When estimates overshoot delivered throughput, check inputs that commonly skew optimistic. Revisit duty cycle at full 320 MHz, since puncturing or interference can force narrower operation more often than planned. Inspect retry rates by tier and lower MAC efficiency if collisions rise, especially with mixed legacy clients. Evaluate primary channel placement so contention does not cluster on a busy segment. If gaps persist, consider enabling airtime fairness and tuning contention windows to protect latency sensitive flows from large file transfers. The tradeoff is that strict fairness can starve bulk traffic, so match settings to business priority rather than aiming for equal slices.

If user counts fall short only during peaks, shape demand or redistribute load rather than adding hardware immediately. Rate limit best effort guests, schedule background updates outside the busy hour, or offload non critical devices to 80 or 160 MHz SSIDs if policy permits. Slightly reducing minimum rates on the widest channel can encourage clients to roam to better cells, improving MCS and lowering retries. The why here ties to airtime as the scarce resource. By lowering contention and retries, effective capacity rises without changing the physical bandwidth. Apply changes one at a time and observe counters to isolate their impact.

For sustained optimization, align your calculator with controller features that enforce the model. Automate channel changes carefully to avoid churn and keep wide lanes viable, and set QoS profiles that mirror your tier priorities. Periodically export counters to compare with previous weeks so you can detect drift as client fleets change. Quick start checklist

  1. Declare service targets: set Mbps per user, latency, and loss budgets.
  2. Group client tiers: define PHY rates, efficiency, and shares for each tier.
  3. Model MAC efficiency: apply editable factors for overhead and retries.
  4. Set duty cycle: estimate percent time at 320 MHz versus narrower widths.
  5. Compute delivered Mbps: aggregate tier throughput and subtract management airtime.
  6. Estimate users: divide delivered Mbps by target per user rate.
  7. Validate and tune: compare with counters and revise assumptions as needed.
