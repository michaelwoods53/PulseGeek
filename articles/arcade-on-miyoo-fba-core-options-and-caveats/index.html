Arcade on Miyoo: FBA Core Options and Caveats

By Jared Butler • Published January 17, 2026

Arcade performance on Miyoo depends on how FinalBurn Neo interprets hardware, handles timing, and reconciles ROMset quirks. Players see big differences when toggling options like audio sync, frameskip, and runahead, yet each choice carries tradeoffs. This guide explains practical FBA settings, what they mean for latency and stability, and where Miyoo’s limits nudge you toward alternate cores. We focus on repeatable decision paths rather than one size fits all answers, with examples drawn from CPS2, Neo Geo, and older boards often favored on handhelds. If you understand why a setting exists, you can bend it to the game’s needs without breaking saves or introducing jitter that only appears after longer play sessions.

Key takeaways

  • Match your ROMset to the emulator database to avoid silent failures.
  • Prefer audio sync first, then adjust frame delay for latency balance.
  • Use frameskip sparingly when brief drops beat persistent microstutter.
  • Switch to MAME2003-Plus for outliers with timing or protection issues.
  • Test long sessions to catch thermal slowdowns and creeping jitter.

Concepts and definitions

FBA on Miyoo usually refers to FinalBurn Neo, a consolidated arcade core that emulates many boards with a focus on speed and accuracy within handheld limits. The core relies on specific ROMsets that align with its database, so mismatches often present as missing files or subtle glitches. A useful baseline is to keep audio sync enabled to anchor timing, then evaluate whether the video chain can keep pace without stutter. On these devices, stable frame pacing matters more than chasing the absolute lowest latency, because handheld screens magnify jitter. The why here is simple: small displays make microstutter more visible, and their refresh characteristics can amplify uneven pacing. Starting from a sync-first position yields predictability before you fine tune responsiveness.

Latency in FBA involves input processing, emulated frame construction, and display delivery. A common approach is to set modest frame delay, which holds the frame slightly longer to reduce lag, then confirm the device still meets full speed across your library. For quick examples, CPS1 titles usually tolerate conservative delay without drops, while some Neo Geo titles approach limits when sound processing spikes. The tradeoff is that pushing delay too far risks missed frames that appear as occasional hitching. Understanding the mechanism matters because lag is not one number but a stack of small intervals. Shifting any part of that stack affects perceived tightness, and on Miyoo the margin between crisp and choppy can be surprisingly narrow.

Frameskip exists as an escape valve when occasional load spikes threaten sustained speed. Enabling a low, non aggressive value can mask brief slowdowns during heavy scenes, but it also introduces temporal inconsistency. For shooters that demand steady scrolling or precise bullet timing, inconsistent cadence feels worse than a tiny bit of added latency. In contrast, some beat em ups survive a mild skip because impacts remain readable even if one frame is bypassed. That is why frameskip belongs late in your tuning order, after sync and delay are settled. You solve the larger pacing problem first, then decide whether isolated pressure points justify selective skipping or whether the side effects undermine the experience.

Verifying set compatibility avoids dead ends where a non matching set wastes time. With known good content, choose audio sync to keep clocks aligned, then increase frame delay in small increments while watching for sustained full speed. If drops appear, back off delay until pace is steady. Only enable frameskip if rare stutters persist during intense scenes. This framework works because it addresses root timing first, then refines responsiveness without masking underlying issues. For games with rapid inputs, prioritize stability over a few milliseconds of theoretical gain that the handheld may not consistently deliver.

Examples and short scenarios

A CPS2 fighter that feels slightly sluggish but steady under FBA likely benefits from small frame delay reductions rather than disabling sync. Try stepping down delay one tick, validate speed on busy stages, and confirm inputs still land predictably. If the game remains solid, you can experiment with one more step, but monitor for tiny hitches that show up after several rounds. The reason this resolves the issue is that fighters amplify input timing variance, and shaving modest delay can restore feel without dismantling frame pacing. If instead you disable sync, you may chase intermittent microstutter that appears only during complex scenes, which hurts muscle memory more than a marginal increase in tightness helps.

A Neo Geo action game that exhibits occasional audio crackle during explosions points to a load spike rather than a persistent timing mismatch. Keep audio sync on, reduce frame delay a notch, and consider a minimal frameskip only if explosions coincide with consistent hitching. Test a long session to surface heat related slowdowns, since handheld thermals can erode headroom over time. This approach works because reducing pressure on the pipeline removes the need to skip frames, preserving cadence where it matters most. If the problem persists and aligns with specific effects, try the same ROMset on MAME2003-Plus to see whether its timing model avoids the crackle without creating new issues.

A vertical shooter with intense scrolling that tears intermittently suggests the video path cannot maintain even pacing at chosen settings. Maintain sync, pull back delay, and disable any overlays that add compositing work. If stutter survives these changes, a tiny frameskip may be less harmful than visible tearing, but evaluate carefully with the hardest stages. The how here is to prioritize cadence consistency and strip optional overhead before modifying temporal behavior. Many shooters reward stable frame times more than razor thin input windows, so a conservative configuration yields the best aggregate result. If your library mixes older boards with heavier later releases, profile a few representative games and record per title overrides to avoid one size fits none presets.

Pitfalls, limitations, and edge cases

ROMset mismatches cause more pain than most option mistakes. A set built for a different revision may load but show broken features or silent stability problems hours later. Always verify audits against the core’s expected database and avoid mixing sources across libraries. Another frequent pitfall is overusing shaders or overlays that look great in screenshots but add enough overhead to trip performance under stress. The tradeoff is harsh on handhelds, where extra processing tightens margins that gameplay quickly consumes. Keeping visuals simple preserves headroom for moments when the emulated hardware surges, which is common in effects heavy titles and late stage boss sequences.

Input latency experiments sometimes confuse cause and effect by bundling unrelated changes. If you alter frame delay, turn off one overlay, and swap audio rates in a single test, you cannot attribute the outcome to any specific change. Use an ordered approach and log results with short notes per game, because Miyoo screens and speakers can highlight different artifacts than a desktop. An edge case appears with titles that rely on unusual protection or timing circuits. If the game resets unexpectedly or desyncs audio at fixed intervals, it may sit outside FBA’s smooth path on this device. In that case, switching cores is a cleaner remedy than forcing aggressive frameskip.

Save integrity becomes fragile when switching between cores without a plan. SRAM and state formats can differ, and casual experiments risk losing progress. Before swapping, back up saves and verify that another core reads the same SRAM layout for the title. The reason to prepare is that testing arcade options often involves multiple cores and variants, and a single incompatible state can undo hours of tuning and play. Protecting saves keeps experimentation low risk and reversible.

Frequently asked questions

Should I disable audio sync for lower latency on Miyoo?

Usually no. Audio sync anchors timing and prevents microstutter. Start with sync on, then reduce frame delay in small steps to balance responsiveness without breaking pacing.

When is frameskip acceptable with FBA on Miyoo?

Use it sparingly for rare load spikes that would otherwise cause visible hitches. Shooters and scrolling games often suffer more from frameskip than from a slight latency increase.

What if a game resets or has persistent audio crackle?

Confirm a correct ROMset first. If issues persist across clean tests, try an alternative core like MAME2003-Plus, which may handle timing or protection differently on the device.

Will switching cores risk my arcade saves?

Yes, potentially. Backup SRAM and states before switching. Different cores can use incompatible formats, and unplanned swaps may corrupt or orphan existing progress.

Do overlays or shaders hurt performance noticeably?

They can. Even light overlays add compositing work that reduces headroom. Disable extras while tuning, then reintroduce them only if performance remains stable under heavy scenes.
