Physics Timestep: Fixed vs Variable, Stability First

By Ethan Palmer • Published December 28, 2025

Choosing between a fixed timestep and a variable timestep defines how physics integrates with your render loop, input, and networking. When stability is the priority, many teams default to a fixed physics step, because consistent integration reduces solver noise and jitter. Yet variable stepping can feel more responsive on lightweight scenes by adapting to frame time and avoiding backlog. This comparison focuses on physics stability, responsiveness, and performance across common engines like Unity, Unreal, Godot, PhysX, and Box2D. We will frame tradeoffs with concrete decision rules and show a minimal loop for context. Along the way, you will see how synchronization, interpolation, and iteration counts shape outcomes, and why a stable budget often outperforms raw throughput over long play sessions.

Key takeaways

  • Use a fixed physics step when stability, determinism, and replay matter.
  • Choose variable stepping only with light scenes and tight latency needs.
  • Cap catch-up ticks to prevent spiral of death during frame stalls.
  • Interpolate render transforms to hide fixed-step motion quantization.
  • Tune solver iterations alongside timestep size for balanced stability.
  • Profile CPU budgets and jitter before adjusting timestep or iterations.

Evaluation criteria and how to weigh them

Start with stability as the primary criterion, because physics integration error scales predictably with timestep size. A fixed step bounds numerical drift and keeps forces consistent, which helps stacks, joints, and ragdolls remain believable. As a rule of thumb, choose a physics step between 1/120 and 1/50 seconds for real-time play, lowering the step when tall stacks or precise constraints appear. The tradeoff is CPU time and potential tick backlog when frames spike. Variable stepping can appear smoother under light loads but risks inconsistent collision resolution. Weight stability at 40 to 50 percent when gameplay depends on contacts, and reduce that weight only for pure arcade motion where collisions are forgiving.

Next weigh responsiveness, defined as perceived input-to-motion latency measured at the render frame. Variable stepping often maps inputs directly into motion at frame time, which can shave a few milliseconds of feel when scenes are simple. Fixed physics introduces quantization at the step boundary, so developers rely on interpolation to blend render poses between ticks. A practical heuristic is to prioritize responsiveness when movement is continuous and collision light, such as camera orbit or drone flight. The limitation is that input pacing can deviate under frame drops. Assign responsiveness 20 to 30 percent of your decision weight unless your core loop is twitch-sensitive and physics-light.

Finally evaluate performance and determinism as coupled criteria, because they reinforce or constrain each other. Fixed steps enable deterministic replays and lockstep networking by removing frame variability from integration, while variable steps require careful state capture to reproduce outcomes. On performance, fixed steps risk a spiral of death if the game attempts unlimited catch-up ticks after stalls. Mitigate this by capping accumulated time and optionally skipping non-critical simulation. Variable stepping saves work during trivial frames but can introduce solver instability when dt spikes beyond stable limits. Weight performance and determinism jointly at 30 to 40 percent if you support replays, authoritative servers, or physics-heavy AI.

  • Cap work during spikes and use interpolation if responsiveness matters.

Deep dives per attribute with tradeoffs and examples

Stability depends on integrator error, collision resolution consistency, and solver iteration counts. With a fixed step, the integrator receives a constant dt, which lowers variability in contact impulses and joint constraints. For example, a stack of boxes resting under gravity holds more reliably at 1/90 s with 6 to 10 solver iterations than at 1/30 s with the same iterations. The tradeoff is more CPU per simulated second. Variable dt can remain stable only if you clamp dt to a maximum that respects solver limits, but then fast frames provide no additional stability, only less work. Favor shrinking the fixed step slightly and budgeting iterations rather than hoping variable dt will average out errors.

Responsiveness is the felt time from input to visible motion. Fixed physics introduces a tick boundary that can add one step of delay unless you interpolate render transforms between the last and current physics state. For a character controller, you can process inputs each frame, accumulate them, and apply during the next physics tick while rendering an interpolated pose. Variable dt can feel snappier by integrating immediately with the current frame’s input, but dt spikes will produce inconsistent acceleration. If responsiveness is central and collisions are minor, variable dt with a tight clamp can work. Otherwise, interpolation on a fixed step delivers smooth visuals without risking solver inconsistency.

Determinism and networking rely on predictable state evolution. Fixed steps simplify replays by removing frame time variability, provided the same floating point mode and initial conditions are used. Lockstep or server authority benefits because every client or server tick consumes identical dt, keeping divergence bounded to input deltas. With variable dt, reproducing a bug can require recording every frame’s delta along with inputs, which is heavier and still fragile if hardware timing differs. For rollback networking, either approach can work, but a fixed step eases resimulation because you roll back a known number of ticks. Reserve variable stepping for arcade motion where exact reproduction is not required.

Fit by scenario with explicit recommendations

For physics-heavy gameplay with stacks, joints, or ragdolls, adopt a fixed step with interpolation. A stable default is 1/60 s and 6 to 10 solver iterations, lowering dt if stacks wobble or joints jitter. Cap catch-up ticks so a single stall does not cascade into frame drops, and drop non-essential simulation if the cap is reached. This fits cooperative or competitive titles that value consistency, replays, or authoritative servers. The caveat is extra plumbing to maintain two transforms per body for interpolation. When you must support deterministic tests or sync across clients, the fixed approach removes a major variability source and simplifies debugging of edge cases like tunneling and friction transitions.

For action titles where motion is mostly kinematic and collisions are sparse, a variable step with dt clamping can be acceptable. Keep dt below the solver’s stable threshold and avoid frame-dependent thresholds in gameplay code. This can reduce latency for camera turns or UI linked motions, especially on platforms where frame pacing is reliable. The limitation is susceptibility to outliers that skew acceleration or penetration. If you choose this route, layer safeguards such as velocity limiting and CCD on fast movers that might otherwise tunnel across thin colliders. Continually test under simulated stutters using frame debuggers to observe how dt spikes affect feel.

For networking strategies, align timestep to your sync model. Lockstep and deterministic replays favor fixed steps because every simulation tick is identical and easy to roll back or record. Rollback networking can tolerate variable steps, but resimulation quality improves with fixed ticks that map cleanly to rewound frames. Server authority with client prediction often runs a fixed simulation rate on the server, while clients render interpolated states. The tradeoff is periodic reconciliation stutters if you do not smooth corrections. Choose the simplest consistent timing model across client and server to prevent drift and reduce the surface area of desync bugs during long sessions.

Frequently asked questions

Should I always use a fixed physics timestep?

No. Use a fixed step when collisions, stacks, joints, replays, or deterministic networking matter. Variable stepping can work for kinematic motion with sparse collisions if you tightly clamp dt and test under simulated frame spikes.

What fixed timestep value is a good starting point?

Start around 1/60 second. If stacks wobble or constraints jitter, try 1/90 or 1/120 and profile CPU cost. If performance suffers, consider increasing solver iterations modestly before shrinking dt further to balance load and stability.

How do I prevent a spiral of death with fixed steps?

Clamp the maximum accumulated time and cap catch-up ticks per frame. If the cap is reached, skip non-critical systems or degrade effects, then let interpolation hide minor visual gaps while the simulation recovers to real time.

Does interpolation add extra latency?

Interpolation does not change simulation timing. It blends render poses between the last and current physics states to reduce visual quantization. Input still applies on the next physics tick. Keep interpolation math light to avoid frame latency.
