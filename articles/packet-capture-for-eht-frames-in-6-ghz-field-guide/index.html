Packet Capture for EHT Frames in 6 GHz

By Courtney Lawson • Published March 8, 2026

Capturing EHT frames in 6 GHz requires deliberate choices around packet capture, adapters, and filters so the data reflects reality. This field guide assumes a modern Linux laptop with driver support and a USB adapter that can monitor the new band. You will learn how to plan channels, enable monitor mode, select filters that show useful management and data frames, and validate decodes. The goal is a repeatable workflow that preserves timing and headers without adding interference or gaps. Following these steps reduces confusion when analyzing multi link behavior, channel utilization, or throughput problems. If your primary use case is benchmarking, plan to pair capture with structured tests so trace files and results align. For broader context on EHT features, see the authoritative primer with PHY and MAC updates via an EHT overview of core capabilities and migration patterns.

Key takeaways

  • Use 6 GHz capable adapters and drivers that expose monitor mode reliably.
  • Fix center frequency and width to match target EHT channel plans.
  • Capture with radiotap headers and avoid promiscuous band hopping.
  • Validate decodes by checking EHT fields and expected MCS ranges.
  • Record environment context to interpret packet capture timing correctly.

Plan the work

Start by scoping the packet capture so every later choice supports a single objective. Decide whether you need management beacons, association flows, or data frames under load, because each requires distinct channel width and timing. For example, capturing EAPOL handshakes benefits from targeting the AP’s primary channel and lowering noise from adjacent operation. If your aim is verifying EHT PPDU fields, you need a frequency plan that keeps the sniffer locked on the channel where those frames occur. The tradeoff is obvious: hopping across multiple channels increases coverage but introduces gaps and inaccurate timing. Prefer a single locked channel for deep analysis of EHT behavior, then run separate sessions for other bands or widths so files remain focused and interpretable.

Define success criteria that you can verify in the trace without guesswork. A useful rule of thumb is to list three observable outcomes, like beacons showing EHT Operation elements, association responses carrying correct capabilities, and data PPDUs decoding with expected MCS ranges. Tie each outcome to a verification method, such as a display filter or a statistics view in your analyzer. This pre-commitment helps you reject partial captures that lack decisive evidence. The limitation is that some edge cases, like multi link operation transitions, may require longer windows or multiple angles. Address this by planning a control capture on a quiet channel to establish a baseline before recording complex behavior.

Choose a session schedule that reduces confounders and aligns with real traffic. Schedule baseline beacons early when the environment is quiet, then run controlled load tests during periods that reflect production. If testing roaming or throughput, coordinate with a throughput methodology that defines traffic generators, metrics, and controls. You can draw a template for these steps from repeatable EHT throughput methods emphasizing valid results. The tradeoff is time: staging multiple runs lengthens the day but yields data you can compare confidently. Prioritize fewer high integrity sessions over many noisy runs. Document expected AP configuration versions so later readers understand the context behind observed frame contents.

Prepare environment

Select hardware that supports 6 GHz monitor mode and exposes radiotap headers. Many adapters that work at 5 GHz lack 6E monitor capability, so confirm driver notes and community reports before field use. A practical approach is to keep two known good USB radios with different chipsets, then prefer the one that maintains channel lock under load. Bring short USB cables to avoid case interference and allow optimal antenna placement. The tradeoff with external adapters is physical fragility, but the benefit is portability and easier swapping if a device misbehaves. Record firmware and driver versions in your notes because decoding anomalies sometimes trace back to subtle driver changes that alter reported metadata fields.

Stabilize the RF environment so captured frames reflect true behavior rather than scanning noise. Disable any background clients on your sniffer laptop that might generate traffic or kick the radio out of monitor mode. Position your antenna with clear line of sight to the target AP when possible and avoid stacking metal objects near the capture setup. If the site is busy, consider a low gain directional antenna for better capture of beacons and control frames. The tradeoff is narrower field, which can hide off-channel events, but it strengthens your view of the channel of interest. Take a quick noise floor reading with your analyzer to estimate interference before recording.

Before launching a full session, verify that your analyzer correctly parses EHT elements. Open a short warm up capture and check for EHT Operation and PHY header decoding in the tool’s packet details. If the fields appear undefined, you may need a newer build or updated dissectors. When using open source tools, ensure the capture includes radiotap metadata that indicates channel frequency, width, and MCS so the decoder can reason about EHT PPDUs. A limitation to note is that some stable releases lag behind draft changes. If your tool mislabels fields, prefer a vetted nightly build and save both the PCAP and tool version notes so others can reproduce your view.

Execute steps

Execution should favor a narrow, locked capture that matches your planned scenario. Begin on the target AP’s primary channel to ensure beacon and management frames are complete. If your objective is data path analysis under EHT, ensure traffic generators are active before starting the capture and keep background applications paused. A simple practice is to record two files: a short control run to confirm decoding, then the main session for analysis. The tradeoff to single channel focus is missing off-channel control frames, but your data will be continuous and timestamps trustworthy. If you must observe multiple widths, record separate runs for 20 and 80 or 160 MHz rather than changing width mid file, which can confuse decoders and invalidate comparisons.

Apply minimal capture filters so you do not throw away essential context during recording. At capture time, stick to broad 802.11 types, then use display filters later for surgical views. For example, keep management, control, and data frames so you can reconstruct association flows and correlate data bursts. If you filter out control frames at capture time, you may lose block acknowledgment or RTS details that inform throughput analysis. The tradeoff is larger files, but storage is cheap relative to the time cost of repeated field work. When space is tight, consider limiting duration or rotating files by time while still preserving complete frame types for later decoding.

When channel plan or interfaces change, annotate each run with environment notes to aid interpretation. Record the AP SSID, BSSID, primary channel, configured width, and any special features like multi link operation. These context details help you understand why certain EHT fields appear or why MCS ranges shift during a test. They also make it easier to compare against structured measurements of multi link performance that require controlled scenarios and safeguards, like the methods described in measuring multi link performance. The drawback is extra documentation effort, but it pays off when reviewing traces weeks later or sharing findings with peers who were not present on site.

Validate results

Validation begins by confirming the file contains complete frames with radiotap metadata. Open the trace and check that beacons include EHT Operation and related information elements, and that the radiotap header reports expected frequency and channel width. A quick sanity test is to compare the AP’s configured width with the decoded width in the header for beacons and data frames. If they mismatch, you likely tuned the sniffer incorrectly or the adapter could not maintain lock. The limitation is that some adapters report partial metadata under heavy load, so cross check using a shorter control capture recorded immediately before the main session to see if the behavior is repeatable.

Next, verify that the expected PHY features appear in data frames and that MCS and RU allocations align with your plan. Use your analyzer’s PHY layer panel to inspect EHT fields, then apply a display filter to isolate frames from the target BSSID. For example, filter on the BSSID and frame type to see whether block acknowledgments and high throughput data bursts align with your traffic generator patterns. If EHT decodes look inconsistent, confirm your tool’s version admits recent dissector updates and retry with the control capture. The tradeoff is time spent learning filters, but it produces evidence rather than intuition when documenting issues.

Finally, evaluate the capture for completeness and potential gaps by inspecting time sequences. Look for long intervals with no frames when the network should be active, which may indicate channel misalignment or buffer overruns. Use I/O graphs to visualize frame rates and identify steady states versus transitions. When discrepancies appear, compare with a throughput log or a latency benchmark to triangulate ground truth. For guidance on building latency tests that match capture insights, review benchmarks and relevant KPIs. The tradeoff is added correlation work, but it prevents misattributing problems to EHT when the cause is test harness instability or RF interference outside your control.

Troubleshoot and optimize

When captures lack expected EHT fields, start with capability mismatches. Confirm the adapter and driver expose 6 GHz monitor mode, then retune to the primary channel with a narrower width such as 20 MHz to stabilize decoding. Some drivers drop metadata at wider widths under load, producing incomplete radiotap information. A practical test is to record a one minute beacon scan on 20 MHz and compare decodes to your wider run. If the narrow run shows proper EHT Operation and the wide run does not, the limitation likely sits with the adapter. Swap to your alternate radio or update drivers. Maintain a log of which combinations reliably preserve PHY and MCS details.

If you see gaps or sequence resets, suspect buffer overruns or interference rather than protocol anomalies. Reduce capture bandwidth by narrowing width, shorten duration, or rotate files to ease write pressure on storage. Consider a faster storage device if the laptop disk is saturated during capture. Also verify that no background processes are scanning or using the adapter. The reason to prioritize system hygiene is that missed frames distort timing and can hide critical transitions in multi link behavior. If issues persist, move closer to the AP or use a directional antenna to increase received signal strength while controlling for multipath.

When throughput appears worse in traces than lab expectations, remember that field conditions differ and can dilute EHT advantages. Compare your findings with a perspective on lab versus real world performance to set expectations and identify narrowing tactics in a comparison of lab and field outcomes. If speed remains low, consult targeted diagnostics for slow performance recovery described in common causes and practical fixes. The tradeoff is time spent cross referencing, but it helps separate RF limitations from configuration or client behavior. Document every change so the final successful configuration can be repeated on the next engagement with confidence.

Quick start checklist

  1. Confirm adapter capability: verify 6 GHz monitor mode and radiotap support.
  2. Lock channel and width: set frequency and width to match the AP.
  3. Start a control capture: record 30 seconds and check EHT decodes.
  4. Run the main session: capture during traffic and annotate context.
  5. Validate metadata: confirm radiotap frequency and width are correct.
  6. Review timing graphs: check for gaps and correlate with test logs.

Frequently asked questions

Can I capture 6 GHz EHT frames with any Wi Fi adapter?

No. You need a 6 GHz capable adapter and driver that support monitor mode. Many legacy 5 GHz radios cannot lock 6E channels for passive capture. Verify capability with release notes or a short beacon capture before field work.

Should I use capture filters or display filters for EHT analysis?

Prefer minimal capture filters to avoid throwing away context. Keep management, control, and data frames during capture. Use display filters later to focus on beacons, association exchanges, or specific BSSIDs while preserving the raw evidence.

What channel width is best when decodes look unstable?

Start at 20 MHz on the AP’s primary channel to stabilize monitor lock and radiotap metadata. After confirming proper EHT elements in beacons and data PPDUs, try wider widths in separate runs and compare decodes for consistency.

How do I know if my capture missed frames?

Look for unexpected time gaps during active traffic or sequence number jumps that break continuity. Check I O graphs for dips and verify storage write speed. If gaps persist, reduce width, rotate files, or move closer to the AP.

Is passive capturing MLO behavior reliable on one channel?

You can observe parts of multi link behavior on a single primary channel, but you will miss traffic on secondary links. Use separate runs per link or coordinated captures from multiple sniffers when full visibility is required.

Looking ahead

The most reliable packet capture habits are small and repeatable. Keep a portable kit with two vetted 6 GHz adapters, a tested script for monitor mode, and a short validation checklist so you can confirm EHT decodes in minutes. As your use cases expand, add coordinated captures across links when investigating multi link behavior, and pair each session with a structured performance test so packets correlate with measurable outcomes. Rinse and refine by comparing field traces with baseline lab results to spot environmental factors at play. Finally, maintain versioned notes of drivers, analyzer builds, and capture settings so future sessions start from known good states rather than guesswork, reducing time to insight on every engagement.
