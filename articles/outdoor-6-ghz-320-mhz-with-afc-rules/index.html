Outdoor 6 GHz 320 MHz with AFC Rules

By Rachel Morgan • Published February 9, 2026

Outdoor 6 GHz design changes under AFC because power and channels are not entirely yours to choose. When you consider 320 MHz bandwidth, the authorization system decides which frequencies and maximum EIRP are safe. That gatekeeping protects incumbents like fixed service links while allowing standard power access points to operate. The practical question becomes how to align site layout, antenna patterns, and client mix with probable grants so your network performs reliably. This article focuses on the mechanics of AFC decisions, the suitability of 320 MHz outdoors, and the design tradeoffs that follow. Readers will gain a clear framework to plan coverage that respects rules and still reaches intended throughput goals.

Key takeaways

  • AFC grants constrain outdoor 6 GHz power, channels, and time windows.
  • Wide 320 MHz needs clean spectrum, precise siting, and directional antennas.
  • Throughput gains fade if grants force low EIRP or frequent puncturing.
  • Model likely grants before mounting hardware to avoid redesign churn.
  • Plan fallbacks to 160 or 80 MHz when AFC windows tighten.

Concepts and definitions

AFC, or Automated Frequency Coordination, is the authorization layer that permits standard power devices to use outdoor 6 GHz while avoiding licensed incumbents. The system evaluates your reported location, height, and antenna characteristics, then responds with permissible channels and power limits. For 320 MHz, that means the feasibility of a single ultra-wide channel depends on whether an uninterrupted block is available at sufficient EIRP. A rooftop with clear line of sight may still receive a tight cap if a nearby fixed link shares azimuth. Designers must understand that the grant is not static and can change with database updates. Planning around likely variability reduces operational surprises and supports sustained capacity when authorization windows shift.

Standard power differs from low-power indoor in both EIRP and control precedent, because AFC-mediated access protects primary users. The authorization outcome may include bandwidth restrictions or require channel puncturing to avoid narrow incumbents. With 320 MHz, puncturing can remove subchannels and reduce effective width, often steering you toward 160 or 80 MHz to maintain link budget. The inherent tradeoff is straightforward: the wider the channel, the more spectrum must be simultaneously clean and approved at usable power. When that alignment fails, coherence and modulation depth suffer. Recognizing that AFC shapes not only channels but also transmit power helps set conservative expectations for throughput in mixed urban environments.

Equipment behavior under AFC matters because controllers and access points must accurately report geolocation and antenna details. Height above ground, antenna pattern, and downtilt influence off-axis energy that could reach incumbents. Even with compliant hardware, errors in coordinates, terrain models, or gain entries can cause denials or overly cautious limits. For 320 MHz outdoors, narrow-beam directional antennas are a common mitigation, confining energy to a designed sector that the AFC system considers safer. Still, beam misalignment or sway can widen the effective footprint in practice. Treat AFC grants as operating envelopes, then engineer physical stability and pattern control to keep real emissions inside those envelopes during wind and temperature changes.

Frameworks and decision lenses

A practical lens starts with demand density versus spectrum cleanliness, because 320 MHz only helps when the environment can support a wide, quiet lane. If the expected AFC grant historically fragments the band, choose 160 MHz with stronger EIRP instead. Use a simple threshold: if more than one third of the subchannels are routinely punctured in your survey window, model a narrower plan. Consider power as well as width, since higher EIRP at 160 MHz can sustain MCS rates better than a power-limited 320 MHz link. The why is physical: modulation requires SNR headroom, and wider bandwidth increases noise power without guaranteeing signal gain.

Topology alignment is the next lens, because site geometry and antenna patterns dictate how energy overlaps protected paths. Point to multipoint rooftops can leverage sectorized antennas that naturally fit within tighter AFC limits, while stadium concourses might prefer multiple smaller cells to reduce composite footprint. Use historical weather and sway data to choose mounts that minimize beam drift, especially on tall masts. Add a margin to height and tilt entries so the AFC model does not underestimate exposure. The tradeoff is potential overconservatism, but stable grants are often worth a small reduction in predicted EIRP. Reliability improves when your real-world emissions stay below modeled bounds.

Operational resilience is the third lens, because AFC queries and updates occur on real timelines that affect service. Plan for grant refresh latency and potential denials by defining fallback channel plans and rate adaptation floors. Instrument the controller to alert when permissible EIRP drops or when a 320 MHz authorization becomes fragmented. This supports controlled transitions to 160 or 80 MHz during constrained periods. The interlock between power, width, and QoS prioritization will determine perceived application quality during changes.

Examples and short scenarios

Consider a municipal rooftop backhaul where two buildings have near line of sight across low-rise terrain. The AFC history shows consistent approval for the upper 320 MHz segment, but with a 3 dB lower EIRP than your target. You deploy a high-gain, narrow-beam antenna and accept the EIRP cap, relying on alignment to sustain MCS 8 to 9 for most clients. When wind events exceed design sway, the link drops to MCS 6 briefly yet remains stable because the grant is wide and unpunctured. The tradeoff is limited margin for new subscribers on that sector, suggesting a parallel 160 MHz lane for growth without exceeding authorization limits.

Now take a waterfront venue where shipborne reflections and temporary structures cause fluctuating multipath. AFC grants frequently puncture the center of what would be a 320 MHz channel, carving out blocks to protect an intermittent path. You test both 320 with punctures and clean 160, observing that consistent 160 delivers higher median throughput because rate adaptation remains in a healthy range. The lesson is that a nominally wider channel does not guarantee higher goodput when power and spectral continuity are constrained. Planning for clean narrower bandwidths ensures steadier latency and avoids retries that waste airtime on marginal symbols.

In a dense district with several fixed service incumbents, the authorization often fragments the band into safe islands. Here, multi-link operation can help aggregate two 160 MHz lanes when clients support it, but careful antenna isolation and timing are required. If your controller reports frequent AFC refreshes that alter permissible subsets, prefer a deterministic 80 or 160 plan and keep 320 as a conditional enhancement. The path to stability is aligning client capabilities with the most repeatable grants you observe.

Looking ahead

The most reliable path forward is to forecast grants before hardware is mounted, then validate with on-roof measurements after AFC authorization. Build a repository of site-level grant histories so you can correlate weather, season, and construction changes with authorization shifts. This lets you tune margin for EIRP and width proactively. Adopt a review cadence where any sustained drop in permissible power triggers a design check. The aim is to keep modulation rates stable rather than chase raw channel width. Embedding this rhythm into operations maintains quality even as the authorization ecosystem evolves with additional incumbents.

Expand planning models to include antenna sway envelopes and real downtilt variability, since small changes move energy into protected azimuths. For tall structures, evaluate dynamic load conditions that may increase mast flex. Use these insights to select brackets and guying that rigidly hold the intended pattern. Consider inventorying multiple antenna options per site, enabling fast swaps when AFC grants reward narrower beams. The tradeoff is increased upfront complexity, but it reduces later truck rolls. When AFC updates alter available channels, your team can adapt with swifter hardware alignment rather than complete redesign.

Finally, design graceful degradation paths that keep applications responsive when 320 MHz narrows or power drops. Preconfigure profiles for 160 and 80 MHz with QoS policies that protect latency sensitive flows during transitions. Set thresholds that trigger plan changes only when benefits outweigh churn, avoiding unnecessary flaps. Keep stakeholders aware that authorization is a living constraint, not a one-time permit. By treating AFC like weather that you forecast, track, and respect, outdoor 6 GHz deployments remain predictable. This mindset delivers sustainable performance while staying inside the guardrails that make shared spectrum workable.

Frequently asked questions

Does AFC always allow a full 320 MHz channel outdoors?

No. The grant depends on incumbents near your location and antenna pattern. Many sites receive fragmented approvals or lower EIRP that make 160 or 80 MHz more practical for consistent SNR and throughput.

How often do AFC grants change in real deployments?

Change frequency varies by region and database updates. Treat grants as dynamic and monitor for shifts. Build fallbacks and alerting so bandwidth or EIRP adjustments happen deliberately rather than reactively during busy periods.

Are directional antennas required for outdoor 6 GHz standard power?

They are not universally required, but narrow patterns often help meet limits by reducing off-axis energy. Directional designs can preserve SNR at lower EIRP and are frequently favored in grant-constrained environments.

When is 160 MHz better than a punctured 320 MHz grant?

Choose 160 MHz when punctures remove a significant portion of subchannels or when EIRP caps reduce modulation robustness. Clean narrower channels often sustain higher median rates and steadier latency.

What should I log for AFC troubleshooting?

Record coordinates, height, antenna model and tilt, grant timestamps, permissible channels, EIRP limits, and changes over time. Correlate these with weather and performance to refine design assumptions.
