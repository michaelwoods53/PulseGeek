How Wide Should EHT Channels Be? Deploy with Intent

By Courtney Lawson • Published February 22, 2026

The short answer is to start with 80 MHz channels for EHT, widen only when your spectrum and client mix can sustain it, and measure the results. Wider channels promise peak throughput, but EHT networks live or die by interference control and scheduling. This article unpacks how channel width interacts with EHT features like puncturing and RU allocation, when 160 or 320 MHz makes sense, and how to validate decisions. You will see how wide channels affect contention, why narrower widths often win in dense deployments, and how to test performance safely before rolling changes across sites.

Key takeaways

  • Default to 80 MHz, widen only with clear spectrum and proof.
  • Puncturing salvages capacity when a few subchannels face interference.
  • RU scheduling favors stable medium access over headline data rates.
  • Validate with controlled airtime, retries, and latency distributions.
  • Use 320 MHz only where clients and spectrum truly support it.

Short answer and nuance

Start with 80 MHz channels for EHT unless your spectrum is unusually clean and your client base supports wider operation. This baseline balances peak rate with manageable interference across typical enterprise floors and public venues. For example, a campus with moderate AP density can schedule uplink and downlink OFDMA efficiently at 80 MHz, preserving fairness for laptops and phones while limiting adjacent channel exposure. The tradeoff is lower single‑station headline speeds than 160 or 320 MHz, but the network often gains throughput stability and predictable latency. The why is simple: wider channels multiply the chance that part of the channel overlaps with interferers, raising contention and retries that harm real workloads.

Consider 160 MHz when spectrum is lightly used, client radios and drivers are verified, and you can contain co‑channel interference across floors. A lab with separated test rooms or a small office with few neighbors might benefit, especially for high‑bitrate transfers like media editing. The benefit is higher PHY rates for capable clients, yet the risk is more frequent partial‑band interference that triggers puncturing or forces fallbacks. If your area includes unmanaged neighbors, DFS regions, or mixed AP vendors, wider operation can fragment airtime with frequent channel availability checks. The key is to pilot in isolation first and observe retry spikes, MCS distribution, and latency tails.

Reserve 320 MHz for rare cases where 6 GHz spectrum is clear, AP placement is sparse, and client support is known good. Some specialized environments like controlled labs or demo spaces can exploit it, but only when you protect surrounding networks. The upside is extreme burst throughput for a few devices, while the downside is broad spectral footprint that increases adjacent and co‑channel contention risk. Even with EHT preamble puncturing, you still lose effective bandwidth when subchannels are impaired. The mechanism is straightforward: larger channels expose more bandwidth to potential blockers, so the probability of impaired subcarriers rises with width, eroding the promised gains under real conditions.

When it applies vs when it does not

Widen channels when your environment shows low noise floors and minimal overlapping BSS, confirmed by spectrum scans across the full band. In greenfield 6 GHz deployments, you may find contiguous spectrum blocks that justify 160 MHz with conservative AP counts per floor. For example, a robotics lab with time‑segmented traffic can schedule heavy transfers during maintenance windows, avoiding contention with latency‑sensitive flows. The tradeoff is scheduling discipline: drift back toward unscheduled peaks and the wider channel can degrade everyone. The reason is that OFDMA gains require orderly airtime allocation, and unmanaged bursts create collisions that negate the theoretical rate advantage of wider bandwidths.

Do not widen in high‑density venues where many APs and clients share limited spectrum, such as lecture halls or arenas. In these spaces, 80 MHz or even 40 MHz often maintains higher usable throughput across the population by reducing co‑channel overlap and keeping contention domains small. Picture a stadium concourse where neighboring APs cannot be physically isolated. A 160 MHz choice doubles overlap risk and drags down median MCS as clients back off. The limitation is that humans move in bursts, creating correlated airtime demand. Narrower channels contain the blast radius so RU scheduling can keep more clients serviced at steady rates.

Mixed‑capability fleets complicate widening, because legacy or mid‑tier EHT clients may not track DFS events or puncturing patterns consistently. If your telemetry shows frequent channel change announcements, periodic radar detections, or uneven driver behavior, a narrow baseline gives you a safer control surface. One mitigation is deliberate segmentation: confine experimental wide channels to a dedicated SSID and AP group with a limited client allowlist. The drawback is operational complexity and possible roaming friction, but it prevents surprises for business‑critical devices. The underlying principle is simple: stability for the many beats peak for the few when signal hygiene and client conformance are uncertain.

  • Keep dense venues at 80 or 40 MHz to limit overlap and retries.

How to implement or validate

Implement width changes in a small pilot, with explicit pass and fail criteria that include throughput stability and latency percentiles. Begin by pinning an AP group to a candidate width and defining allowed channels that avoid known interferers. Use preamble puncturing conservatively to mask occasional narrowband blockers, but assume it cannot save a channel that is broadly contested. Your validation plan should gather per‑client MCS, retry rate, and RU allocation metrics during normal and peak periods. If your latency distribution fattens in the 95th percentile or retries exceed a safe baseline, roll back. This method protects production users while producing actionable evidence.

On infrastructure that exposes CLI or controller templates, set width, primary channel, and allowed lists explicitly, then lock transmit power to maintain target cell size. The goal is to avoid accidental expansion of contention domains when widening. After applying changes, verify the channel map with a passive scan and ensure neighboring APs do not overlap on primary or secondary segments. If your platform supports scheduled changes, stage them during low‑impact windows and keep a revert profile ready. The limitation is that some consumer clients cache channel behavior aggressively, so plan a short roam cycle to refresh state before testing.

Alternatives and related questions

When width cannot increase safely, use spatial reuse, transmit power tuning, and AP placement to raise capacity without broadening contention domains. Spatial reuse with careful OBSS_PD thresholds can permit simultaneous transmissions by reducing defer behavior across distinct BSS, but only when clients implement it well. Another lever is targeted RU allocation and scheduling that prioritizes small uplinks like telephony frames, which stabilizes latency even at 80 MHz. The tradeoff is deployment complexity and the need for controller insight, yet the payoff is consistent user experience. Looking ahead

The safest path is to standardize on 80 MHz for general EHT service, then pilot wider channels in clearly bounded spaces with well‑instrumented measurements. Use your pilot to build local evidence: spectrum scans over time, per‑client MCS and retry patterns, and latency percentiles under realistic load. If the data proves gains without harming neighbors, expand cautiously. If not, invest in scheduling, placement, and power control improvements. This layered approach protects users while letting you exploit EHT advances when conditions truly allow it.

Frequently asked questions

Is 320 MHz worth it for office Wi Fi?

Only in rare cases with very clean 6 GHz spectrum, few APs, and verified client support. Most offices get better stability from 80 or 160 MHz with careful placement and power control.

Should I switch all APs to 160 MHz at once?

No. Pilot in a limited area, validate retries and latency percentiles, and check neighbor overlap. Roll out gradually only if evidence shows improvement without harming adjacent cells.

Does preamble puncturing make wide channels safe?

Puncturing helps salvage capacity when a few subchannels are impaired, but it cannot fix broadly contested spectrum. Use it as a mitigation, not a reason to over widen channels.

When is 40 MHz still a good idea in EHT?

In very dense areas where 80 MHz still creates too much overlap or when clients show unstable behavior with wider operation. Smaller contention domains often improve median throughput and fairness.

How do I know if clients support wider widths?

Check client capability reports, driver release notes, and real association frames for advertised bandwidth and MCS. Then confirm in practice with throughput, retry, and latency measurements during load.
