Frame Pacing and V‑Sync Settings for Remote Play

By Jacob Reed • Published January 23, 2026

Remote play feels best when frame pacing stays consistent, V‑Sync is matched to the display, and latency remains predictable. The biggest wins arrive from aligning the game’s output to the network encoder and the handheld display, rather than chasing raw FPS. This article explains how pacing irregularities add judder, why V‑Sync settings can either mask or expose tearing, and where frame caps reduce encode delay. We will map common Deck scenarios like 40 Hz, 60 Hz, and external VRR screens to practical settings. Expect clear decision rules you can test quickly during a session. The aim is to balance smoothness with responsiveness, not to maximize counters. We will also point to codec and bitrate adjustments that complement pacing choices without complicating your workflow.

Key takeaways

  • Stable frame pacing beats higher but uneven FPS for remote play.
  • Cap near display refresh to cut encode delay and reduce judder.
  • Use client V‑Sync or VRR when server pacing is already steady.
  • Prefer 40, 50, or 60 caps over awkward fractional frame times.
  • Monitor end to end latency while adjusting caps and V‑Sync mode.

Concepts and definitions

Frame pacing describes how evenly frames arrive at the display and encoder, and it matters more than absolute FPS for remote play. Even 45 FPS can look smooth if frame times sit near 22 ms with minimal variance, while 60 FPS with spikes to 40 ms produces visible hitching. V‑Sync aligns buffer swaps to refresh to prevent tearing, but it can hide pacing issues by delaying late frames. On the network side, the encoder prefers a steady cadence to avoid bursty output that challenges bitrate control. The goal is not perfect synchronicity, which is impossible across PC, network, and Deck, but sufficient regularity that the client can predict and present frames smoothly. Understanding this hierarchy helps you pick which knob to turn first during tuning.

V‑Sync has variants with different tradeoffs, and labeling often causes confusion during remote play. Classic V‑Sync waits for refresh and can add a full frame of delay when a frame misses its slot. Fast Sync and similar modes decouple render from present, reducing tearing at high FPS but increasing variance at moderate rates. Adaptive V‑Sync engages only when FPS exceeds refresh, then disables during drops, which can worsen judder if frame times oscillate. On handheld displays without variable refresh rate, a simple cap that meets or slightly undercuts refresh often outperforms clever V‑Sync modes. The reason is predictability: the encoder and client thrive when frames follow a monotonic rhythm that survives minor timing noise.

Frame caps serve two roles in remote play: they pace the game’s workload and they shape encode latency. A well chosen cap reduces render queue depth, shortens the time a frame spends waiting to be encoded, and stabilizes the client’s jitter buffer. For a 60 Hz screen, capping at 60 with proper V‑Sync can work if the GPU has headroom; capping at 58 to 59 can be safer when spikes exist. For Deck’s 40 Hz mode, a 40 cap often feels smoother than unlocked 50 to 60 with stutter, because each 25 ms frame lands predictably. The limitation is CPU bound scenes where a cap cannot fix uneven simulation time; there, trimming graphics load or reducing resolution is necessary to restore cadence.

Frameworks and decision lenses

A practical rule is to start with the display’s refresh, then choose the lowest cap that the game can sustain under typical load. On a 60 Hz panel, test 60, 59, and 58 caps with V‑Sync on, watching for input delay and tearing. If the overlay shows recurrent spikes, select the lower cap to preserve pacing, since stable 58 often beats erratic 60. For a 40 Hz Deck setting, begin at 40 with V‑Sync enabled, then adjust game settings until frame times hold below 25 ms. This sequence minimizes encoder back pressure because frames arrive on schedule. The tradeoff is slightly higher blur at lower FPS, but the gain in consistency improves motion perception and reduces rebuffering risk across the link.

Use VRR differently: with a VRR monitor connected, cap just under its upper bound and disable strict V‑Sync on the server to avoid missed slots. The client’s presentation then tracks actual frame time, which lets the network jitter buffer breathe. A cap two frames below the VRR ceiling, such as 116 on a 120 Hz display, reduces oscillation near the limit. When VRR is not available, prefer a hard cap tied to the refresh divisor, like 40, 50, or 60, to avoid fractional rhythms. The limitation appears with heavy physics or streaming stutters where VRR cannot correct CPU stalls; better asset prefetch or reduced draw distances will help more than sync tweaks.

Map your choice to the encoder behavior, since codec control reacts to cadence. Constant bitrate prefers steady input, so a fixed cap often yields cleaner frames at the same budget. Variable bitrate can adapt to bursts, but variance increases quantization swings that reveal banding in dark scenes. If your goal is the sharpest image at modest bandwidth, pair a well paced 40 or 60 cap with a high quality preset and consistent keyframe interval. For deeper codec selection, see the codec deep dive on when to pick H.264, HEVC, or AV1, which outlines latency and hardware support considerations. Proper pacing makes whatever codec you use perform closer to its ideal because rate control sees fewer surprises.

Examples and short scenarios

Consider a 60 Hz Deck session where the PC can render 70 to 90 FPS in open areas but dips to 55 in cities. Uncapped with V‑Sync off, tearing appears and the encoder faces uneven bursts that inflate quantization. Enabling V‑Sync smooths presentation but introduces latency spikes during heavy scenes. Capping at 58 with V‑Sync on stabilizes frame times near 17 ms plus small overhead, which the encoder handles gracefully. The visual result is fewer swings in detail on foliage and cleaner motion vectors. The tradeoff is marginally higher input lag than an unlocked run, but end to end delay remains more consistent, which players often perceive as more responsive.

Now evaluate a 40 Hz target on the internal screen where the game struggles between 36 and 44 FPS. Running unlocked causes cadence flip flopping that the client struggles to present without judder. Setting a 40 cap with V‑Sync on forces a predictable 25 ms rhythm and leaves headroom for brief spikes. If the simulation frame occasionally exceeds 25 ms, the client shows a repeated frame instead of a late tear, which looks smoother in handheld use. Pairing this pacing with tuned bitrate and resolution, as described in a guide that matches bitrate to resolution, preserves clarity with fewer artifacts. The remaining limitation is motion blur perception at 40 FPS, which some fast shooters may dislike.

For a VRR external monitor, imagine a 48 to 120 Hz range with a PC that hovers around 100 FPS. Disable strict V‑Sync and set a 112 to 116 cap so the renderer does not bang into the ceiling. VRR tracks each frame interval, which keeps motion stable while avoiding queuing. This leaves the encoder with near constant intervals that reduce keyframe timing conflicts. If occasional drops to 70 occur, VRR conceals them better than fixed 60 because no resync is needed. The caveat is streaming over congested Wi‑Fi, where network jitter dominates; in that case, reduce bitrate or switch to a codec profile suited for lower bandwidth rather than chasing higher caps.

Looking ahead

The next step is to validate pacing choices while watching real metrics rather than relying on feel alone. Start with your target display mode, select a cap as outlined above, and monitor frametime graphs alongside end to end delay. If you observe repeating spikes tied to effects or streaming loads, lower the cap by one to two frames and retest. When cadence steadies, refine bitrate or resolution to remove encoder stress rather than pushing FPS. This methodical loop ensures each change serves the overall path from render to display. It also builds a reusable baseline you can apply across new games without repeating guesswork.

Broaden your toolset by understanding how codecs behave at different rates and resolutions, since pacing and compression interact. A focused comparison of H.264, HEVC, and AV1 clarifies where quality per bit improves and how hardware support shapes latency. Use that knowledge to avoid chasing higher caps when a better codec setting could deliver clarity at the same frame budget. For more detailed setup across the LAN, an end to end walkthrough helps align network choices with your pacing targets. Linking insights across these domains prevents local optimizations that harm the full system.

Finally, establish a small test matrix that mirrors your common play patterns, such as busy city zones, fast driving, and dense foliage. Record frame time variance, encode latency, and tearing frequency for each cap and sync combination you try. Over two or three short sessions, you will see which settings deliver robust results across diverse scenes. Preserve one conservative preset for unstable networks and one aggressive but still steady preset for wired LAN. This practical documentation makes future adjustments faster and more reliable, especially when game updates shift performance characteristics.

Key terms

  • Adaptive Sync — Display tech that matches refresh to frame rate.
  • Client Station — A Wi‑Fi device that connects to an AP.
  • Frame Rate Cap — A set limit on frames per second to control performance and power.
  • Frames Per Second — The number of images shown each second in a game.
  • Input Latency — Delay between pressing a control and seeing the result.
  • Network Latency — The time it takes for data to travel between your device and the game server.
  • Screen Tearing — Visible split between frames on the display.
  • Variable Refresh Rate — Display tech that changes refresh rate to match frame rate.

Frequently asked questions

Should I enable V‑Sync on the server or the client?

Enable V‑Sync on the server when using fixed refresh modes like 40 or 60 Hz. With VRR displays, prefer a cap just below the upper bound and disable strict server V‑Sync so VRR can track actual frame times.

Is a 40 FPS cap better than an unstable 60 for handheld use?

Yes, a stable 40 often looks smoother than a fluctuating 60 because consistent 25 ms frames reduce judder and encoder variance. If input latency feels high, lower graphics load to sustain a solid 60 instead.

How close should my cap be to display refresh?

Start at refresh when headroom is ample, then test one to two FPS below if spikes occur. For VRR, cap two to eight FPS under the ceiling to avoid oscillating at the limit while keeping pacing steady.

Does VRR remove the need for perfect pacing?

VRR tolerates moderate variance by matching presentation to render timing, but it cannot hide CPU stalls or long simulation frames. Good caps and stable frame times still improve encoder behavior and perceived smoothness.

Will capping FPS reduce input latency for remote play?

Often yes. A cap can shrink render and encode queues, lowering end to end delay. If the cap is too low, perceived latency can increase, so find the highest stable cap that maintains even frame times.
