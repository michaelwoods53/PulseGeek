802.11be PHY Conformance Testing Checklist

By Allison Grant • Published February 1, 2026

Use this checklist to structure 802.11be PHY conformance testing with clear pass and fail gates. The items align to PHY behaviors like masks, EVM, OFDMA, and MLO timing, and they map to how labs verify limits. Each section shows an actionable step, a concrete example, and a tradeoff so you can balance rigor with time. Where deeper context helps, we reference resources on the 802.11be standard and certification prep. This format aims to reduce surprises when results leave your bench and enter a chamber, keeping measurements defensible and repeatable across environments.

Key takeaways

  • Tie each PHY measurement to a stated limit and tolerance.
  • Record RF setup, cables, and losses to maintain repeatability.
  • Probe EVM and spectral mask under worst MCS and bandwidth.
  • Validate MLO timing windows with controlled latency injection.
  • Cross check MU precoding with sounding errors and CSI freshness.
  • Sweep sensitivity by MCS and bandwidth with PER targets.

1) Channelization and RU masks

Start by proving bandwidth, puncturing, and resource unit masks because RU integrity underpins reliable EHT operation. Verify that 160 and 320 MHz modes honor allowed puncturing patterns and that RU allocations align with the allocation bitmap for EHT OFDMA. For example, drive an AP to transmit EHT PPDU with a mixed 106 and 484 tone layout, then confirm subcarrier occupancy and guard band structure using a vector signal analyzer with appropriate span and RBW. The tradeoff is measurement speed versus resolution. Narrow RBW and longer sweeps reveal leakage and misaligned notches but extend run time and increase sensitivity to environmental drift. Establish a rule of thumb like 30 kHz RBW for mask shape screening, then step to 10 kHz for borderline cases to balance throughput and certainty.

A practical workflow is to script traffic that forces deterministic RU maps and channel puncturing while you capture spectra and demodulated carriers. Use a packet capture companion to correlate PPDU fields with the spectrum you observe, ensuring the allocation index and puncture flags match. For instance, while validating allocation masks, compare captured headers to analyzer traces to spot illegal overlap with restricted subchannels. The limitation is that not all traffic generators expose fine grained RU control, so you may need a firmware debug build or a dedicated test mode to reach specific layouts. When those are unavailable, choose representative RU sizes and document coverage gaps so reviewers understand the test envelope and its assumptions.

2) EVM and transmitter spectral mask

Validate error vector magnitude and spectral mask across bandwidths because they quantify modulation quality and spectral containment in EHT PPDUs. Exercise highest MCS per chain and widest bandwidth to stress linearity, then repeat at mid power where many radios are cleaner. As a concrete step, measure EVM on data fields under MCS 13 or vendor max, 320 MHz, with dynamic EHT-LTF counts and pilot tracking enabled. Cross check the transmitter mask by sweeping offsets with a 100 kHz RBW and comparing against allowed dBr limits. The tradeoff is PAPR and crest factor management. Enabling peak reduction can improve mask margins but may distort EVM. Record both states and justify the shipping configuration so the mask and EVM pair remains within limits for the intended deployment.

To tighten repeatability, normalize cable loss and account for switch path variations before power leveling. Calibrate at the analyzer input with a fixed reference and document the chain. For example, set target conducted power at 12 dBm per chain, then adjust analyzer attenuation to avoid compression while holding EVM measurement range stable. If your device supports test modes that bypass MAC scheduling, use them to isolate PHY behavior and remove traffic burst variability. The limitation is that chamber OTA runs introduce additional uncertainty due to reflections and polarization mismatch. Use conducted testing to establish a baseline and treat OTA as confirmation under realistic but noisier conditions, noting differences that trace to antenna patterns rather than baseband impairment.

Section highlights

  • Stress EVM and mask at highest MCS and widest bandwidth.
  • Document calibration and treat OTA as confirmation of margins.

3) MLO PHY timing and sync

Confirm multi link operation timing because PHY skew between links impacts aggregation and reliability. Measure alignment of PPDU start times across links and verify conformance with allowed window under expected interconnect delays. For example, inject controlled latency on one link path using a coax delay or programmable baseband hold, then capture both links with dual channel analysis to quantify skew and jitter. The tradeoff is tool complexity. Dual channel captures require time aligned triggers and known path delays. If unavailable, estimate skew using synchronized sniffers and beacon timestamp drift, accepting larger uncertainty. Document the maximum misalignment observed and the conditions, since validation relies on bounding worst case skew rather than proving exact equivalence in every dynamic state found in the field.

Stress MLO with asymmetric bandwidth and differing MCS per link to emulate heterogeneous channel quality. Drive link A at 320 MHz and link B at 160 MHz while alternating primary traffic to confirm robust aggregation and recovery when one link degrades. A practical scenario is to jam one link slightly with controlled interference, then verify scheduling avoids stalls and that PHY signaling remains valid. When you need structured test cases that relate to certified expectations, consult a clause level overview of the amendment to map terminology and features to your device modes. For more detailed standard context, study a foundational overview that explains EHT features and how the amendment integrates with base 802.11 so your timing tests use correct assumptions.

Section highlights

  • Quantify cross link skew with controlled delay and aligned capture.
  • Exercise asymmetric links to verify aggregation under degradation.

4) OFDMA and MU-MIMO precoding accuracy

Assess OFDMA integrity and multi user MIMO precoding because PHY conformance depends on correct RU isolation and beamforming accuracy. Capture EHT MU frames with sounding enabled, then compute error relative to expected steering based on channel state information age and feedback granularity. As an example, run two STA emulators with controlled paths and known SNR, issue NDP sounding, then transmit MU data to verify per user EVM and inter user leakage across RUs. The tradeoff is equipment cost versus insight. Dedicated channel emulators yield repeatable CSI freshness and delay spread but may be unavailable. If using cabled paths, vary cable lengths and attenuators to approximate delay, noting that results are less representative of multipath but sufficient to catch gross precoding mistakes.

Include a check for CSI timeout and fallback behavior. Verify that when feedback grows stale the AP reduces MU depth or reverts to SU transmission rather than persisting with invalid steering matrices. A concrete check is to delay sounding beyond the configured freshness threshold and confirm throughput degrades gracefully while EVM remains within single user expectations. This connects to readiness for interoperability tests where predictable fallback prevents unfair failures. For hands on planning of interoperability scenarios such as MLO and roaming interactions that affect MU scheduling, reference an interoperability test plan outline so your PHY runs anticipate mixed mode behavior and avoid conflating MAC transitions with PHY impairment during result analysis.

Section highlights

  • Measure per user EVM and leakage to validate MU steering.
  • Verify CSI age triggers graceful fallback to SU transmission.

5) Receiver sensitivity and PER

Quantify receiver sensitivity using packet error rate sweeps because PHY conformance hinges on reliable demodulation near noise floor. For each bandwidth and MCS, generate a downlink stream with known payload and count PER while stepping conducted power. For example, measure PER across MCS 0 to mid tier at 20, 80, 160, and 320 MHz to map performance knees. Use enough packets per point to reach statistical confidence, then fit a curve to interpolate where PER crosses your pass threshold. The tradeoff is test time. More packets and fine power steps increase confidence but lengthen the matrix. Use coarser steps to find the slope, then refine around the crossover region. Keep thermal stabilization in mind, as sensitivity drifts with device temperature during extended runs.

To separate PHY limits from MAC behavior, use continuous PPDU test modes or carefully configured traffic to avoid backoff interactions. Log decoder states if available, such as LDPC iteration counts or fallback events, to explain sharp PER transitions. A practical step is to compare sensitivity with and without spatial streams enabled, since combining gains impact PER at the edge. Looking ahead

Store analyzer presets, power leveling notes, and traffic sequences alongside result files so another engineer can reproduce the measurement within expected uncertainty. As you iterate, tighten worst case scenarios that exposed fragile behavior and document why margins moved. This audit trail is valuable during reviews and helps maintain confidence when firmware changes shift performance. Key terms

  • 802.11be Extremely High Throughput — The IEEE spec that defines Wi‑Fi 7 features and limits.
  • Channel State Information Reference Signal — Known signals used to estimate the channel for CSI.
  • Error Vector Magnitude (EVM) — A measure of the difference between ideal and received constellation points.
  • Modulation and Coding Scheme — An index pairing of modulation order and coding rate.
  • Modulation and Coding Scheme Index — A number indicating modulation and coding level.
  • Multiple-Input Multiple-Output — Using multiple antennas to increase throughput and reliability.
  • Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiple Access — A multi-user transmission method dividing channels into subcarriers.
  • Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiple Access — A scheme where multiple users share subcarriers simultaneously.

Frequently asked questions

How many packets are enough for a reliable PER point?

Pick a count that makes random variation small relative to your threshold. Many teams start with thousands of frames, then increase near the crossover to tighten confidence without exploding total test time.

Should EVM be measured conducted or over the air?

Use conducted for baseline because it reduces multipath and polarization variables. Then confirm margins over the air to capture antenna effects and chamber realities. Record both so differences are understood.

How do I choose RBW for spectral mask testing?

Use a moderate RBW to screen for gross violations quickly, then switch to finer RBW near limit edges. This balances run time with resolution and reduces false passes or fails around steep mask slopes.

What is the best way to emulate CSI aging?

Increase time between sounding and data or introduce controlled channel changes. Watch for MU fallback to SU or reduced user count, and verify EVM and throughput remain within acceptable limits.
