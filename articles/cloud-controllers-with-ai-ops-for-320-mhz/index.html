1) AI Ops baselines that learn 320 MHz reality

Start with a claim that matters: AI Ops only helps 320 MHz if its baselines mirror ultra-wide channel dynamics across time. A practical example is modeling normal latency variance by hour for a lab floor with mixed clients, then flagging only deviations beyond learned confidence bands rather than static thresholds. This approach avoids false positives when busy but healthy traffic pushes jitter slightly up. The tradeoff is warm-up time. Systems need several days of clean data before guidance is reliable, and sudden topology changes can reset learning. Favor controllers that show how baselines were learned and let operators pin maintenance windows, so the algorithm does not teach on noisy periods that would make future detection sloppy.

Effective baselines for 320 MHz also separate controllable airtime usage from noise-driven retries that ride the wide channel’s larger spectral footprint. For instance, a controller might compare retry rates on the primary 20 MHz against the full 320 MHz occupancy to infer whether puncturing a few subchannels could relieve collisions without harming aggregate throughput. The upside is targeted recommendations based on evidence, not heuristics. The limitation is visibility scope. If your fleet includes multiple controller domains, learning may silo per domain and miss cross-site patterns. Look for exportable baselines and APIs that synchronize learned norms across regions to keep actions consistent after changes in client mix or RF environment.

3) Analytics aware of puncturing and MLO behavior

AI Ops must understand puncturing patterns and multi-link operation to avoid misleading guidance on 320 MHz. A useful example is surfacing per-link latency and effective throughput when a client spreads streams across two 160 MHz links. If analytics show one link is constrained by neighboring occupancy, the controller can recommend persistent punctures on those subchannels while keeping the other link wide. The advantage is targeted stability without collapsing capacity network-wide. The constraint is client diversity. Not all clients implement MLO equally, and some will prefer link-steering that conflicts with controller advice. Pick platforms that display per-client link selection and document how recommendations interact with client behavior to avoid chasing unattainable targets.

Good systems also test the effect of puncturing changes on airtime before recommending fleet-wide policies. For example, an A/B window compares retry rates and achieved MCS on lightly punctured 320 MHz versus fully open width during similar traffic loads. AI then quantifies whether narrower but cleaner air improved net throughput. The benefit is deliberate change control with data. The downside is temporary complexity during tests and the risk of bias if traffic composition shifts mid experiment. Controllers should schedule matched test windows and publish uncertainty bands. 4) Low-noise DFS and latency alerting patterns

Alerting should focus on DFS events and latency breaches with deduplication so operators act quickly without alert fatigue. A concrete setup is a threshold that triggers when p95 one-way latency exceeds a learned baseline for three consecutive intervals and a DFS hit occurs within the preceding five minutes on the same AP group. This correlation avoids pinging teams on transient blips. The tradeoff is sensitivity versus delay. Longer windows reduce noise but can slow response to real performance cliffs. Choose controllers that support composite conditions, grouped notifications, and quiet hours that match maintenance windows. For planning a broader dashboard that spots contention early, build a utilization view that highlights 320 MHz occupancy when latency strains before user complaints surface.

To automate a proven pattern, use a controller API to declare a composite alert with baseline-relative thresholds and DFS correlation. The call should specify the metric, window, and aggregation, then define dedupe keys by AP and channel. The expected outcome is a quiet, meaningful notification stream that highlights service degradation rather than false alarms. Adjust thresholds after a week of observations to balance coverage and noise. Keep credentials in a secure store and never embed secrets in clear text.

5) SNR and RSSI interpretation on ultra-wide channels

Accurate SNR and RSSI interpretation is essential because 320 MHz widens the noise aperture, which can mute perceived gains. A practical scenario is comparing SNR on the primary 20 MHz against composite SNR across the full width to detect noise floor shifts that mimic coverage problems. If primary SNR is healthy but composite SNR dips, the issue is often adjacent occupancy or narrow interferers. The tradeoff is complexity in presentation. Too many plots confuse operators, yet insufficient detail leads to wrong fixes like raising transmit power. Favor controllers that normalize readings, explain calculation methods in plain language, and flag when SNR changes align with retry spikes. For hands-on guidance that avoids misreads, study how to interpret SNR and RSSI on ultra-wide channels where noise and CCI distort expectations.

Good designs also tie SNR shifts to MCS outcomes rather than treating SNR as a standalone badge. For example, when SNR hovers in a midrange but MCS collapses under load, rate adaptation is reacting to contention, not RF decay, which suggests exploring queue tuning or narrower punctures. The upside is avoiding power changes that increase cell overlap and raise contention. The limitation is client diversity and antenna layouts that skew signal paths. Choose platforms that show per-SS and per-client histograms and keep annotations that explain when a suggested change is expected to help specific device classes. Frequently asked questions

Do I need 320 MHz everywhere or only in select areas?

Use 320 MHz where client density and traffic patterns benefit from wider lanes and where interference is low. Many environments perform better with mixed widths tuned by time of day and application demands.

How long should AI baselines train before trusting suggestions?

Plan for several days of representative traffic and exclude maintenance windows. Reevaluate after topology changes or firmware updates that may alter traffic composition or radio behavior.

Are auto-remediations safe to enable on day one?

Start in recommendation-only mode, review evidence, then enable bounded actions on a subset of APs. Expand gradually after verifying improved airtime and steady latency under realistic load.

What single metric best predicts user complaints on 6 GHz?

No single metric is sufficient. Correlate p95 latency, retries, and MCS shifts. Rising latency paired with retry spikes is a consistent early indicator of degraded experience.
