Channel Change Automation (RRM) for 320 MHz

By Rachel Morgan • Published February 4, 2026

Channel change automation matters when 320 MHz channels magnify both capacity and interference risk. Robust RRM policies decide when radios should stay put or move, using telemetry like noise floor, neighbor load, and DFS history. The goal is stability first, speed second, because wide lanes amplify any misstep. This guide defines the decision surface for automated changes, clarifies safe cadences, and flags tradeoffs that appear with multi link operation and puncturing. Readers who own controller policies or design guardrails will find practical thresholds and rollback ideas that keep latency sensitive traffic steady. When automation is predictable, operators can raise utilization without gambling on disruptive swings.

Key takeaways

  • Favor stability when automating wide 320 MHz channel changes.
  • Use time windows and hold timers to protect active sessions.
  • Score channels with noise, occupancy, DFS history, and overlaps.
  • Require multi step confirmation and enable fast rollback paths.

Concepts and definitions

Channel automation for 320 MHz is a policy that scores candidate channels and authorizes changes under strict timing. The core inputs are noise floor, channel utilization, overlapping basic service sets, and DFS event history within regulatory windows. A good mental model is a continuous scoring loop that resists short spikes by averaging over a minimum hold time, often ten to thirty minutes. The policy should separate detect, decide, and act stages so that data collection does not force movement. This matters because wide lanes react strongly to neighbor behavior, creating feedback if changes propagate too fast. Designing the loop to dampen oscillation prevents ripples across adjacent cells and keeps applications stable under varying client mixes.

RRM, or radio resource management, aligns channel width, power, and selection across access points to minimize contention and maximize airtime fairness. With 320 MHz, the selection step dominates because a single decision spans many 20 MHz units and may collide with multiple neighbors. That is why puncturing, which disables subchannels with interference, acts like a relief valve but cannot rescue a fundamentally bad lane choice. Multi link operation adds complexity since links may prefer different widths or carriers at once. In practice, RRM should recognize when to maintain wide lanes and when to prioritize narrower, cleaner paths that preserve reliability for latency sensitive traffic.

Definitions matter for safe timing. A hold timer is the minimum time the AP must remain on a channel after a change, while a dwell window is the period used to average telemetry before any action. A maintenance window is a recurring interval when disruptive moves are allowed, such as overnight local time. Distinguish forced changes triggered by DFS radar detection from elective changes that seek better quality. The first requires immediate evacuation with regulatory non occupancy timers, while the second should respect application quiet periods and client load thresholds. Clear definitions help auditors confirm that policy changes are deliberate, testable, and reversible without guessing.

Frameworks and decision lenses

Start with a scoring function that blends noise, measured occupancy, DFS risk, and overlap penalties. A practical approach weights stable signals higher than bursty ones, for example giving noise and long horizon utilization sixty to seventy percent of the score. Add penalties when neighbor co channel presence exceeds a threshold or when recent radar events make evacuation likely. Then apply gates before acting, such as a minimum improvement delta, a maximum change frequency per AP per day, and a cap on simultaneous changes per floor. These gates slow contagious swings. Cadence is where stability is won. Elective channel changes should happen within defined maintenance windows, for example a two hour overnight local period with a per AP change quota. Outside those windows, only emergency triggers should act, such as DFS radar events or extreme interference surpassing a safe ceiling. Require multi step confirmation, like consecutive windows above threshold, before authorizing a move. After moving, impose a generous hold timer to absorb neighbor adjustments. Coordinate this with power control to avoid compounding coverage shifts. APs should change channels without surprising users. Use session aware quiet periods that detect low voice or video concurrency before acting, and broadcast channel switch announcements when client features support it. Track rollback paths if post change telemetry degrades, reverting after one or two failed health checks. Controllers can stage moves in waves, leaving anchor cells unchanged to preserve roaming predictability. When automation fails to find a clean 320 MHz option, prefer narrower but cleaner lanes to recover airtime. This choice is less risky than stretching puncturing across many subchannels, which often fragments capacity. Predictable behavior builds trust and shortens troubleshooting when incidents occur.

Next steps

Begin with a dry run that computes scores without acting, then compare predicted choices against manual assessments from site surveys. This calibration phase reveals weighting mistakes, like overemphasizing short term utilization spikes. Once gates look reasonable, enable action only inside the maintenance window and keep a one change per day ceiling until behavior proves stable. Track outcomes with a small dashboard that logs pre and post change noise, utilization, retransmissions, and client health. When analysis shows repeated conflicts, revisit width selection and test narrower lanes for a week. Evolve policies with client diversity in mind. Not all devices benefit from 320 MHz and many will attach at modest MCS rates that do not justify the width. Create roles or profiles that bias certain SSIDs or AP groups toward narrower widths during business hours, reserving full width for off peak data pushes. Align this with QoS so voice and video flows retain predictable airtime even as automation explores new lanes. When adoption grows for multi link operation, consider asymmetric widths across links to stabilize latency. This layered approach helps maintain predictable roaming while enabling targeted bursts when the spectrum looks clean.

Finally, document human override procedures. Operators need a simple switch to freeze automation during incidents and a clear path to review change logs with timestamps, scores, and justification fields. Weekly review keeps drift in check and highlights sites that outgrow current thresholds. Share findings across teams so design choices feed back into controller defaults. As you expand to new buildings, use lessons from early sites to pre tune default hold timers and windows. Frequently asked questions

How often should 320 MHz channels change under automation?

Limit elective changes to a predictable nightly window, typically once per AP per day at most. Outside that window, act only on emergency triggers like DFS radar. This cadence prioritizes stability and reduces roaming surprises.

What signals should the RRM score favor most?

Favor stable indicators like noise floor and long horizon utilization. Penalize overlapping neighbors and recent DFS events. Use bursty metrics for confirmation only, not as primary drivers of movement.

How do I prevent oscillation between two wide channels?

Use a hold timer, a minimum score improvement threshold, and multi step confirmation. Cap simultaneous changes per area and stage moves in waves to avoid contagious shifts that cause feedback.

Should I use puncturing to keep 320 MHz during interference?

Puncturing can help in the short term, but persistent interference often makes a narrower clean lane better. Prefer reliable airtime and predictable latency over maintaining width at any cost.
