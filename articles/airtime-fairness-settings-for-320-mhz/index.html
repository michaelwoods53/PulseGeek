Airtime Fairness Settings for 320 MHz

By Rachel Morgan • Published February 1, 2026

Wide channels change how airtime, fairness, and 320 MHz scheduling behave. This how-to guides you from planning intent to validating results so time on the medium is shared without punishing latency traffic. You will define profiles, map traffic classes, assign conservative weights, and test with realistic flows. The environment assumes WiFi 8 hardware with 6 GHz capability, controller or AP firmware that exposes airtime fairness knobs, and access to simple test tools like iperf and a VoIP client. If your platform lacks a feature, the principles still apply through per-SSID or per-client limits and queue tuning.

Key takeaways

  • Decide fairness intent first, then map it to profiles and queues.
  • Use conservative weight ratios to avoid starving slow clients.
  • Protect voice with strict priority and bounded airtime caps.
  • Validate with counters plus live flows, not synthetic speed alone.
  • Adjust for 320 MHz interference by watching retries and puncturing.

Plan the work

Start by defining fairness intent, because 320 MHz magnifies both gains and mistakes. Decide if you want equal time per client, per SSID, or weighted sharing based on device capability or role. For example, a design may give conference room media endpoints twice the airtime of casual browsing clients, but still cap them during busy hours. The tradeoff is that strict weighting can hurt edge devices if retry rates rise on ultra-wide channels. Frame goals with measurable outcomes like target latency under 50 ms for voice, minimum throughput for large file transfers, and maximum retries per minute. This clarity ensures that every later tuning decision maps to a business outcome rather than a guess.

Inventory clients and traffic classes next, because queue priorities and MCS diversity drive airtime needs. Categorize devices into latency sensitive voice or video, high-throughput laptops, and IoT or background sync. As a concrete example, keep a small strict-priority slice for voice using WMM AC_VO while preventing it from expanding unbounded under bursts. Edge cases include devices that mis-mark traffic, which can hog high queues. Establish a rule of thumb that latency classes receive priority treatment but not more than a fixed percent of airtime during congestion. This ensures fairness settings guard critical flows while preventing runaway priority that degrades bulk data.

Map environment constraints so weights stay realistic on 6 GHz with 320 MHz channels. Document regulatory limits, building attenuation, and any channel puncturing patterns expected from neighbors. A simple worksheet can record observed noise floor, average MCS on laptop uplinks, and client counts by SSID during peak hours. The risk is overestimating average PHY rate on ultra-wide channels, which leads to weights that appear fair on paper but stall in practice as retries accumulate. Planning with conservative estimates, such as using median MCS rather than peak, prevents oversubscription and keeps latency objectives achievable under real contention.

Prepare environment

Verify platform capabilities before touching weights, because not all controllers expose equivalent airtime fairness controls. Confirm support for per-SSID and per-client sharing, WMM queue enforcement, and optional features like client load classification. As a working example, some systems provide equal-share, flow-aware, and weighted modes with configurable ratios like 1:1 or 2:1. If your stack lacks weights, you can achieve similar results using rate limits and queue minimums. The limitation is coarser behavior compared to native airtime accounting, but it still guards latency when channels are saturated. Update firmware to the latest stable train to pick up 6 GHz fixes for 320 MHz.

Collect baseline measurements to separate configuration impact from environmental noise. Capture current retries, average MCS per SSID, busy time, and queue drops over a typical hour. Run two small tests: one UDP voice call or synthetic RTP and one TCP download from a local iperf server. Record jitter and throughput while the network is untouched. A baseline like 25 ms jitter and 600 Mbps on a single client provides a concrete yardstick for later comparisons. Edge cases include intermittent DFS-like events on 6 GHz indoor standard power variants governed by AFC rules. Note any puncturing behavior so later fairness changes are not blamed for channel fragmentation effects.

Select a starting profile that matches goals and realities. If you serve mixed device generations, begin with equal per-client airtime to prevent newest clients from monopolizing the medium. For sites prioritizing AV or conferencing, choose a modest weighted profile such as 2:1 for media devices over general clients, with a strict cap for voice at 10 to 15 percent during congestion. The tradeoff is that too-aggressive weighting may starve telemetry uploads from slow devices when retries spike on 320 MHz. Start conservatively, then plan to tune after validation. Execute steps

Apply settings in a controlled order so changes are measurable and reversible. Begin by assigning per-SSID or per-client fairness mode according to your plan, then configure queue priorities for voice and video. As an example, set AC_VO strict priority and reserve a small bounded share under congestion. Next, add weights for designated device groups if supported, limiting the ratio to modest values like 2:1. The tradeoff of higher ratios is burstier latency for background clients during retries on 320 MHz. Document each change and timestamp it to correlate with telemetry. For broader optimization across policy and radio automation, compare these settings with the guidance in the optimization and policy best practices.

Extend configuration to rate and station limits that reinforce fairness behavior. If the controller supports per-client airtime caps, set a ceiling during congestion windows so a single downloading laptop cannot dominate. For systems without native airtime caps, set per-SSID max throughput values that approximate your goals during busy times. Include safeguards for roaming by enabling fast transitions that preserve queue state where supported. The limitation of throughput-based caps is imperfect correlation with airtime on changing MCS, especially across 320 MHz. Monitor if slow clients hit caps early due to retries and adjust ceilings accordingly. Keep a change ticket that notes intended effect and rollback trigger conditions.

Schedule these changes during a low-risk window, but test under realistic load to validate stability. Spin up a few TCP flows from laptops positioned at typical distances, plus a steady voice call generated by a softphone. Watch busy time and queue drops while weights take effect. If the voice stream shows rising jitter past your target, tighten the voice cap or adjust queue minimums rather than boosting weight ratios. Validate results

Validate with both counters and live traffic so fairness is proven, not assumed. Compare retry rates, queue drops, and client distribution before and after changes over the same timeframe. For a concrete test, run one sustained UDP voice flow and two to four TCP downloads during peak hours. Check that jitter remains within your threshold and total throughput drops minimally compared to baseline. Edge cases appear when puncturing reduces usable spectrum on 320 MHz, inflating retries and hiding fairness gains. In that case, fairness is functioning but channel quality masks benefits. Note both telemetry and user experience to judge success.

Examine per-client airtime share to confirm the policy matches intent. If your platform exposes airtime percentages, verify that priority endpoints stay near the target ratio during congestion windows. For equal-share deployments, expect distribution to converge within a narrow range across active clients. Outliers may signal sticky clients on distant APs or devices with faulty traffic markings. The limitation is that not every controller measures airtime identically, so treat exact numbers as directional. Look for consistent trends across multiple intervals rather than single snapshots and confirm voice flows retain low jitter during concurrent downloads.

Run scenario-based tests that match real work. For example, execute a fifteen-minute videoconference while three users download large files and one endpoint syncs backups. Observe MCS spread, retries, and voice jitter. If the videoconference remains stable and total throughput stays within ten percent of baseline, your fairness configuration is likely sound. If you see jitter spikes during file bursts, reduce weights rather than raising voice priority beyond the bounded cap. Troubleshoot and optimize

Diagnose mismatches between intent and outcome by isolating the variable causing unfairness. If high retries correlate with wide-channel use, try modestly reducing channel width or enabling channel puncturing where supported to avoid noisy subchannels. This is a temporary step to confirm that RF conditions, not weights, are the culprit. Another lever is client steering to spread load across adjacent APs so equal-share policies have room to work. The tradeoff is potential roam events that can briefly affect latency. Use small adjustments and measure each change to avoid compounding effects that conceal the real issue.

When priority traffic disturbs bulk transfers, optimize queue behavior rather than inflating weight ratios. Tighten the bounded cap on AC_VO and verify that queue starvation does not occur for best-effort traffic. If your platform supports explicit congestion notification on WiFi transport stacks, enable it for large file flows to smooth bursts through buffers. The limitation is variability in client support, so treat ECN as an enhancement rather than a dependency. If queue drops persist, reduce the number of simultaneous parallel downloads in test harnesses to reflect realistic user behavior and retest results before changing weights again.

Refine profiles over time using scheduled reports from the controller. Track weekly medians for jitter, retries, and per-client airtime to decide if adjustments are warranted. If results drift, reassess client mix and role definitions rather than chasing noise with micro-tweaks. Frequently asked questions

Will equal-share airtime slow high-end clients too much?

Equal-share may cap peak throughput for fast clients during congestion, but it improves consistency. If needed, add a gentle weight for essential devices rather than abandoning fairness entirely.

How do I know my weight ratio is too aggressive?

Watch jitter and retries on background clients during mixed tests. If latency spikes or retry bursts appear when priority devices transmit, reduce the ratio or tighten priority queue caps.

Does airtime fairness fix poor RF on 320 MHz?

No. Fairness allocates time but cannot overcome noise or interference. If retries remain high, consider puncturing, adjusting channel width, or improving placement before tweaking weights.

Should I prioritize video the same as voice?

Usually no. Give voice stricter priority with a bounded cap and place video in a high queue with limits. This protects conversational latency while keeping long streams from dominating airtime.
