Mesh Backhaul with 320 MHz Best Practices

By Rachel Morgan • Published January 24, 2026

Goal oriented configuration of mesh backhaul on 320 MHz starts with spectrum reality and finishes with measurements. This guide assumes two or more WiFi 8 nodes, power over Ethernet or mains nearby, and safe rooftop or ceiling mounting that preserves line of sight. You will plan channels, align radios, and confirm firmware features before changing width or power. The outcome is a stable high capacity backhaul that handles bursts without collapsing when clients roam or links retune. Along the way, we will reference backhaul alignment, best practices for channel width, and verification techniques. Expect tradeoffs where 160 MHz may win in noisy environments or with mixed clients. The process works in homes with short spans, but it becomes essential on longer runs between buildings.

Key takeaways

  • Lock backhaul to one clean 6 GHz channel and fixed width.
  • Use moderate EIRP and aligned antennas to stabilize 320 MHz links.
  • Validate with iperf3, RSSI, and retry counters under steady load.
  • Fallback to 160 MHz if SNR dips or duty cycle spikes persist.
  • Firmware must expose EHT settings and support DFS behavior correctly.

Plan the work

Start by defining success for 320 MHz mesh backhaul using measurable targets that align with your environment. A practical approach sets a minimum signal to noise ratio of roughly 28 dB at both nodes under daytime conditions, because wider channels raise noise floor and need extra margin. Walk test with a spectrum scan or a quiet time measurement to see duty cycle patterns, then map likely interference windows. If building penetration exists, prefer rooftop or window mounting to preserve line of sight and reduce multipath. The tradeoff is exposure to weather and cable runs, so plan weatherproofing and surge protection. Set a fallback goal for 160 MHz when SNR drops or DFS events force retunes, so you can maintain stability instead of chasing peak numbers that only appear late at night.

Define topology and hop count before touching settings, because each hop halves effective capacity under simple store and forward behavior. Favor a star layout where the primary node has clear, short links to each remote node rather than chaining many points in a line. In scenarios with obstructions, set one directional hop as the preferred backhaul and deprioritize alternate paths to avoid oscillation. The limitation is that automatic path selection can override preferences if metrics swing, so lock metrics where possible. Document cable lengths, PoE budgets, and mounting heights to prevent surprises the day of installation. This clarity lets you size your target throughput and placement, making 320 MHz practical without guessing whether a wide channel is truly beneficial for your span.

Choose channels with an eye on regional rules and device capabilities to avoid dead ends during deployment. In 6 GHz, channel availability and power limits vary by country and by class of device, and some nodes enforce automatic frequency coordination or indoor only modes. When wide lanes look attractive, confirm both radios support the same 320 MHz segment and that firmware exposes EHT configuration explicitly. If your clients or operating systems lag, hold 320 MHz for dedicated backhaul only and keep service set identifiers on narrower widths to conserve airtime. Prepare environment

Confirm device capabilities and software readiness so 320 MHz does not fail silently at runtime. Check silicon families, radio chains, and release notes for explicit EHT support and backhaul profiles, then stage a firmware known to expose channel width controls. If your nodes differ by chipset, match the lowest common backhaul feature set to avoid asymmetric behavior that breaks mesh metrics. Maintain a configuration backup for each node, and label a rollback plan if a build degrades stability. Validate physical path viability to justify a wide channel by assessing line of sight and Fresnel clearance along the intended beam. A simple check uses binoculars or a drone photo to find consistent obstructions like tree limbs that sway and cause intermittent fades. If aligned directional antennas are available, set both nodes to the lowest power that maintains at least mid 30s RSSI to hold SNR headroom. The tradeoff is reduced near field coverage, which is acceptable because these radios serve backhaul rather than clients. Secure mounts against drift, mark compass bearings, and note cable strain relief. This groundwork tightens link budgets so 320 MHz has the necessary margin to avoid rate flapping when weather shifts or nearby networks wake up.

Prepare channel plans that isolate the backhaul from client service sets to prevent hidden airtime contention. Assign a dedicated SSID or hidden service for the mesh link and pin it to a specific 6 GHz channel segment that both radios can hold. Disable auto channel for the backhaul to avoid retunes during busy hours, and favor auto only for access service sets if needed. Execute steps

Move from planning to action by applying changes in a reversible sequence. Begin on the root node so downstream devices follow a stable parent and do not cascade retunes while you test. Lock channel and width first, then set transmit power conservatively, and only after that adjust antenna orientation. This order minimizes disruptions and makes failures obvious to attribute. Avoid enabling auto optimization features during setup because they can mask poor choices by moving channels mid test. When possible, schedule work early morning when nearby networks are quieter, then repeat at peak to confirm worst case behavior. The intent is a tight loop where each step yields a measurable change rather than a bundle of edits that blur cause and effect.

Define clear thresholds that govern whether 320 MHz remains justified while you tune. Use a short baseline of RSSI, SNR, and retry rates with a constant low bandwidth stream so you can see stability rather than raw peak throughput. If retries remain elevated or MCS oscillates, reduce power slightly and refine antenna alignment before downgrading width. Only pivot to 160 MHz when SNR cannot stay above your target or when duty cycle climbs enough to cause latency spikes. This tiered approach preserves capacity where the path allows it yet avoids stubbornly holding a wide lane in marginal conditions. It also prevents reactive changes when a single burst looks slow due to transient external interference.

Before running validation commands, align both nodes physically and apply the same beacon and guard interval settings to avoid odd interactions. Matching beacon intervals keeps control overhead predictable across the link, while consistent guard intervals help MCS selection stay stable. If your platform supports multi link operation, disable it during initial tuning so you measure the backhaul path cleanly instead of cross link effects. Note that some systems couple client service and backhaul defaults, so verify backhaul SSID isolation after each change. With alignment and basic consistency in place, you are ready to configure.

Validate results

Validate the backhaul by measuring sustained throughput and stability instead of chasing a single peak speed test. Start with a five to ten minute iperf3 run at a fixed load that is below maximum capacity so rate adaptation has room. Track RSSI, SNR, MCS, and retry counts during the run and record averages plus the worst one second interval. A stable link holds MCS near its upper range and shows retries that do not trend upward across the interval. If duty cycle or noise spikes appear regularly, repeat measurements at another time of day to confirm patterns. The reason for sustained tests is simple. Wide channels can burst quickly yet collapse under steady flow if headroom is thin.

Use a simple two command setup to keep test mechanics reliable and repeatable across sites. Place an iperf3 server on the remote node and drive traffic from the root node across the backhaul interface. Run both upstream and downstream tests because direction can change rate algorithms and power behavior. Capture text output and store it with timestamps so you can compare to later changes. When you have a known good baseline, alter one setting at a time or change width to 160 MHz and rerun. This controlled approach helps you choose the best width without speculation. Troubleshoot and optimize

When results fall short, isolate the largest constraint with a small set of reversible tests. If throughput swings or latency spikes appear during steady iperf3 runs, inspect retry counters and noise floor first. Retries that trend upward often point to alignment or power that is too high, which inflates interference footprints. Reduce transmit power one or two dB and repeat alignment before sacrificing width. If the link is stable overnight but degrades during business hours, duty cycle is the likely pressure. In that case, shift to another 6 GHz segment or step down to 160 MHz and retest. Document each change and leave time gaps so rate control can settle, otherwise you may confuse transient recovery with improvement.

Optimize for long term behavior by balancing coverage and isolation across your property. Consider physical shielding like small rooftop parapets to block nearby indoor networks that share partial line of sight while keeping your path unobstructed. If a single hop remains marginal, try lifting the remote node by one or two meters to clear Fresnel zones, then re align antennas. Evaluate MLO later, after the backhaul is stable on one link, so you understand how multipath strategies affect metrics. The theme is to add complexity only when baseline behavior is repeatable. By staging enhancements carefully, you avoid time consuming blame shifts between features that interact in subtle ways.

Revisit client service once the backhaul is proven, because mixed WiFi generations can distort backhaul load unexpectedly. Keep client facing SSIDs on 80 or 160 MHz to conserve airtime and leave the 320 MHz path for backhaul. If service SSIDs share the same node radios, ensure airtime fairness and queue management are configured to prevent saturation at peak. Frequently asked questions

Should the backhaul SSID be hidden or broadcast?

Hidden is fine and reduces accidental client associations. Stability matters more than visibility, so ensure authentication and isolation are configured correctly. Keep a record of settings for recovery during maintenance windows.

What if a DFS event forces the link to move channels?

Expect a brief outage while the radios vacate and scan. If events are frequent during business hours, select a channel segment with fewer radar hits or step down to 160 MHz to regain stability with lower sensitivity to noise.

How much transmit power is too much for 320 MHz?

Use the lowest power that maintains your SNR target during busy periods. Excess power increases interference footprints and can raise retries. If SNR is marginal even at good alignment, consider 160 MHz instead of raising power.

Do I need multi link operation to benefit from 320 MHz?

No. Stabilize a single wide link first, then evaluate MLO if the platform supports it. Adding MLO complicates rate control and masking effects can hide underlying alignment or channel issues you should address first.

Can client traffic share the same radio as backhaul?

It can, but separation is safer. Use a dedicated backhaul SSID and keep client SSIDs narrower. Configure airtime fairness and queueing to prevent access traffic from starving the transport path during peak loads.
