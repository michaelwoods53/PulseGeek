UNII-5 to UNII-8 320 MHz Channel Mapping

By Rachel Morgan • Published January 14, 2026

Planning WiFi 8 in 6 GHz requires precise understanding of UNII‑5 through UNII‑8 and how they combine into 320 MHz channels. Teams often assume any wide lane will fit, yet contiguous bandwidth and puncturing constraints decide what is practical. This article maps spectrum segments and shows where 320 MHz lanes align cleanly, where they require surgical puncturing, and where regulations reshape choices for indoor or outdoor use.

Key takeaways

  • Contiguous 320 MHz lanes span multiple UNII ranges and must avoid edges.
  • Preamble puncturing salvages airtime when narrow interferers overlap subchannels.
  • Indoor and outdoor rules constrain which 6 GHz blocks are selectable.
  • MLO reduces risk by steering flows to cleaner partner links.
  • Prioritize reuse plans that limit co‑channel overlap at 320 MHz.

Concepts and definitions

Channel mapping starts with definitions. UNII‑5 through UNII‑8 are contiguous 6 GHz spectrum blocks segmented for policy and coexistence, and a 320 MHz channel is eight adjacent 40 MHz subchannels or sixteen 20 MHz units. The useful rule is simple: a viable 320 MHz lane must remain contiguous inside allowed ranges, and center frequency should not straddle restricted edges. For example, when a portion of a UNII block is unavailable in a region, the wide lane must either shift its center or use puncturing to sidestep the gap. The tradeoff is that shifting can pressure reuse plans, while puncturing reduces the effective width but may preserve stability when interference is localized to a few subcarriers.

Regulatory status shapes options. Indoor Low Power allowances span much of UNII‑5 to UNII‑8 in several markets, while outdoor or higher power may require Automated Frequency Coordination where available. The key mechanism is that availability can be asymmetric across buildings or campuses, so a 320 MHz lane chosen for one floor may not be legal or optimal outside. Teams should record which UNII segments are permitted by environment and power class, then mark edges that force guard decisions. The limitation is that those permissions evolve over time, so designs should include a fallback to narrower 160 MHz options when new incumbents emerge or when certain channels are withdrawn pending coordination.

Client capability closes the loop. Many early WiFi 8 devices advertise 320 MHz support, yet mixed estates include WiFi 6E and WiFi 6 clients that top out at 160 MHz or 80 MHz. The design goal is to provide at least one clean 320 MHz lane for capable devices without starving legacy clients of efficient lanes. One practical lens is to reserve a contiguous 320 MHz channel in the quietest portion of UNII‑6 or UNII‑7 while maintaining additional 160 MHz capacity elsewhere. The downside is AP density may rise to sustain SNR at higher MCS across wider bandwidths, raising cost. Multi‑Link Operation can mitigate by steering flows to a partner link that remains clear when the 320 MHz lane is busy.

Frameworks and decision lenses

A structured selection framework reduces guesswork. First choose a primary contiguous window that minimizes overlap with noisy incumbents and adjacent edges, then evaluate whether one 320 MHz lane plus one or two 160 MHz lanes best balances throughput and reuse. A practical rule is to prefer the center of UNII‑6 and UNII‑7 for the wide lane when permitted, since mid band often sees fewer building services interferers. The tradeoff is that crowding may increase where neighboring tenants follow the same logic, so a site survey should validate noise floors. For readers planning multiple floors, stagger primary channels vertically to reduce co‑channel coupling through slabs and open atria.

Use puncturing intentionally rather than by default. Preamble puncturing can remove a few contaminated 20 MHz subchannels and keep the rest of the 320 MHz lane usable. This works best when interference is narrowband or localized, such as a small segment affected by a neighboring WLAN. The mechanism preserves higher PHY rates across the remaining subcarriers, but it also reduces aggregate capacity and complicates scheduling for mixed clients. As a guardrail, avoid puncturing more than a small fraction of the lane on a sustained basis. If more than a quarter of the lane is often excluded, shifting the center frequency or stepping down to 160 MHz will usually produce steadier airtime.

Plan reuse like a spectrum budget. At 320 MHz width, co‑channel overlap becomes a dominant limiter, so allocate lanes to minimize same‑channel hearing between adjacent cells. A thumb rule is to target at least one wall or 15 to 20 meters of attenuation between like channels in office layouts, adjusting for construction density. When that spacing is infeasible, reduce power or tilt toward directional antennas to shrink contention domains. Multi‑Link Operation can carry latency sensitive flows over a partner 160 MHz link when the 320 MHz path is congested. The limitation is that both links still share the 6 GHz band, so improvements rely on diversity in interference patterns rather than pure additional spectrum.

Pitfalls, limitations, and edge cases

A frequent mistake is mapping a 320 MHz lane that straddles a regulatory edge, then relying on the controller to resolve conflicts. This can produce unexpected downgrades, like forced narrowing during busy periods that look like random capacity drops. The safer approach is to precompute which center frequencies remain legal across all target areas and environments, then commit to those lanes in templates. The tradeoff is reduced flexibility when using a single policy across diverse sites. Where buildings differ, keep a small catalog of approved profiles. One profile can anchor in mid UNII‑6 for indoor offices, while another reserves UNII‑8 capacity when AFC is active. A separate pattern can retain 160 MHz primaries for legacy heavy wings.

Another pitfall is overusing transmit power to compensate for wide channel SNR demands. Wider bandwidth raises the noise floor, so holding high MCS across 320 MHz typically requires shorter cell radii and more APs. If cells are too large, contention grows and clients back off to lower rates, negating the goal. The better path is to right‑size power and consider more AP density or directional patterns. Where clients are mixed, a hybrid design keeps a clean 320 MHz lane for WiFi 8 while distributing 160 MHz lanes for older devices. Edge cases appear when neighboring tenants adopt similar wide lanes. Even if your design is clean, external co‑channel overlap can degrade airtime. The mitigation is coordination where possible, but also maintaining a fallback band plan that can step down the 320 MHz lane or shift the center. Multi‑Link Operation becomes valuable here since it can select a partner link that experiences a different interference pattern. The limitation is that MLO needs compatible clients and well tuned policy to avoid oscillation. When uncertainty remains, keep clearly documented change windows and monitoring thresholds so shifts are deliberate rather than reactive. Looking ahead

Future‑proof mapping means treating 320 MHz selection as a living document rather than a one‑time choice. Start by cataloging permissible UNII ranges for each site type and power class, then list approved centers that maintain contiguity with guard. Schedule periodic reviews to reflect regulatory updates and observed interference patterns. The advantage is faster, safer changes when new devices arrive or neighboring deployments shift behavior. Where environments are heterogeneous, keep two to three tested profiles per region to avoid ad hoc mapping. A stable workflow reduces misconfiguration risk and helps teams communicate expectations around SNR, reuse, and client behavior across operations and design groups.

Validation should be routine. Use spectrum snapshots during peak and off‑peak periods to confirm that your 320 MHz lane remains contiguous and that any puncturing is sparing and temporary. Frequently asked questions

Do I need contiguous spectrum to use a 320 MHz channel?

Yes. A 320 MHz channel requires a contiguous run of subchannels. Preamble puncturing can remove small contaminated slices, but if exclusions become large or frequent, shifting the center or using 160 MHz is usually better.

When should I prefer puncturing over moving the center frequency?

Prefer puncturing when the interference is narrowband and intermittent. If more than a small fraction of the lane is excluded often, a center shift or stepping down in width will provide steadier airtime and simpler scheduling.

How does Multi Link Operation help wide channel reliability?

MLO lets clients use an alternate link that may face different interference. When the 320 MHz lane is busy or partially punctured, the partner link can carry sensitive flows, reducing retries and smoothing latency for capable devices.

Can I rely on higher transmit power to offset wider bandwidth?

Not reliably. Wider bandwidth raises the noise floor, so maintaining high MCS usually needs smaller cells and more APs. Overusing power enlarges contention domains and can reduce throughput due to increased co channel overlap.
