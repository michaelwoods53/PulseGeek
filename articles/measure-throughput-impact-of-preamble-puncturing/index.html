Measure Throughput Impact of Preamble Puncturing

By Michael Carter • Published February 7, 2026

Our goal is to measure the throughput impact of preamble puncturing with a workflow that stands up to scrutiny. You will plan controlled scenarios, run repeatable tests, and validate that puncturing actually occurred during the measurement window. We assume access to a Wi‑Fi 8 capable access point and at least one client, basic familiarity with iperf3, and a protocol or spectrum analyzer for cross checks.

Key takeaways

  • Define test intent before touching knobs and lock variables early.
  • Verify puncturing events occurred while throughput sampling was active.
  • Use matched TCP and UDP trials to isolate rate and loss.
  • Record channelization and RU use alongside throughput for context.
  • Repeat trials and report median with spread, not single peaks.

Plan the work

Start by declaring the measurement objective in one sentence, because a precise scope prevents false comparisons. For example, aim to quantify mean downlink TCP throughput change when preamble puncturing disables a 20 MHz sub‑channel in a 160 MHz allocation. Write down environment factors you will hold constant, like distance, antenna orientation, client power, and traffic pattern. A rule of thumb is to freeze at least five high‑impact variables that most affect rate selection and modulation stability. The tradeoff is less realism, but tighter control explains variance sources. This structured intent ensures you can attribute any throughput delta to puncturing rather than hidden changes, which is essential when sharing results with peers or operations teams.

Next, define success metrics and acceptable uncertainty so your conclusions remain useful under noise. Choose median throughput across at least five trials as the primary metric and include interquartile range to capture spread. Add a secondary metric such as UDP goodput with packet loss to spot saturation effects that TCP hides. The limitation is longer test time, yet paired metrics reveal whether observed drops come from rate fallback, contention, or frame loss. By setting thresholds upfront, like requiring at least a 10 percent shift beyond noise bands to claim impact, you preempt overfitting and avoid cherry‑picking favorable runs.

Finally, plan evidence to confirm puncturing truly occurred during each throughput measurement. Decide which tools will show punctured subcarriers or event logs, and what constitutes proof. A spectrum analyzer can visualize the missing sub‑channel energy, while a protocol analyzer flags puncturing signaling. When tooling varies, document equivalence, such as matching event counters or PHY headers. Include a timeline noting when you enable or trigger interference that leads to puncturing, and when the throughput tool samples. The subtle risk is mistaking unrelated interference for puncturing. Cross‑checking modalities reduces misinterpretation and strengthens the causal link to your throughput results.

Prepare environment

Anchor your testbed on a wired iperf3 server and an isolated Wi‑Fi 8 SSID to reduce noise. Place the access point on a stable channel and width that supports puncturing and disable background services like load balancing that could alter rate control. For the client, ensure a driver that exposes Wi‑Fi 8 features and confirm consistent power management settings. A practical example is running the server on a gigabit wired host with CPU headroom, because server limits can bottleneck rather than radio behavior. The compromise is less representative of production, yet isolation removes confounders. This setup yields cleaner throughput baselines and makes puncturing effects easier to attribute.

Calibrate radio geometry so small movements do not dominate results. Fix the client position using tape marks and maintain a known orientation to the access point. Measure baseline received signal strength and note a target range to hold during all trials, aiming for consistency within a few dB. If your environment is reflective or crowded, use absorbers or select off hours to minimize unpredictable multipath. The tradeoff is extra preparation time, but it avoids misleading rate shifts. By protecting the RF path from drift, you keep modulation and coding scheme transitions from obscuring puncturing’s actual throughput impact.

Stage your analyzers and define what each will confirm. A spectrum view should display the nominal channel mask and the notched segment when puncturing activates. A protocol analyzer should record the signaling that accompanies the decision to puncture. Execute steps

Use a paired baseline and punctured sequence so differences map cleanly to the feature. Begin by running downlink tests with the client as iperf3 receiver, then mirror with uplink if needed. Set test duration long enough for rate selection to settle, often 20 to 60 seconds per run. Label each run with timestamps so you can align captures. Choose TCP for steady throughput assessment and add UDP to see loss behavior as offered rate varies. The tradeoff is doubling test permutations, but it prevents misreading TCP smoothing as genuine radio stability. This structured pairing builds a trustworthy comparison set.

Before running commands, decide on flags that ensure repeatability and sufficient detail. For TCP, pin parallel streams and buffer size to avoid client defaults shifting across runs. For UDP, sweep a small set of offered rates around expected capacity, such as 400, 600, and 800 Mbps, to profile loss and jitter bands. Validate results

Confirm that puncturing occurred during each throughput sample, not before or after. Align iperf3 log timestamps with analyzer captures, looking for the missing sub‑channel in spectrum or puncturing indicators in protocol traces. A fast check is to overlay time windows and ensure features co‑exist. If evidence is inconsistent, reject that run rather than rationalizing it. The limitation is fewer usable samples, but quality beats quantity when drawing conclusions. By insisting on co‑timed proof, you avoid attributing throughput swings to unrelated interference or scheduler quirks, which preserves the integrity of your reported impact.

Summarize results with central tendency and spread so readers understand variability. Compute median throughput for baseline and punctured sets separately, then express absolute and percentage change with interquartile ranges. For UDP, report goodput at each offered rate along with loss and jitter to capture saturation shape. Avoid cherry‑picking peak runs because they overweight lucky fades. An effective presentation is a small table with baseline versus punctured medians and a concise percent delta. The tradeoff is less dramatic storytelling, but decision makers prefer honest ranges. Clear statistics make it easier to compare sites, device models, and configuration tweaks over time.

Interpret causality by checking for confounders that mimic puncturing effects. Review rate control logs if available and note MCS transitions that could explain throughput changes. Examine RU allocation stability and channel utilization from analyzers to ensure the scheduler did not shift behavior between runs. When confidence is low, repeat trials or tighten controls rather than forcing conclusions. Troubleshoot and optimize

When puncturing does not appear, investigate triggering conditions and thresholds. Verify the access point channelization supports puncturing on your chosen width and confirm client capabilities. Introduce or reposition controlled interference to the target sub‑channel if your setup uses an interferer. Watch the analyzer for the expected notch and rerun a short probe. Edge cases include dynamic spectrum decisions that disable puncturing under certain regulatory or load constraints. If conditions are borderline, widen your run count and filter by runs that show strong evidence. This approach avoids false negatives while acknowledging that activation criteria may vary by implementation.

If throughput results vary excessively, reduce sources of randomness before retesting. Shorten the physical path by moving a bit closer to improve SNR stability and reduce MCS flapping. Disable background network traffic on the LAN and ensure CPUs on both endpoints are not pegged by unrelated tasks. Consider fixing the number of spatial streams and guard interval if your gear exposes those controls. The tradeoff is less real‑world fidelity, yet your goal is to isolate the puncturing variable. Stabilizing foundational factors gives your comparisons sharper resolution and makes differences attributable rather than speculative.

When results still feel ambiguous, triangulate with additional views that speak to the same phenomenon. A visual mask comparison before and after puncturing helps confirm the RF change, and a protocol trace can show event reliability. Frequently asked questions

How long should each iperf3 test run to get stable throughput?

Run 20 to 60 seconds per trial so rate control converges and short fades average out. Keep the duration identical across baseline and punctured runs to maintain a fair comparison.

What if I cannot reliably trigger preamble puncturing during tests?

Verify channel width and client support, then adjust the interference source that targets the sub channel. If triggering remains unstable, increase run count and only keep samples with clear analyzer evidence.

Should I test TCP or UDP when measuring impact?

Use both. TCP shows sustained throughput under congestion control, while UDP reveals loss thresholds and jitter at specific offered rates. Paired views help explain why throughput changed.

How many trials are needed to trust the percentage change?

Aim for at least five usable runs per condition, then report the median and spread. More trials narrow uncertainty, but quality evidence of puncturing during each sample matters most.

Could CPU limits on endpoints distort my results?

Yes. Ensure the iperf3 server is on wired infrastructure with headroom and verify the client CPU is not saturated. If needed, reduce parallel streams or offered rate to avoid local bottlenecks.
