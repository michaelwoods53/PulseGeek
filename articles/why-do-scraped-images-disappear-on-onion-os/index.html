Why Do Scraped Images Disappear on Onion OS?

By Sarah Carter • Published January 16, 2026

Yes, scraped images can disappear on Onion OS when caches reset, filenames diverge from ROM entries, or storage becomes unavailable. Most losses trace to predictable triggers like updates, SD card hiccups, or scraper mismatches rather than permanent deletion. This article explains quick checks for cache behavior, file naming, and storage state, then expands into safe validation and durable practices. You will learn how to reproduce the issue in a controlled way, confirm root cause, and recover artwork without risky rescans or breaking playlists.

Key takeaways

  • Most disappearing images trace to cache resets or mismatched filenames.
  • Validate fixes by testing one system and a few ROMs first.
  • Keep storage stable and writable to prevent silent cache rebuild failures.
  • Prefer predictable naming and known-safe scraper settings for Onion OS.
  • Back up playlists and images before major updates or wide changes.

Short answer and nuance

The most common reason artwork disappears is a cache refresh that no longer maps to your files. A theme or frontend may rebuild thumbnails after updates, but if ROM filenames changed or paths moved, the cache points to nothing and images seem gone. As a quick example, renaming a ROM from Game A.zip to Game A USA.zip can orphan an image that still uses the old base name. This mismatch does not delete the picture; it only breaks the link. The tradeoff is that automatic cleanup keeps libraries tidy, but aggressive rules can over-prune entries. Understanding where your frontend stores thumbnails and how it matches filenames helps you prevent surprises.

A second driver is storage volatility that makes caches unavailable at boot. If the SD card mounts read only or a secondary partition fails to mount, the frontend cannot access existing images and may create fresh empty cache folders. You might even see zero byte thumbnail files after a failed write. A conservative rule is to confirm free space and write permissions before rescanning anything. The downside is that strict checks slow you down during quick tweaks, yet they avoid compounding corruption. Stable storage and consistent mount points are prerequisites for reliable artwork display across sessions.

Finally, scraper configuration affects visibility more than expected. Mixed regions, inconsistent extensions, or duplicate titles can produce mismatched records where the scraper names files differently than your ROMs. Consider a scenario where your scraper uses Title - Year while your ROM set uses Title Year. The display logic may rely on exact name matching, so one subtle character change breaks links. The benefit of standardized naming is predictable results, but it limits flexibility for personal naming styles. Establish a single naming convention early, then adjust scraper templates to conform rather than retrofitting later.

When it applies vs when it does not

This guidance applies when artwork was previously visible and then vanished after an update, theme change, scraper run, or a batch rename. A telltale sign is that some systems still show images while others do not, which points to naming or cache scope rather than a global failure. For instance, PlayStation assets might render while Game Boy assets disappear after you moved that handheld’s ROM folder. The nuance is that visibility can fail without data loss. The files often remain on disk but are no longer referenced. Verify by browsing the images directory directly before trying any rescrape that could overwrite work.

It does not fully apply if images never appeared in the first place. In that case the root cause likely lives earlier in your setup, such as missing BIOS, incorrect folder hierarchy, or unsupported extensions that prevent indexing. How to implement or validate

Start with a targeted test to avoid breaking the whole library. Pick one system and three ROMs. Ensure storage is writable, then clear only the thumbnails for that system and rescan those titles. If images return, the cache was stale. If not, inspect filenames: the base name before the extension should match the image name exactly, ignoring case rules your frontend uses. As a rule of thumb, keep extensions consistent and avoid punctuation that differs between scraper and ROM set. The tradeoff is time spent auditing a sample before mass changes, but this greatly reduces the risk of erasing good assets with an unnecessary full rescrape.

Alternatives and related questions

If scraping repeatedly breaks, consider artwork packs or manual curation for key systems. Packs trade freshness for stability, giving you known good names and formats that rarely shift during updates. This approach works well when your ROM set is stable and matched to a specific naming scheme. The limitation is that you must keep packs aligned with ROM renames you introduce later. A hybrid method is effective: keep a pack as baseline, then selectively rescrape for new additions. When choosing, ask whether your library changes weekly or quarterly, since stable libraries benefit from prebuilt assets while fast changing sets need adaptable scrapers.

Another alternative is to decouple visible names from filename matching. Some frontends support playlists where a unique identifier links ROM entries to assets without relying entirely on literal filenames. When available, this reduces the risk that a small rename breaks images. The downside is extra maintenance and potential lock in to a specific frontend. If you prefer this route, document the identifier format you use and export playlists regularly. Also consider a controlled test where you rename one ROM while retaining the identifier to verify that images persist across the change.

Related questions often surface after fixing visibility, such as why thumbnails are slow to load or appear pixelated. Performance issues usually involve image dimensions or heavy themes, which you can audit by sampling a few large images and recording load time improvements after resizing. Quality problems point to aggressive compression or scaled sources. Decide on a target width that your theme actually displays and standardize resampling to that size. If symptoms include missing metadata like descriptions or release dates, refine scraper rules rather than rescraping blindly, and keep a text export so edits do not vanish with the next refresh.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to rescrape everything if images vanish?

No. Test a small system first, rebuild that cache, and verify filename matches. If the sample works, apply the fix to the rest. Only rescrape broadly when names or sources are beyond repair.

Why do images show while scraping but disappear after reboot?

This often indicates remote links or a cache stored on unavailable storage at boot. Save images locally, ensure the SD card mounts read write, and confirm the cache directory persists across restarts.

How can I check if filenames cause the mismatch?

List ROM basenames and compare them to image basenames. If titles differ by punctuation or region tags, standardize one scheme and batch rename carefully. A small script can report missing or orphaned pairs.

Will theme changes remove my artwork files?

Theme changes usually rebuild or reference different cache paths but they rarely delete source images. If art disappears after a theme switch, confirm the new cache location and refresh thumbnails for one system.

What storage issues cause artwork to vanish unexpectedly?

Insufficient free space, read only mounts, or sudden power loss can interrupt cache writes and leave empty files. Check space and permissions before rescans, and rebuild only the affected cache directories.

Looking ahead

Stability comes from predictable naming, durable local caches, and measured changes. Keep a small test set for rapid validation, document your scraper rules alongside the theme you use, and export playlists or image lists before big updates. When something goes wrong, reproduce the symptom with three games and one system, then fix only what the test proves is broken. Over time, a short routine of space checks, writable mounts, and a quick mismatch scan will prevent disappearing images from resurfacing and keep your Onion OS library consistent.
