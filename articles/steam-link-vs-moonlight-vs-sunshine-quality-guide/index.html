Steam Link vs Moonlight vs Sunshine: Quality Guide

By Zachary Hill • Published January 26, 2026

Choosing between Steam Link, Moonlight, and Sunshine hinges on streaming quality under real constraints. We will evaluate how codec choices, bitrate control, and HDR handling shape clarity while keeping latency low and inputs responsive. The lens is practical: identify the strongest path for your hardware and displays, then test small tweaks before reaching for bigger changes. Steam Link integrates tightly with the Steam client, Moonlight speaks NVIDIA GameStream protocol, and Sunshine is an open server that pairs with Moonlight. The right fit depends on what matters more to you, such as artifact reduction, lower latency, or better HDR tone mapping, and how your network behaves when bandwidth fluctuates during action scenes.

Key takeaways

  • Moonlight with Sunshine offers flexible codecs and robust bitrate shaping.
  • Steam Link is simplest on living room TVs with solid 60 FPS.
  • Prefer HEVC at 1080p to 1440p when Wi‑Fi is congested.
  • Use AV1 only if both GPU and client decode it reliably.
  • Lock a constant frame rate to steady latency and image quality.
  • Test HDR tone mapping on your display before committing to it.

Evaluation criteria and how to weigh them

Start by ranking what quality means for your setup, then attach measurable checks. If motion clarity matters most on a 120 Hz TV, prioritize codec efficiency and frame pacing before chasing peak resolution numbers. A reliable rule is to rate image stability first by counting macroblock artifacts during rapid pans, then confirm that input-to-photon delay stays consistent over a 60 second repeatable scene. Accept that wireless networks drift, so design around variance. For example, aim for a bitrate that sits 15 to 25 percent below your worst observed throughput to leave recovery headroom. This avoids oscillation where encoders overreach, drop frames, and smear detail during explosions or rain.

Next, separate codec choice from device decode limits to avoid false tradeoffs. AV1 is attractive for detail retention at lower bitrates, yet real gains appear only when both GPU encoding and client decoding are stable at your target frame rate. If your handheld struggles to sustain AV1 at 60 FPS, HEVC at a moderate bitrate often looks cleaner because it avoids stutter and late frames. When Wi‑Fi congestion spikes, prefer constant frame rate with capped bitrate over variable frame rate that can cause cadence judder. For households with multiple streams, shape traffic with router QoS to protect consistent latency rather than squeezing maximum resolution.

Finally, weigh HDR against SDR fidelity by testing tone mapping on your specific display. Poor tone mapping crushes shadows or clips highlights, making SDR at higher bitrate look better than mishandled HDR. Many PC-to-TV paths change color formats under load, especially when bandwidth bursts collide with Wi‑Fi retries. A practical approach is to test a 2 minute scene that mixes bright UI, skin tones, and dark backgrounds, then switch HDR on and off while keeping bitrate constant. If HDR introduces banding or raised blacks, stick to SDR and reinvest bandwidth into more stable detail. This choice often reduces encoder stress and sharpens texture edges during fast motion.

Side by side overview table with key attributes

At a glance, Steam Link, Moonlight, and Sunshine differ in how they encode video, expose controls, and recover from bandwidth dips. Steam Link favors simplicity with good defaults and solid 60 FPS paths that suit TVs and couch play. Moonlight acts as the client for NVIDIA GameStream and also pairs with Sunshine on AMD or Intel rigs. Sunshine, as the open server, brings wide control of codecs, bitrates, and input handling, which can be decisive on tricky networks. Before reading the table, note your GPU’s hardware encoders and your client’s decode capabilities so you do not chase options your device cannot sustain under load.

Use the table to match priorities. If you are chasing the lowest latency on wired Ethernet with an NVIDIA GPU, Moonlight with GameStream or Sunshine both deliver efficient encoding and predictable input response. If you stream to a smart TV or a set‑top box and value an easy path, Steam Link often wins because its defaults handle frame pacing well without manual tuning. For mixed Wi‑Fi apartments or mesh networks, Sunshine’s bitrate controls and Moonlight’s client options can adapt better to congestion, preserving readable edges during motion while avoiding dropped frames that make inputs feel mushy.

Treat HDR and high refresh as optional layers rather than default requirements. HDR is worth enabling when your display’s tone mapping does not introduce raised blacks, and high refresh helps only if the decode pipeline and network sustain it without jitter. If your client or route inserts buffers to hide jitter, the added latency can offset the benefit of higher frame rates. When in doubt, lock 60 FPS with a conservative bitrate and verify consistency before experimenting with headroom. Deep dives per attribute with tradeoffs and concrete examples

Codec choice determines how much detail survives motion at a given bitrate, but only when encode and decode stay real time. On modern GPUs, HEVC often retains cleaner texture edges than H.264 at the same bitrate, especially during foliage and particle effects. AV1 can beat both in efficiency when your client decodes it at the target frame rate without hiccups. A common trap is enabling AV1 on a handheld that drops frames under thermal or power constraints, which makes inputs feel sluggish despite prettier stills. Validate with a 60 second strafe-and-pan test, then inspect for smearing around moving objects. If you observe judder or late frames, fall back to HEVC and re-test before raising bitrate.

Bitrate control balances clarity and stability, and the best setting depends on your worst network moments. On Wi‑Fi, set a cap 15 to 25 percent under the slowest sustained throughput you measured in the room where you play. This avoids rate oscillations that cause brief sharpness followed by muddy blocks when retries spike. Sunshine exposes fine‑grained bitrate caps and quality targets that help keep motion consistent during congestion, while Steam Link’s simpler presets favor safe values to prevent stalls. Moonlight as a client lets you align buffer depth with your risk tolerance. For a broader blueprint to stabilize frame delivery and tune bitrate and codecs, consult our guide on cutting latency and balancing quality.

HDR handling can elevate highlights and gradients, but it is only an upgrade when tone mapping is correct across the entire path. Check whether your GPU, encoder, client app, and display agree on color format and limited versus full range. Mismatches can raise blacks or clip bright UI. To test, run a mixed scene with bright sky, skin tones, and deep shadows while toggling HDR. If you see banding or crushed detail, disable HDR and reclaim bandwidth for steady motion. Sunshine with Moonlight gives you more visibility into format and mapping settings, while Steam Link aims for simpler compatibility on common TVs. Choose SDR at a stable bitrate over flaky HDR that compromises readability.

Its defaults keep frame pacing steady and limit risky bitrate spikes that expose Wi‑Fi weaknesses. Start with HEVC at a moderate cap and SDR if your set’s tone mapping is unpredictable. If your TV supports 120 Hz but the client app buffers more to hide jitter, stay at 60 FPS to keep latency predictable. Pair a wired controller or low‑latency adapter when possible. Key terms

  • Client Station — A Wi‑Fi device that connects to an AP.
  • Frame Rate Cap — A set limit on frames per second to control performance and power.
  • Frames Per Second — The number of images shown each second in a game.
  • High Dynamic Range — A video feature that expands color and brightness for richer images.
  • Input Latency — Delay between pressing a control and seeing the result.
  • Network Latency — The time it takes for data to travel between your device and the game server.
  • Quality of Service — Network rules that prioritize or shape traffic to manage latency.
  • Refresh Rate — How often the screen updates per second.

Frequently asked questions

Does AV1 always look better than HEVC for streaming?

No. AV1 can look better at the same bitrate, but only if the encoder and client can process frames at your target rate without stutter. If decode struggles or thermals throttle, HEVC at a steady frame rate often produces a cleaner, more responsive result.

Should I enable HDR by default in Moonlight or Steam Link?

Only after testing tone mapping on your display. If toggling HDR introduces raised blacks, banding, or clipped highlights, keep SDR and spend bandwidth on a stable bitrate and frame pacing. Many setups benefit more from consistent SDR quality than imperfect HDR.

What bitrate should I start with on Wi‑Fi?

Measure sustained throughput where you play, then cap 15 to 25 percent below the worst value you observe. This leaves room for retries and prevents oscillation. Verify over a repeatable 60 second scene with fast motion to confirm that frames arrive on time.

Is 120 Hz streaming worth it on a TV?

Only if your client decodes at that rate and the network avoids jitter without deep buffering. Many TV apps add buffers to hide variability, which increases latency. A locked 60 FPS stream with tight pacing usually feels more consistent for most games.

When should I choose Sunshine over Steam Link?

Choose Sunshine when you need granular control of codec, bitrate, and buffering, or when your GPU is not served by GameStream but you still want Moonlight as a client. It suits challenging Wi‑Fi, mixed devices, and users who will tune settings.

Looking ahead

Move from theory to validation with a simple loop that exposes weak links. First, lock 60 FPS and a conservative bitrate, then run a 2 minute scene that mixes strafing, particle effects, and UI. If frames arrive on time and detail holds, raise bitrate in small steps and re-test. If you see microstutter or muddy blocks, drop bitrate or switch codecs before chasing higher resolutions. When AV1 decode proves solid, treat it as a late optimization. Revisit HDR only after motion is stable and color format negotiations are predictable. This progression narrows variables and lets Steam Link, Moonlight, or Sunshine show their best version on your actual devices and network.
