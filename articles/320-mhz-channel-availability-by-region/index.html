320 MHz Channel Availability by Region

By Rachel Morgan • Published February 27, 2026

Planning 320 MHz channel use depends on regional 6 GHz allocations, practical availability, and contiguous spectrum. This list weighs spectrum ranges, power classes, and coexistence so teams avoid assuming wide lanes exist everywhere. We prioritized regions with active unlicensed 6 GHz rules and focused on what governs real deployment, not just theoretical bandwidth.

Key takeaways

  • Contiguous 6 GHz spectrum largely determines whether a 320 MHz channel fits.
  • AFC and power classes shape feasibility more than peak PHY numbers.
  • Client support and MLO often outperform forcing wider channels alone.
  • Regional rules differ, so verify frequency ranges and indoor limits.
  • Use puncturing and RU planning when clean 320 MHz is not possible.

1) United States

United States availability is shaped by the full 6 GHz allocation and how cleanly 320 MHz can fit without overlapping incumbents. The claim is that wide lanes are possible in principle because unlicensed spans across 5925 to 7125 MHz enable multiple bonded groupings. For example, an indoor low-power deployment can use 80 or 160 today and consider a single 320 MHz lane where contention is controlled and channel edges remain clear of interference. The tradeoff is that standard power requires Automated Frequency Coordination, which may constrain choices dynamically and complicate deterministic planning for a single massive lane. Teams should also note that client radios must support the bandwidth and that real airtime can be dominated by mixed clients even if the access point advertises 320 MHz.

A practical approach in the US pairs spectrum hygiene with fallbacks such as preamble puncturing when a portion of the bonded group is busy. A mid-density venue might center designs on reliable 160 MHz and shift to 320 only in zones with light contention and strong signal-to-noise. If co-channel reuse is required across floors, narrower channels reduce adjacent-cell coupling and preserve spatial reuse. The mechanism is simple physics: a wider channel absorbs more noise and potential interferers, which can erase the headline throughput gain. A helpful background on wide-lane overhead and capacity math is available in this deep-dive on bonding, mapping, puncturing, and MLO efficiency.

  • Start with 160 MHz, then expand where interference remains minimal.

2) Canada

Canada’s allocation across the 6 GHz range suggests that 320 MHz channels are feasible where contiguous blocks remain clean and client capability aligns. The claim is conditional because planners must validate local interference, power rules, and building materials that attenuate signals and reduce external coupling. For instance, a corporate campus using indoor low power can model a single 320 MHz lane for demonstration areas while keeping production floors on 160 MHz to preserve reuse. The tradeoff is that wider lanes can dilute spectral efficiency when many clients operate at lower modulation and coding schemes, so the aggregate can underperform compared to two narrower lanes. Designers should stage pilot tests that compare per-user throughput and latency under realistic loads before promoting 320 MHz as a standard profile across sites.

Coexistence features like multi-link operation can stabilize throughput when one link is impacted by localized noise. A tactical design might bond a 320 MHz lane for headline traffic while enabling a second link on a narrower channel to handle latency-sensitive flows. This approach does not conjure spectrum where there is none, but it can keep sessions moving when part of the bonded block is punctured. 3) European Union

Across the European Union, current unlicensed rules focus on the lower 6 GHz portion, which constrains 320 MHz formation due to limited contiguous bandwidth. The claim is that planners should not expect a clean 320 MHz lane under prevailing allocations. A realistic example is an indoor office where 80 or 160 MHz channels provide healthier reuse and consistent modulation. Because the usable span is narrower, attempting a 320 MHz design would collide with band edges or regulatory boundaries. The tradeoff is clear. You gain reuse and predictable airtime with narrower lanes but lose the theoretical top-end rate. In practice, average client modulation, not the maximum channel width, drives application performance for mixed fleets. Documenting the specific national table remains essential, because exact frequency edges can vary by country within the EU framework.

Given these limits, investment should prioritize capacity per square meter using features like OFDMA resource unit planning and smart BSS coloring rather than chasing a single wide channel. A floor with many voice and collaboration users will usually benefit from multiple 80 MHz channels to keep latency bounded. If a portion of the spectrum is impacted by neighboring WLANs, preamble puncturing can salvage large chunks of an 80 or 160 MHz lane without wholesale channel changes. Key terms

  • Modulation and Coding Scheme Index — A number indicating modulation and coding level.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need AFC to use 320 MHz indoors?

Standard power commonly requires AFC while indoor low power may not, but rules vary by country. Check your regulator’s table and your access point’s power class to see whether coordination is needed for your target profile.

Can preamble puncturing create a 320 MHz channel?

No. Preamble puncturing can mute busy subchannels inside a bonded group, but it cannot manufacture additional contiguous spectrum. It preserves usable bandwidth when part of the channel is impacted by interference.

Is 160 MHz usually better than 320 MHz?

Often yes in shared environments. 160 MHz improves reuse and reduces interference sensitivity compared to 320 MHz, which needs very clean spectrum. Measure airtime, retries, and per-user throughput to decide for your site.

Do most clients support 320 MHz today?

Client support is still emerging and varies by chipset and firmware. Inventory real devices, test associations in the lab, and confirm that reported widths and negotiated rates match your design assumptions before rollout.

Will Multi-Link Operation replace the need for 320 MHz?

Not exactly. MLO can stabilize throughput by using multiple links, often across bands, but it does not add new spectrum. It complements wide channels by providing alternatives when one link faces interference.
