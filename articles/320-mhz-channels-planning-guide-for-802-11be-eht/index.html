320 MHz Channels Planning Guide for 802.11be EHT

By Courtney Lawson • Published February 14, 2026

Designing 320 MHz channels for 802.11be requires careful planning, especially when working within 6 GHz regulations and practical coexistence constraints. This guide provides a stepwise path from scoping to validation, with security‑first checks that reduce interference risks. Assumptions include enterprise or advanced residential deployments, Wi‑Fi 7 clients present, and tooling for spectrum observation. You will plan coverage and throughput targets, select safe preamble puncturing patterns, and confirm results with measurements rather than guesswork. The process avoids fragile settings, limits DFS surprises, and documents tradeoffs so stakeholders understand why a narrower channel or a different primary might be the safer option.

Key takeaways

  • Start with constraints, then size 320 MHz only where risk is low.
  • Use puncturing to bypass interference hotspots without collapsing throughput.
  • Pick a stable primary channel to protect RU scheduling efficiency.
  • Validate with spectrum, latency baselines, and controlled client mixes.
  • Document fallbacks to 160 or 80 MHz when DFS exposure grows.

Plan the work

Begin by fixing objectives and constraints so 320 MHz channels are justified rather than assumed. State target applications, like high‑density content delivery or short‑burst AR, and define latency and throughput ranges each must meet. For example, real‑time collaboration might accept 10 to 20 millisecond one‑way latency but require consistent mid‑MCS stability. List non‑negotiables such as building materials, AP density limits, and incumbent services. Identify where 6 GHz low power indoor and standard power rules apply, plus any DFS exposure. This framing reduces the urge to over‑widen channels and highlights rooms where 160 or 80 MHz provides safer headroom. Clarity up front shrinks redesign loops and anchors every later decision in measurable needs.

Next, map spectrum conditions and coexistence expectations at the site. A quick walk test with a 6 GHz‑capable analyzer reveals noise floors, intermittent spikes, and neighboring WLAN footprints. Capture a daytime and an evening sample to catch periodic interferers like wireless AV bridges. Use a simple rubric to classify areas: green for consistently quiet, yellow for occasional bursts, red for frequent contention. The goal is not to find perfection but to locate where 320 MHz can survive with puncturing versus zones that warrant 160 MHz. Accept that green pockets still shift, so you will build a fallback path. This reconnaissance informs AP placement, primary channel selection, and density before configuration begins.

Finally, document decision rules that determine when 320 MHz remains enabled. A practical rule is to keep 320 MHz only where the primary 20 MHz segment holds a stable MCS at moderate RSSI and where adjacent 20 MHz slices show low occupancy. Add a threshold for retries and a threshold for bufferbloat under load, since wide channels can tempt bursty queues. Create a stoplight decision sheet that flags when to move a wing from 320 to 160 MHz, such as repeated DFS hits or rising hidden node indications. This living document makes change control predictable and gives operations a checklist to defend choices during audits or incident reviews.

Prepare environment

Validate capabilities across APs and clients before touching settings. Confirm radios and drivers genuinely support 320 MHz and the needed modulation and coding schemes under 802.11be. Mixed fleets often include devices that top out at 160 MHz, which can distort test results and increase scheduling complexity. Build a small matrix listing AP model, firmware, client chipset generation, and supported widths. Run a quick link budget check to ensure effective isotropic radiated power adheres to local rules for indoor low power or standard power. Without this inventory, a single incompatible NIC can mislead you into thinking the environment is unstable when it is simply falling back to narrower operation.

Establish a clean control plane and management baseline to reduce noise unrelated to RF. Disable experimental features that add variable airtime costs until validation is complete, including aggressive multi‑link operation toggles that your clients might not negotiate well. Set predictable QoS defaults and lock target wake time schedules to reduce random contention spikes. Prepare monitoring: enable station counters for retries and MCS, spectrum view for power spectral density snapshots, and a traffic generator that can produce both small and large frames. These foundations make later results interpretable and keep troubleshooting focused on spectrum realities, not on conflicting features that push and pull the scheduler.

Plan primary channel placement with an eye toward puncturing flexibility. Choose a primary 20 MHz segment that sites in a relatively quiet portion of the intended 320 MHz span. This protects control exchanges and improves resource unit scheduling because the primary carries critical headers and beacons. As a rule, avoid placing the primary adjacent to known narrow interferers, since you cannot puncture around the primary itself. Sketch likely puncture masks ahead of time by noting 20 MHz slices that are most likely to be busy. This prework prevents last‑minute improvisation and reduces the odds that you will need to abandon wide operation after a single burst of interference.

Execute steps

Apply 320 MHz settings only after rehearsing your puncture options. The central idea is to keep a contiguous usable band for high‑MCS clients while masking narrow interferers that would otherwise disrupt the whole span. Start with a clean, contiguous 320 MHz channel in your quietest test area. Then introduce a planned puncture on a known noisy 20 MHz slice and compare throughput, retries, and latency. Expect a small drop from theoretical peak, but watch for stability improvements that matter more in practice. The right balance keeps utilization healthy without letting a single hotspot collapse performance across the entire allocation.

Use structured traffic to exercise different RU sizes so you expose scheduling weaknesses early. Small frames from voice and control traffic test RU granularity, while large payloads push peak PHY rates. If the scheduler struggles after a puncture, consider shifting the primary to a cleaner 20 MHz segment within the 320 MHz band. That adjustment protects control traffic and restores smoother allocations. Be wary of over‑correcting. Frequent primary changes can confuse clients and increase roaming stickiness. Make one controlled adjustment at a time, then retest with the same traffic profile so your comparisons reflect the configuration, not random client behaviors.

When analytics suggest a specific 20 MHz slot is persistently bad, formalize the mask rather than chasing transient spikes. Codifying a repeatable puncture pattern reduces troubleshooting fatigue and avoids unplanned downgrades to 160 MHz. If two or more nonadjacent slices are impaired, note that returns from 320 MHz may diminish relative to 160 MHz in mixed client rooms. At that point, sizing down for that area can improve predictability even if peak benchmarks fall. The overarching principle is sustained throughput for real workloads, not record numbers in a lab. Document each change, the rationale, and thresholds that would reverse it later.

Validate results

Validation starts by confirming the primary 20 MHz remains clean under load. Measure retries, MCS distribution, and latency at three distances that represent your layout. A healthy configuration shows modest retry rates and stable MCS on the primary even when secondary segments see occasional spikes. Cross‑check with a spectrum snapshot to ensure punctured slices align with observed interferers. If a puncture overlaps clean spectrum or misses a noisy slot, reconsider the mask. Treat this as a safety check for control traffic and scheduling health. It is better to discover a masking mismatch now than during a production burst from an unplanned video stream.

Next, compare effective throughput and goodput using representative application profiles. Stream two simultaneous high bitrate flows alongside transactional traffic and see if latency bounds hold. Wide channels can make queues seem healthy while jitter grows, so include a time series view. The goal is consistent delivery, not isolated peak records. If results fluctuate with client roaming, examine roaming thresholds and primary placement before declaring width unsuitable. Stability across runs is your pass criterion. If variations persist, benchmark the same area at 160 MHz to expose whether width or site dynamics dominate the outcome.

Lastly, validate coexistence by watching neighbor impact during busy periods. Monitor airtime fairness and observe whether background networks show rising retries when your AP transmits. Responsible 320 MHz planning aims to avoid becoming the neighborhood’s loud talker. If evidence shows unfair dominance, reduce transmit power slightly or consider a thinner width in that zone. You are seeking peaceful coexistence that sustains performance through the week, not a one‑time lab win. Document the measurements, attach screenshots, and store them with the change record. Visibility into why you kept or adjusted width prevents future finger‑pointing and speeds incident response.

Troubleshoot and optimize

When issues appear, isolate whether the fault stems from spectrum conditions, feature interactions, or client limitations. Begin with a quick swap test: hold the area constant and toggle between 320 and 160 MHz while keeping traffic profiles and power identical. If instability follows the width, focus on puncturing and primary choice. If instability persists regardless of width, suspect client firmware or roaming thresholds. Keep a short playbook with three branches for faster triage. This method reduces time spent debating causes and moves you directly to the likely fix, whether that is a mask change, width downgrade, or client driver update.

Optimize long‑term behavior by aligning RU scheduling with your client mix. Where many devices cannot use 320 MHz fully, ease contention with sensible airtime policies and measured power trims rather than forcing wide channels everywhere. Consider separating areas by use case: quiet rooms for demonstrably stable 320 MHz and busy corridors set to 160 MHz that still deliver solid goodput. Revisit masks quarterly because interference patterns shift with tenant changes and new electronics. The small cost of periodic checks defends user experience and prevents drift toward fragile, high‑maintenance settings that only shine on the day they were installed.

Track decisions with a simple register that pairs each change to a measurable outcome. Record why a puncture was added, which segments were masked, and the observed effect on retries and jitter. Include a preapproved rollback for each setting so on‑call engineers can recover service quickly during incidents. Over time, this register becomes a playbook for similar buildings and accelerates safe deployments. Treat it as part of your security posture because it guards against ad‑hoc tweaks that introduce new risks. With discipline and transparency, wide channels remain a tool you use selectively, not a default that uses you.

Frequently asked questions

Should I enable 320 MHz everywhere if my AP supports it?

No. Use 320 MHz only where spectrum is consistently quiet and clients benefit. In mixed or noisy areas, 160 MHz or 80 MHz often delivers steadier performance with fewer retries and more predictable latency.

How do I pick the primary 20 MHz segment?

Place the primary on the quietest 20 MHz within the chosen span. The primary carries beacons and control frames, so keeping it clean improves scheduling and stability even when other slices need puncturing.

When is puncturing better than shrinking to 160 MHz?

Prefer puncturing when one or two narrow interferers disrupt an otherwise clean 320 MHz span. If multiple nonadjacent slices are noisy or variability persists, 160 MHz usually produces more consistent results.

Do 160 MHz clients suffer on a 320 MHz AP?

Not inherently. They can operate on subsets, but scheduling overhead rises with mixed capabilities. Validate airtime fairness and consider narrower widths in areas dominated by 160 MHz devices.

What metrics prove the design is stable?

Look for low retries on the primary, steady MCS distribution, controlled latency under mixed traffic, and consistent results across repeated runs. Neighbor fairness should also remain acceptable during busy periods.

Looking ahead

Adopt a phased rollout that scales 320 MHz only where evidence continues to support it. Start with a few quiet zones, publish results, and invite feedback from users who rely on low latency. Then expand selectively, keeping the ability to revert to 160 MHz one change away. Schedule quarterly spectrum reviews and revalidate puncture masks after tenant or device mix changes. As chipset firmware evolves, retest primary placement and RU behavior to ensure assumptions remain true. The goal is a living design that evolves with the site, not a one‑time configuration snapshot. Treat documentation as part of your reliability posture and keep decisions transparent and reversible.
