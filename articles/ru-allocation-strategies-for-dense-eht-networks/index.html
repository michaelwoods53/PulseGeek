RU Allocation Strategies for Dense EHT Networks

By Courtney Lawson • Published February 25, 2026

These strategies focus on RU allocation in dense EHT networks where contention, latency, and fairness must be balanced. Items were chosen based on measurable impact under mixed client capabilities and realistic spectrum constraints, not idealized lab cases. Each pattern includes a concrete example, a rule of thumb, and a tradeoff to clarify when the approach helps or hurts. You will see how allocation decisions interact with channelization, multi-RU scheduling, and puncturing so you can move beyond guesswork and tune for predictable results across busy cells.

Key takeaways

  • Match RU size to observed MCS and SNR for stable efficiency.
  • Reserve small RUs to protect triggers and control under congestion.
  • Use latency-driven weights when voice and AR traffic must preempt.
  • Adapt puncturing maps only when interference is persistent and localized.
  • Separate traffic classes by SSID or slice to avoid starvation risks.
  • 320 MHz packing helps peak throughput, but raises interference exposure.

1) Latency-first RUs for voice and AR

Prioritize small RUs for low-latency flows when jitter is the limiting KPI rather than raw throughput. For example, dedicate one or two 26-tone or 52-tone RUs every scheduling interval to traffic classified as EF or voice bearer in WiFi calling. This keeps codec playout buffers fed and prevents head-of-line blocking behind bulk data. The tradeoff is reduced spectral efficiency when these RUs go partially utilized during quiet periods. You mitigate that with a maximum hold time and a reclaim rule if no eligible frames arrive within two intervals. The why is simple. Packing short frames into compact RUs reduces contention and airtime variability, which matters more to conversational traffic than maximum PHY rate ever will.

Set explicit latency budgets for the reserved RUs so preemption is predictable rather than ad hoc. A practical guardrail is a 10 to 20 millisecond scheduling window that always includes one low-size RU for high-priority traffic, plus a fallback to merge it when queues are empty. Consider a busy open office where AR guidance frames must stay under 30 milliseconds one-way. When bulk uploads surge, the reserved RU still carries control and small payloads on-time. The downside is capacity leakage during bursts of best-effort traffic. That risk is acceptable when latency violations trigger visible failures while throughput dips only degrade transfer time.

Section highlights

  • Reserve compact RUs on a tight window to bound jitter.
  • Reclaim unused slots quickly to limit efficiency losses.

2) Buffer-aware multi-RU scheduling

Allocate multiple RUs per station based on buffer occupancy and headroom, not only on PHY rate. The claim is that queue-aware splits produce steadier latency and goodput under bursty application mixes. For instance, a laptop with two pending large frames and a stable MCS 11 could receive a 106-tone RU plus a 52-tone RU, while a handset with tiny buffers keeps a single 26-tone RU. This reduces re-queue churn and smooths MAC delays. The tradeoff is complexity. You must cap per-TXOP grants to avoid hogging airtime and starve neighbors. A simple rule is a moving average of queue depth over three intervals to filter spikes. This works because RU size tracks actual demand, which raises efficiency when frame aggregation alone cannot keep queues thin.

When implementing a queue-aware policy, start with a minimal scoring function combining queue depth, recent retries, and MCS. Then select RU sizes from a small catalog to simplify testing. It favors clients with sustained backlog while leaving room for small RUs. The tradeoff is that coarse catalogs can mismatch niche cases, so review telemetry weekly and adjust thresholds. The mechanism helps because it binds allocation directly to visible demand rather than guessing from device class.

Section highlights

  • Bind RU size to demand via queue depth and recent retries.
  • Constrain per-station tones to prevent airtime dominance.

3) Size RUs by MCS and SNR

Match RU size to effective MCS and SNR to avoid wasting tones on unreliable links. The claim is that shrinking RUs for lower SNR clients reduces retransmissions and improves aggregate throughput. For example, a client that oscillates between MCS 7 and 8 at edge-of-room SNR benefits from a 106-tone RU instead of 242, because shorter transmit time lowers collision exposure and retries. The cost is extra scheduling overhead and reduced peak throughput for that client. A rule of thumb is step down one RU size when retries exceed a moving threshold like 8 percent over five intervals. This works because smaller RUs compress vulnerable airtime for weaker links while reserving larger blocks for stable, high-SNR stations that can fill them efficiently.

Integrate link-quality driven sizing with your rate control and sounding cadence. If your AP updates CSI infrequently, consider conservative RU downsizing until fresh feedback arrives, then ramp up as confidence improves. Picture a stadium concourse where clients wander and SNR fluctuates. Larger RUs can become liabilities when motion introduces fast fading. A cautious allocator that tightens RU size during uncertainty yields fewer long-frame retries. The drawback is administrative complexity when many clients change state simultaneously. Keep the ladder simple, such as only three RU tiers tied to retry and MCS bands, which reduces oscillation and avoids per-client micro tuning that distracts from more impactful RF decisions.

Section highlights

  • Shrink RU size when retries rise or SNR drifts downward.
  • Use a simple three-tier ladder to limit oscillation risk.

4) Protect small RUs for control and trigger

Reserve a fraction of spectrum for management frames, triggers, and negotiation so congestion cannot stall coordination. The claim is that safeguarding a couple of 26-tone RUs per PPDU prevents spiral failures where control traffic competes with data and starves. Consider a high-density lecture hall. When everyone downloads content, trigger frames and block acknowledgments still need timely airtime. Protecting small RUs keeps the fabric synchronized and shortens the path to data transmissions. The tradeoff is slightly lower peak throughput during calm conditions because those RUs are off-limits to bulk flows. Mitigate that by allowing opportunistic borrowing with a hard preemption rule that evicts best-effort packets as soon as control traffic appears.

Align this protection with channelization and puncturing policy so the reserved RUs are mapped to cleaner subchannels. The limitation is that interference can migrate across time and frequency, so static reservations sometimes miss the mark. Use telemetry to rotate the protected RUs toward subcarriers with persistently lower error rates. This improves resilience without hard coding offsets that later conflict with neighbors.

Section highlights

  • Reserve tiny RUs so control and trigger frames stay timely.
  • Place protected tones on cleaner subchannels using telemetry.

5) Time fairness with RU weights

Apply weighted airtime fairness so critical flows earn proportionally larger or more frequent RUs without starving others. The claim is that weights enforce predictable service levels when client PHY rates differ widely. In a mixed office, set voice SSID weight at 2, collaboration apps at 1.5, and best-effort at 1. Then, in each TXOP, allocate RUs in proportion to active weighted demand. The tradeoff is potential underutilization if high-weight classes go idle and the scheduler is slow to rebalance. Counter that with a decay timer that quickly returns unused entitlement to the pool. This approach works because airtime, not bits, is the scarce resource in WiFi, and weights map better to user experience than raw throughput quotas.

Keep weights conservative to avoid hard starvation. A practical range keeps the highest class within two to three times the lowest. Imagine a clinic where voice must stay within 50 milliseconds one-way, but medical records sync can wait. Weighted fairness ensures the voice queue repeatedly receives a 52-tone RU before bulk transfers claim larger allocations. For context on how EHT’s overall MAC changes affect latency goals and deployment, this primer on 802.11be features and latency aims is helpful: primer on 802.11be EHT features and latency aims. The limitation is that misclassified traffic can exploit priority. Audit QoS markings at the edge to keep the policy honest.

Section highlights

  • Allocate RUs proportionally by weighted airtime demand across classes.
  • Use decay timers to reclaim entitlement when queues go idle.

6) Adaptive puncturing with RU remap

Use preamble puncturing selectively to avoid localized interferers while remapping RU placement to maintain service for sensitive flows. The claim is that removing a noisy subchannel and redistributing RUs across the remaining bandwidth improves reliability without abandoning wide channels entirely. Picture a warehouse near a microwave backhaul spur that corrupts one segment. Puncturing that block and reassigning a 106-tone RU for telemetry prevents repeated retries. The tradeoff is reduced maximal throughput because fewer tones remain. To keep efficiency acceptable, only puncture after confirming persistent errors on a specific segment, not transient spikes. This works because targeted avoidance limits damage while preserving the scheduling rhythm users expect.

Revisit puncturing decisions alongside your RU catalog every maintenance window. If you rely on very wide channels, consider pairing this with a precomputed remap table so small RUs for control retain clean lanes. For a structured overview of puncturing design principles that reduce interference and protect throughput, see this focused preamble puncturing discussion: preamble puncturing design that reduces interference. The limitation is neighbor coordination. If adjacent cells pick different puncture maps, roaming clients may see oscillating performance. Agree on patterns per floor or area to stabilize user experience.

Section highlights

  • Puncture only persistent noisy segments and remap RU placement.
  • Coordinate patterns across cells to avoid roaming instability.

7) Traffic class isolation by SSID or slice

Separate traffic classes by SSID or logical slice when mixed QoS markings cause contention pathologies. The claim is that isolation reduces cross-class interference, letting each queue run a tailored RU policy. Take a venue with IPTV multicast, voice, and guest traffic. Place IPTV on a dedicated SSID with predictable, larger RUs scheduled at fixed cadence, while voice receives frequent small RUs on a business SSID. Guest best-effort then fills the rest with opportunistic allocations. The tradeoff is beacon overhead and management complexity. Limit to a small number of SSIDs and reuse common security settings. Isolation helps because it constrains queue interactions, preventing a misbehaving class from stealing tokens and delaying priority flows.

Combine isolation with guardrails like maximum RU size per SSID and per-user airtime caps. For example, cap guest allocations to 242 tones to prevent one laptop from monopolizing wide grants, while allowing enterprise devices to scale higher when SNR supports it. The limitation is administrative overhead for policy drift over time. Review usage monthly and retire unused SSIDs to keep beacons and discovery fast.

Section highlights

  • Split classes so each RU policy fits its traffic behavior.
  • Constrain RU size and airtime per SSID to curb abuse.

8) Color-aware RU plans with spatial reuse

Leverage BSS coloring and spatial reuse to schedule RUs when neighboring cells are decodable but not disruptive. The claim is that with prudent thresholds, you can run parallel small-RU transmissions that lift area capacity. For example, set OBSS PD to a level that permits reuse when detected energy stays below a safe margin, then assign 26-tone or 52-tone RUs for clients near the AP. The tradeoff is interference risk if thresholds are too permissive. Start conservative and measure retry deltas after enabling reuse. This works because tiny RUs at strong SNR finish quickly, reducing collision windows, while weaker edge clients wait for clearer airtime.

Pair reuse with color-aware contention limits per RU size. Allow frequent small-RU reuse in the cell center and restrict mid to large RUs until the channel looks quieter. In a dense co-working space, this pattern can keep center clients responsive during busy hours. For a wider backdrop on channel width choices that affect reuse feasibility, see this planning guidance on wide-channel feasibility and interference risks: wide-channel feasibility and interference risks. The limitation is policy portability. Thresholds that work in one building may not port cleanly to another with different attenuation, so recalibrate on site.

Section highlights

  • Enable cautious OBSS reuse and favor small RUs near the AP.
  • Tune thresholds per site to limit cross-cell interference.

9) Pack wide channels judiciously at 320 and 160

Use 320 or 160 MHz only when your interference survey supports it and you can keep RU packing efficient. The claim is that wide bandwidth helps station peak rates, but only if you can sustain clean subchannels or puncture predictably. In a lab or low-interference wing, assigning several 242-tone and 106-tone RUs on 320 MHz can clear large queues fast. The tradeoff is fragility. One bad segment hurts many RUs unless the scheduler adapts quickly. For campuses with mixed neighbors, 160 MHz with careful RU catalogs often outperforms aggressive 320 MHz. The why is straightforward. Efficient packing depends on clean slices. When that cannot be ensured, narrower channels with stable RUs beat headline rates that collapse under retries.

Stage a proof window before committing to very wide channels. Measure retry rates per subchannel and compare goodput, not PHY rate, across 80, 160, and 320 MHz. If 320 shows only marginal goodput gains with higher variance, stay at 160 and apply multi-RU allocation to maintain responsiveness. The limitation is device support heterogeneity. Older clients cannot exploit 320, so you may carry the cost without user-visible benefit.

Section highlights

  • Adopt 320 MHz only when subchannels are consistently clean.
  • Compare goodput and variance across widths before committing.

Looking ahead

Adopt these RU allocation patterns incrementally and instrument your network to confirm the expected effect on latency, retries, and goodput. Start with two or three strategies that match your environment, such as latency-first RUs for voice and a modest weighted fairness policy. Measure impact for a week before layering adaptive puncturing or spatial reuse. As you refine, keep channelization in view. Wide channels can help or hurt depending on interference stability. Steady RU catalogs and clear caps reduce oscillation and make behavior reliable. With disciplined iteration, you can reach predictable performance that maps to user experience rather than synthetic peak rates.

Frequently asked questions

How many RU sizes should a practical catalog include?

Use three to five sizes. Fewer simplifies testing and reduces oscillation. Include at least one small RU for control and low-latency traffic, plus mid and larger options for backlog clearing. Expand only if telemetry shows persistent mismatches.

Should I prioritize throughput or latency when setting weights?

Bias toward latency if user experience depends on timely playout or interaction. For bulk transfers, slightly lower throughput usually matters less than stable response. Reassess when business workflows change and adjust weights accordingly.

When is 320 MHz worth the complexity?

Only when subchannels measure clean and clients support it. Compare goodput and variance across 80, 160, and 320 MHz. If wide channels show minimal gain or higher retries, stay narrower and use multi-RU allocation to maintain responsiveness.

How often should I adjust puncturing maps?

Change maps when interference patterns are persistent over days, not minutes. Rotate protected RUs toward cleaner segments during maintenance windows. Avoid frequent flips that confuse roaming clients and destabilize scheduling behavior.

Does spatial reuse help every dense deployment?

No. It helps where center SNR is strong and cross-cell energy stays below safe thresholds. Start conservatively, verify retry rates, and scale reuse only when stability holds. In noisy sites, reuse can increase collisions and harm latency.
