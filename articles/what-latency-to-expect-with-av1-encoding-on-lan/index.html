What Latency to Expect with AV1 Encoding on LAN

By Jacob Reed • Published January 31, 2026

AV1 on a LAN can feel responsive when hardware encoding is available, but latency depends on encode time, decoder buffers, and network pacing. On modern GPUs, the added delay versus H.264 is often small at moderate bitrates, while software AV1 adds noticeable lag. This article answers the core question first, then expands on when AV1 helps image quality, when it can hurt responsiveness, and how to verify the actual end to end delay on Steam Deck. We will compare encoder behavior, unpack bitrate and resolution interactions, and offer validation steps that avoid guesswork. Expect pragmatic thresholds and tradeoffs that let you choose AV1 confidently or fall back to an alternative when needed.

Key takeaways

  • Hardware AV1 adds little latency on LAN when bitrates stay moderate.
  • Software AV1 usually costs multiple frames and harms input response.
  • Short buffers and steady frame pacing matter as much as the encoder.
  • Test end to end latency with on-screen cues and input timestamps.
  • Switch to HEVC or H.264 if encode times spike under load.

Short answer and nuance

The short answer is that AV1 latency on a local network is dominated by encoder and decoder buffering, not the wire itself, which on LAN is effectively near zero for gaming purposes. With a hardware encoder, AV1 can be close to H.264 or HEVC at moderate bitrates and 60 fps, especially when the decoder on Steam Deck keeps a shallow queue. The nuance appears at higher resolutions and bitrates, where encode complexity can momentarily increase frame time and expand the client buffer. A practical rule is to watch for extra frames of delay when switching to AV1 and to verify responsiveness by tapping a key or mouse and observing a visual cue. If the cue lags more than expected, shorten buffers and consider a lower bitrate.

A helpful mental model is to split latency into four buckets: game render, video encode, transport, and video decode with display. On LAN, transport is typically negligible compared with the other steps, so optimizations target encoder and client buffering. Hardware AV1 reduces the extra cost through fixed function blocks, while software AV1 relies on CPU or GPU shaders that can be preempted by the game, increasing variability. If you see uneven response tied to scene complexity, that is often the encoder slipping behind and the client growing its buffer to avoid stutter. Reducing target bitrate or resolution can trim the work per frame and shrink the decoding queue.

Expectations should be tied to frame budgets. At 60 fps, you have about 16.7 milliseconds per frame, so any encoder or decoder delay that occupies more than a frame will be perceptible during quick inputs. Hardware AV1 can fit within that budget on many recent GPUs at 720p to 1080p with moderate bitrates, while higher frame rates like 120 fps cut your margin. If the Deck decodes AV1 in software, power limits can increase the queue depth and add delay despite a fast LAN. Because different games vary in CPU and GPU load, test in both a light title and a demanding one to capture best and worst cases before you decide on AV1 as your default.

When it applies vs when it does not

AV1 fits well when your server GPU offers hardware AV1 and your target bitrate is moderate for the chosen resolution, because this balances encode effort and visual gain. In these conditions, AV1 often delivers fewer artifacts than H.264 at the same bits, which lets you hold frame pacing steady and keep the client buffer short. It is also a good pick when your network is clean but not limitless, such as shared LAN Wi Fi where shaving bitrate helps stability. For Steam Deck, pairing hardware AV1 with 720p or 800p at reasonable targets typically preserves responsiveness while improving fine texture retention compared with H.264, especially in foliage or particle heavy scenes.

AV1 is a poor choice when you lack hardware support, because software AV1 can consume a frame or more beyond your render budget under load. Even with hardware blocks, pushing very high bitrates or resolutions increases motion estimation cost, which may expand latency during complex frames. If the Deck must decode AV1 without acceleration, power and thermal constraints raise the risk of buffer growth. In those cases, choose HEVC or H.264 to save encode and decode time. Borderline situations deserve careful testing because conditions change across games and drivers. For example, competitive shooters emphasize minimal delay, so even a small added encode queue is noticeable, and you might prefer HEVC at a slightly higher bitrate to maintain instant input feel. On the other hand, slower paced games tolerate a small buffer increase if it prevents macroblocking. If you must stick with AV1 for quality reasons, trim the Deck’s decoding queue and cap frame rate to relax the per frame timing budget. How to implement or validate

Implementation starts by constraining the budget. Cap the game to a stable frame rate so render time is predictable, then set AV1 bitrate modestly for the chosen resolution. Keep client buffering tight by disabling extra smoothing or long jitter buffers in the streaming app, and prefer hardware decode on the Deck when available. To validate, use a visual cue test: press a key that toggles a screen element and record with a high frame rate camera. Count frames between input and visible change to approximate end to end delay. Repeat in a light scene and a heavy scene to capture variability. If the count grows with AV1 relative to HEVC, reduce bitrate or resolution first, then consider swapping codecs.

Instrumentation helps isolate where time is spent. Most streaming clients expose statistics such as receive jitter, dropped frames, and decode queue depth. Watch queue depth while alternating between static and complex scenes. If depth grows during motion, your encoder is slipping and pushing frames late. That is your cue to lower target bitrate or switch to a faster encoder preset if available. A codec settings guide that details hardware encoder controls like B frames, reference count, and lookahead can be useful, because disabling features that expand latency often narrows the delay without large quality loss at Deck resolutions.

Network checks still matter even on LAN. Verify that the link is wired or a strong Wi Fi channel, and confirm you are not saturating the uplink with other traffic. Monitor packet loss and retransmissions because even slight losses provoke client buffering. If latency spikes appear only on Wi Fi, consider lowering bitrate or moving to Ethernet for the server. Alternatives and related questions

Alternatives to AV1 include HEVC and H.264, which usually encode faster at the same resolution and reduce the risk of buffer growth under pressure. HEVC often strikes a balance by preserving quality at lower bitrates than H.264 while staying nimble on common GPUs. If your goal is minimum delay for competitive play, try HEVC at a moderate bitrate with B frames disabled and verify responsiveness using the same input to visual method. For image stability at very low bitrates, AV1 still leads, but only when the encoder and decoder keep a shallow queue. Choose based on which variable is binding first in your setup: encode time, network headroom, or decoder capability on the Deck.

Related questions include whether to cap frame rate to tame latency and how bitrate interacts with perceived smoothness. Capping to a stable frame rate often shrinks encoder variability, which holds the client buffer short and reduces input delay. If your network is tight, selecting the right bitrate for your resolution keeps the stream from oscillating and avoids sudden queue changes. For software paths, prefer faster presets or switch codecs entirely if they cannot maintain your frame budget. Frequently asked questions

Does AV1 always add more latency than H.264 on LAN?

No. With hardware encoding and shallow client buffers, AV1 can feel similar to H.264 at moderate bitrates. Software AV1 or aggressive features can add delay, especially during complex scenes.

Is Wi Fi fast enough for AV1 without extra delay?

Yes when the signal is strong and bitrate is conservative. If Wi Fi introduces loss or jitter, the client buffer grows to hide stutter, which increases end to end latency despite a local network.

Should I disable B frames for lower latency with AV1?

Often yes. B frames and deep lookahead can increase internal buffering. Try disabling them or using a low latency preset, then retest input to visual timing to confirm improvement.

What if my Steam Deck decodes AV1 in software?

Expect higher and more variable delay. Prefer HEVC or H.264 in that case, or lower AV1 bitrate and resolution while watching decode queue depth to keep buffers from growing.

How do I measure end to end latency reliably?

Use a high frame rate camera to record a timed key press that toggles an on screen element, then count frames between input and change. Repeat in light and heavy scenes to catch variability.
