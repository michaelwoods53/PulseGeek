EHT Network Design Template for Campuses

By Courtney Lawson • Published January 23, 2026

EHT design for campuses succeeds when capacity targets, security controls, and operational guardrails are defined before hardware choices. This template focuses on 6 GHz planning, AP density, multigig switching, and PoE so coverage and throughput match real workloads. Each item ties a configuration decision to measurable outcomes and the risks that come with shortcuts.

Key takeaways

  • Start with explicit capacity targets and validate against airtime budgets.
  • Prefer 6 GHz primary service with disciplined reuse and low CCI.
  • Rightsize AP density by environment using measured client behavior.
  • Align multigig backhaul and PoE classes to real power draw.
  • Harden with WPA3 and MLO guardrails that match threat models.
  • Prove results with staged validation and continuous monitoring.

1) Capacity and client mix first

Set throughput and concurrency targets before anything else, because EHT radios cannot compensate for unrealistic airtime assumptions. Start by segmenting clients into personas with expected MCS ranges and duty cycles, like lecture hall laptops targeting MCS 7 to 9 half the hour and IoT sensors transmitting for seconds per day. For example, a 300-seat auditorium might plan for 150 active clients at 2 Mbps sustained downlink per user with 10 percent uplink, yielding 300 Mbps plus control overhead. The tradeoff is overfitting to current applications. If you architect to a narrow profile and new video use appears, you will miss targets. Hedge by applying a 20 to 30 percent airtime margin on the busiest SSID and by modeling worst-hour patterns rather than averages to avoid brittle plans that fail under peak.

Convert those targets into airtime budgets per band so capacity maps to physics. Use a simple rule of thumb that a radio’s practical payload is roughly half of its PHY headline once overhead, contention, and retries are included. For example, if a 6 GHz 4x4 radio advertises multi-gigabit rates, budget only several hundred megabits of steady throughput for predictable user experience. That conservative mapping prevents planning based on lab PHY numbers that evaporate in shared air. The limitation is that some rooms with line-of-sight and controlled devices can exceed that budget. Document exceptions instead of inflating the baseline. 2) 6 GHz channel plan with disciplined reuse

Lead with 6 GHz as the primary service layer, because clean spectrum and wider channels reduce contention when clients and AP density support it. In teaching spaces, a 160 MHz plan may be practical if co-channel interference stays low and client capabilities are verified. In dense dorms or labs, prefer 80 MHz or even 40 MHz to increase reuse and stabilize latency. For example, a four-building core might assign non-overlapping 80 MHz blocks per floor stack with static channel exclusion near bridges. The tradeoff is headline speed envy. Wider channels look attractive on spec sheets, but reuse collapses if neighbors hear each other. To stage the move from Wi‑Fi 6, consult a structured migration outline that includes milestones and risk gates using a guide on structured migration with validation gates.

Lock transmit power to minimize cell overlap rather than chasing edge RSSI, because boundary users consume disproportionate airtime at poor MCS. For example, cap 6 GHz EIRP around 18 to 20 dBm indoors as a starting point, then tune down in congested stacks after measuring roaming behavior. Use 5 GHz as a capacity relief layer and keep 2.4 GHz for low-rate IoT only. The tradeoff is edge coverage complaints from isolated corners or stairwells. Address those with targeted micro-cells or directional antennas instead of raising global power. A related limitation is regulatory differences per region, which may restrict PSD or channel availability. Maintain country-specific templates and verify firmware compliance before rollout to avoid accidental violations that force rework across multiple buildings.

Section highlights

  • Prefer 6 GHz with channel widths that preserve reuse and low CCI.
  • Set restrained power targets and add micro-cells for genuine dead zones.

3) AP placement and density by environment

Size AP density to the busiest hour in each environment rather than square footage, because seating patterns and device concurrency drive airtime demand. In auditoriums, plan for more cells with narrower channels and directional antennas to sectorize seats. In libraries, prioritize even signal with 80 MHz where interference is low. For example, a 300-seat lecture hall might use six 6 GHz cells with antennas aimed at distinct seating blocks, plus a reserved SSID for faculty devices. The tradeoff is hardware and cabling cost. Sectorization adds complexity and raises installation effort, but it prevents two APs from contending for the same students. In open study areas, ceiling-mount omnis are fine, but keep spacing tight enough that clients do not stick to far cells at low MCS.

Validate placement with predictive design then confirm with active surveys, because simulation assumptions about attenuation and body loss often understate reality. Walk tests with controlled clients reveal sticky roam thresholds and uplink weakness that predictive maps miss. For example, after installing in the first building, run an active validation on 6 GHz for MCS distribution and retry rates during class changeover. The limitation is time cost. Full surveys across every floor can delay subsequent buildings. Use a sampling approach per archetype space, and codify rules in a template so later installations inherit proven spacing. Section highlights

  • Design density around busiest-hour demand in each space archetype.
  • Confirm predictive models with targeted active surveys before scaling.

4) Multigig backhaul and uplink provisioning

Provision multigig backhaul for EHT APs where 6 GHz and 5 GHz radios will be busy simultaneously, because a single 1 Gbps link can become the bottleneck before airtime does. For example, specify 2.5 Gbps access ports with 10 Gbps uplinks in lecture halls and residence halls, while standard offices might remain at 1 Gbps if measured utilization remains modest. The tradeoff is capital and power overhead on switches. Not every closet needs multi-gig, and some APs downshift during quiet hours. Build per-floor link utilization baselines during pilots and scale only where sustained load justifies the spend. Account for cable quality too, since older Cat5e runs may struggle with higher speeds over distance and could require remediation, which should be phased alongside AP replacements to avoid repeated ceiling work.

Right-size uplink oversubscription at the access and distribution layers, because bursting from synchronized class transitions can momentarily flood links. A conservative target is 4:1 to 6:1 oversubscription from access to distribution when many APs share a closet, tightened to 2:1 in the most congested buildings. For example, a stack of switches feeding 48 APs across a library wing might aggregate with dual 40 Gbps uplinks to a core pair, giving headroom for exam week. The limitation is diminishing returns. Beyond a point, uplink upgrades do little if the WAN or firewall becomes the choke. Coordinate with routing and security teams so the entire data path can absorb peak rates. Section highlights

  • Deploy multigig where radios saturate to avoid access port bottlenecks.
  • Plan uplinks around peak bursts and coordinate with core capacity.

5) Power and PoE budgets that match draw

Budget PoE to the AP’s worst-case draw with margin, because features like additional radios, scanning, and USB peripherals raise power beyond nominal specs. For example, allocate PoE++ Class 6 for high-end EHT models that enable tri-band radios and full spatial streams, leaving 10 to 15 percent slack per port to avoid brownouts during firmware upgrades. The tradeoff is wasted capacity if many APs seldom hit that peak. Use switch telemetry to find buildings where draw remains far below class limits and consider mixed provisioning in later phases. Also verify line length and bundling practices, since voltage drop and thermal constraints can reduce delivered power at the endpoint. A practical hedge is to keep spare higher-class ports in each closet so field swaps do not necessitate re-terminating or moving patching under pressure.

Align power policy with functionality rather than uniform settings, because trimming features on lower-class ports can preserve stability without full switch upgrades. For instance, disable USB accessories, restrict extra scanning radios, or cap transmit power on APs that cannot receive full budget. That keeps them stable and predictable in offices, while reserving high-class ports for auditoriums and labs. The limitation is operational complexity. Divergent profiles increase configuration variance and the chance of mistakes during maintenance. Document a decision tree that maps AP models to PoE classes and allowed features, then audit quarterly. Section highlights

  • Size PoE to worst-case AP features with margin and telemetry checks.
  • Use feature gating on low-power ports to maintain predictable behavior.

6) SSID and security design with WPA3 and MLO

Consolidate SSIDs and enforce WPA3 Enterprise with protected management frames, because fewer beacons reduce overhead and strong authentication blocks trivial abuse. Aim for two to three user-facing SSIDs segmented by role, plus an IoT network with MAC address onboarding workflows that map to per-device identities. For example, deploy a staff SSID restricted to 6 GHz and a student SSID on 5 and 6 GHz, both with 802.1X and PMF required, while IoT remains on 2.4 or 5 with device-specific controls. The tradeoff is onboarding friction during transition. Legacy devices may fail until drivers and supplicants are updated. Mitigate with a temporary compatibility network and a clear retirement date. When enabling multi-link operation, set policy for link steering to avoid pinning clients to poor secondary links in congested halls.

7) Validation and monitoring that prove outcomes

Validate in stages before scaling, because small pilots surface roaming, retry, and power pitfalls that are expensive to undo later. Start with one building per archetype and measure MCS distribution, retries, latency under 50th and 95th percentile load, and roaming times with multi-link enabled. For example, schedule a loaded test during class change in a lecture hall and record airtime by band and per-SSID throughput over 30 minutes. The tradeoff is delayed time to broader rollout. However, staged validation avoids multi-building rework. Capture baselines as dashboards, then lock templates so subsequent buildings inherit the proven configuration. Deploy monitoring that distinguishes RF pain from backhaul or authentication delays, because EHT spans radios, switches, and identity systems. Instrument per-radio airtime, channel utilization, MCS spread, and client failure reasons alongside switch port errors and RADIUS response times. For example, alert when 6 GHz retries exceed a chosen threshold or when MCS skews low in a room that typically runs hot, prompting a site check for new sources of attenuation or noise. The limitation is alert fatigue and false positives in seasonal peaks like finals week. Calibrate thresholds by semester trends and suppress non-actionable noise while preserving true anomalies.   • Monitor RF, backhaul, and identity paths to isolate performance issues.

Frequently asked questions

Should campuses use 160 MHz channels everywhere?

No. Use 160 MHz only where co-channel interference remains low and client capability is verified. Most dense buildings benefit from 80 MHz or 40 MHz to increase reuse and stabilize latency under load.

Is 2.5 Gbps mandatory for every EHT access point?

No. Provision multigig where simultaneous 6 GHz and 5 GHz load saturates a 1 Gbps link. Offices with modest utilization can stay on 1 Gbps if monitoring confirms sustained headroom.

How many SSIDs are ideal on an EHT campus?

Keep two to three user-facing SSIDs plus an IoT network. Fewer beacons reduce overhead and improve MCS. Enforce WPA3 Enterprise with protected management frames for user networks.

What is a safe starting transmit power for 6 GHz indoors?

Begin around 18 to 20 dBm EIRP and tune down in congested areas. Favor smaller cells with better reuse rather than chasing edge coverage with high power.

How do we handle legacy devices during transition?

Offer a temporary compatibility SSID with a retirement date, update drivers where possible, and keep IoT on tightly controlled 2.4 or 5 GHz until replacements arrive.
