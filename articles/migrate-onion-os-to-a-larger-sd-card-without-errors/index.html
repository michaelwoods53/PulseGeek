Migrate Onion OS to a Larger SD Card Without Errors

By Jordan Kelly • Published February 21, 2026

Migrating Onion OS to a larger SD card is safest when you approach it like a small project with clear checkpoints. This guide walks through planning the move, preparing tools, and executing a verified transfer so errors do not follow you to the new media. We assume you can connect both cards to a PC or Mac and that your library fits on the destination. If not, the plan includes trimming or staging content. By the end, you will have a larger card with expanded partitions, validated filesystems, and known-good boot behavior, plus a fallback if anything misfires.

Key takeaways

  • Decide between block cloning or file copy based on card health.
  • Label devices explicitly and confirm sizes before any write operation.
  • Expand and check filesystems after migration to use full capacity.
  • Validate Onion OS boot and run hash checks to confirm integrity.
  • Keep a verified image backup to recover quickly from mistakes.

Plan the work

Start by choosing the migration approach that fits your reality, because method affects risk, time, and validation effort. Block-level cloning mirrors the entire source card, including boot sector and partition table, which is ideal when the source is healthy and you want an exact replica. A file-level copy rebuilds the layout fresh and reduces the chance of replicating low-level errors, but you must reinstall the bootloader or use a known-good image first. As a rule of thumb, clone when the source reads cleanly and passes a verify pass, and copy when you suspect filesystem inconsistencies or questionable sectors. This early decision determines the tools you prepare and the checks you perform afterward.

Define your safeguards next, since a reversible plan reduces stress and mistakes under time pressure. Create a read-only image of the source card before touching partitions so you can revert quickly if the destination ends up inconsistent. Name devices clearly and record sizes in a simple table or note, because misidentifying /dev/sdb and /dev/sdc is the most common destructive error. If you are new to the process, review a broad setup overview to ground expectations, such as the end-to-end Onion OS orientation that explains folder structure and updates, then return to finalize your path. This context prevents surprises later while expanding volumes.

Finally, preview partition growth, since moving to a larger SD card is only half the outcome. The other half is resizing the data partition to claim the additional free space without compromising boot files. Sketch your target state: a small boot partition unchanged, followed by a larger data partition aligned to the device’s erase block for performance. Document which tool you will use to expand, such as a partition editor, and the checkpoint you will use to confirm integrity. This planning artifact becomes your checklist during execution, reducing decisions you must make under time pressure while the card is writing.

Prepare environment

Preparation begins with dependable hardware access, because flaky adapters cause silent read errors that derail verification. Use a direct SD slot or a reputable USB reader known for stable throughput and correct reporting of capacities. On your workstation, install two categories of tools: a sector-imaging utility for cloning and a partition editor for resizing. Confirm you have a checksum tool to compare files or images after the move. Before proceeding, list source and destination device paths and their capacities, then label each with a non-conflicting identifier. Taking five minutes here prevents writing an image to your system drive or the wrong removable disk.

Next, verify the source card’s readability to decide whether cloning is still appropriate. Run a non-destructive read with a progress indicator and watch for repeated retries or I/O errors that signal marginal media. If you see anomalies, prefer a file-level copy from a fresh, known-good Onion OS image to a clean partition layout, then sync your content folders. If reads are clean, a straightforward block clone is efficient and captures boot sectors without manual steps. Either way, capture a baseline hash of critical directories like BIOS, ROMs, and saves, which you can re-check later to confirm a faithful transfer.

When you choose to clone, use a simple, explicit command that is easy to verify and repeat. The goal is a reliable sector copy with write-through behavior so you know data has landed on the destination safely. Expect that larger cards will take proportionally longer, so budget time to complete and avoid interrupting the process mid-write.

Execute steps

Execution starts with a clean baseline, since lingering mounts can corrupt writes or confuse partition tools. Unmount both SD cards on your workstation so imaging or copy tools have exclusive access. If you chose cloning, run your command and let it finish without interruption. For file copy, first deploy a known Onion OS base to the destination, then copy the content folders. Maintain the source card in read-only mode if your adapter supports a lock to prevent accidental changes. This controlled approach reduces write-time conflicts and ensures the next step, expanding the data partition, operates on a stable layout.

After the data lands on the larger SD card, expand the data partition to use the new capacity. Use a partition manager to extend the last partition to the end of the device, then grow the filesystem. Many tools can expand online after imaging, but if the filesystem complains, run the check and then retry the resize. Keep the boot partition untouched, because moving it can break boot behavior on some handhelds. Once complete, sync the filesystem to flush pending writes. This aligns your logical structure with the physical space and prepares for runtime checks on the device itself.

With structure updated, copy integrity checks confirm that content survived the move. Spot-check hashes of a handful of large files and a few small ones across different directories. If your earlier baseline included recorded checksums, compare them against the destination to catch silent corruption. For additional confidence, boot the device with the new card and load a few systems that previously worked. Test save and load operations to confirm metadata directories have appropriate permissions. This layered verification increases trust that your Onion OS environment will behave consistently on the larger SD card under normal play.

Validate results

Validation ensures correctness beyond a successful boot, because subtle file damage can hide until a specific core loads an affected asset. Start by comparing directory sizes for major folders to confirm you did not miss a subdirectory during a file-copy route. Then run targeted checksums on representative files, including one large archive and a few small configuration or save files. If any mismatch appears, re-copy the affected items and re-test. This selective strategy avoids hashing the entire card while still exposing common integrity failures like partial writes or filename encoding issues that surface on cross-platform transfers.

Next, evaluate partition alignment and filesystem health to avoid long-term performance regression. Use your partition tool to report start sectors and ensure the data partition begins on a boundary aligned with the device’s erase block. Run a filesystem check that repairs minor inconsistencies, then mount and browse to ensure normal responsiveness. If load times feel slow, consider whether the new card’s speed class differs from the old one or whether background processes on your workstation throttled the copy. These checks provide a practical view of the new environment’s durability before you retire the original card.

Finally, perform device-side behavioral tests that mirror real usage. Launch a few emulators, load ROMs that rely on BIOS files, and confirm shaders, thumbnails, and playlists appear as expected. Save in-game, exit, and relaunch to verify persistence. Try suspending and resuming the device to test how Onion OS handles rapid power changes on the new media. If all actions behave normally, label the card and store the retired source as a short-term backup. This final step closes the loop on correctness by proving both data integrity and operational stability under everyday tasks.

Troubleshoot and optimize

When the new card will not boot, isolate causes methodically to avoid making damage worse. Check whether the boot partition’s files match a known-good image and confirm that the partition flags remain unchanged after resizing. If you cloned, ensure the device’s partition table was re-read after imaging. For copy-based moves, reinstall the boot files from a verified source and re-run your content sync. If the device still stalls, revert to your original card, confirm the baseline still works, then iterate on the destination. This sequence protects data while narrowing the problem to bootloader content, flags, or device-specific quirks.

Intermittent corruption signals media or connection trouble rather than filesystem defects alone. Inspect the card reader and cable, then test with another known-good reader to rule out flaky hardware. If corruptions persist, compare your card’s capacity and performance claims with a verification tool to detect counterfeits that misreport sizes. For stubborn errors, switch from block cloning to a clean file copy approach, or vice versa, and re-test. Each method stresses the hardware differently and can bypass a class of failures. Keeping both paths available gives you a second chance without starting from scratch.

To improve reliability long term, adopt habits that reduce write risk and speed future recoveries. Eject cards cleanly and avoid power interruptions during writes. Keep a compressed, verified image of a known-good Onion OS layout so you can restore in one pass instead of rebuilding. These practices turn a one-time migration into a repeatable process that scales with your library and hardware changes.

Looking ahead

Your migration is complete, so shift focus to staying resilient and ready for growth. Keep the retired card on hand for a short period as a fallback, then rotate it into offsite storage or reuse it for testing once you trust the new setup. Periodically capture a fresh image of your current card so future expansions or replacements become a single write and resize routine.
