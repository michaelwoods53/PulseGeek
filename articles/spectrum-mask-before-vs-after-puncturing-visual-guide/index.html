Spectrum Mask Before vs After Puncturing

By Michael Carter • Published February 11, 2026

The visual shape of a spectrum mask makes puncturing easier to reason about. By contrasting a clean baseline with a punctured view, you can see how energy redistributes and where the notch forms across occupied subchannels. This guide focuses on practical visualization, not raw math, so you can interpret analyzer displays quickly during Wi‑Fi 8 testing and avoid misreads caused by smoothing or averaging. We will define key terms, offer decision lenses for noisy environments, and show compact scenarios that map symptoms to likely causes. The goal is to help you distinguish expected puncturing behavior from measurement artifacts using consistent cues drawn from real workflows.

Key takeaways

  • A punctured spectrum mask shows a stable notch with softened shoulders.
  • Confirm notches across time windows to rule out transient interference.
  • Correlate protocol events with RF views to validate puncturing claims.
  • Use adjacent channel symmetry to separate analyzer artifacts from truth.
  • Pick span and RBW to balance resolution against noise amplification.

Concepts and definitions

A spectrum mask defines allowable transmit power versus frequency, so baseline visuals show a smooth plateau with tapered shoulders across the configured channel. Under preamble puncturing, a subchannel hit by interference is nulled, creating a recognizable notch that lowers energy across that slice without shifting the center frequency. Think of the notch as intentional silence, not a detune. For example, a 40 MHz channel may present a narrow dip aligned to an impaired 20 MHz segment. The tradeoff is less usable bandwidth, which can reduce peak PHY rates while preserving operation for unaffected resource units. You should expect the shoulders near the notch to soften because windowing and filtering spread edges slightly, which prevents a harsh cliff that would challenge spectral masks and adjacent channels.

A before view typically exhibits consistent energy across occupied subcarriers, whereas the after view displays a notch whose depth exceeds normal ripple from measurement noise. A rule of thumb is to look for a multi dB reduction over a contiguous set of bins that holds across short sweeps. For instance, if an analyzer averages traces, ensure the dip persists when averaging is off to avoid smoothing artifacts that imitate a notch. The limitation is that different devices apply different detectors or envelope modes, which can flatten small features. That is why pairing RF visuals with packet captures helps verify that puncturing decisions align with protocol annotations and are not just cosmetic gaps from display settings.

Notches vary by width based on which subchannels are excluded, but their placement should align with known interference or dynamic frequency selection decisions. When the notch appears off center without correlating RF triggers, suspect an analyzer scaling or span mismatch before concluding adaptive nulling occurred. As an example, a too wide span compresses the display, making the notch appear shallow or shifted relative to center. Conversely, a too narrow span can exaggerate edge roll off and hide neighboring energy leakage. The practical approach is to confirm frequency alignment against the channel plan, then sweep span and resolution bandwidth in small steps to test whether the notch shape remains stable. Consistency across settings indicates a true puncturing effect rather than a view artifact.

Frameworks and decision lenses

A durable interpretation framework starts with correlation across layers. First, confirm that RF energy shows a sustained notch, then correlate with protocol annotations that indicate preamble puncturing was elected. Cross validation prevents over trusting a single view. For example, a spectrum analyzer may show a dip while a protocol analyzer records no puncturing events, pointing to external interference rather than transmitter behavior. The tradeoff is time overhead because capturing both views increases setup complexity. As a mitigation, capture short, synchronized windows that bracket the suspected event so the same bursts appear in both records. This creates an audit trail that distinguishes deliberate channel nulling from passive fades caused by multipath or moving clients.

Measurement settings strongly influence the apparent spectrum mask. Resolution bandwidth determines detail, so choose RBW near subcarrier spacing to reveal the notch without injecting noise variance. For instance, halving RBW improves feature separation but extends sweep time, which can blur fast changes if the analyzer uses slow detectors. Video bandwidth and averaging smooth fluctuations, which clarifies the baseline mask but can hide short puncturing bursts. A pragmatic rule is to record two passes: one high detail with modest averaging and one faster pass with minimal smoothing. If both passes reveal a notch at the same bins, confidence increases. If only the smoothed pass shows it, suspect averaging artifacts rather than a real null.

Decision lenses also include tool choice and view mode. Waterfall views help confirm whether the notch persists over time, whereas max hold traces emphasize worst case edges that can exaggerate shoulders. A balanced approach is to review a rolling average trace for context, a current trace for recency, and a max hold trace for limits, toggling each to see if the notch is structural. Examples and short scenarios

Consider a lab where a clean 80 MHz channel shows a broad plateau before testing. After enabling puncturing, a persistent 20 MHz notch appears at the upper segment, and throughput drops modestly while latency stabilizes. This pattern suggests the device correctly avoided a noisy subchannel while preserving service on the remaining bandwidth. If a protocol analyzer concurrently logs puncturing events for the same timeframe, the visual and packet evidence align. The main limitation is that aggregate metrics may conceal short bursts, so watch for spikes in retry counts around the notch frequency. Now consider a false positive. You observe a dip that appears only when averaging is high and vanishes on live traces. The waterfall shows no consistent gap across time, and the protocol capture contains no puncturing flags. The likely cause is smoothing that carved an artificial trough from normal ripple. The remedy is to lower averaging, widen VBW relative to RBW, and recheck spans that center the suspected bin group. Another frequent misread arises from adjacent channel traffic that creates asymmetric shoulders, tricking the eye into thinking a centered notch exists. To reduce guesswork, compare symmetry around the primary center frequency and check whether the dip is mirrored across the midline, which true puncturing often is not.

Key terms

  • Bit Depth — The number of bits used to represent each audio sample.
  • DFS Channels — 5 GHz channels that avoid radar by dynamically switching.
  • Effective Bandwidth — The portion of a configured channel that is actually usable.
  • Fault Detection — Identifying abnormal conditions like lamp outages or power issues.
  • Preamble Puncturing — A method to use most of a wide channel while avoiding a narrow interfered portion.
  • Resolution — The number of pixels in each video frame.
  • Spectral Mask — Regulatory and standard limits on transmitted power over frequency.
  • Spectrogram — A plot showing signal power across frequency and time.

Frequently asked questions

How do I tell a real puncturing notch from random noise?

Look for a contiguous dip across bins that persists over short time windows and remains visible when averaging is reduced. Confirm alignment with protocol events to distinguish a true notch from transient interference.

What RBW should I use to visualize the notch clearly?

Choose RBW near subcarrier spacing for your channel width to resolve the notch without excessive noise variance. Validate by checking that notch depth and position stay stable when you slightly sweep RBW and span.

Why does the notch shift when I change the span?

A span that is too wide compresses the display and can make features look shifted or shallow. Recenter on the channel and test nearby spans. If the notch position stabilizes, the earlier view was a scaling artifact.

Do I need both spectrum and protocol analyzers to confirm puncturing?

It is not strictly required, but cross validation increases confidence. Use spectrum views to see the notch and protocol traces to verify that puncturing decisions occurred at the same times and frequencies.
