Integer Scaling and Aspect Ratio on Miyoo RetroArch

By Jared Butler • Published January 9, 2026

Integer scaling and aspect ratio decisions determine how clean or blurred classic games look on Miyoo RetroArch. Readers who value crisp pixels need to know how integer multiples interact with viewport size and the handheld’s native resolution. This guide explains the concepts behind pixel-accurate scaling, why small borders can be beneficial, and when fractional scaling is reasonable. We will weigh tradeoffs using practical examples from systems like NES, SNES, and Game Boy. Along the way, I will show how core options, video settings, and overlays align to produce consistent results. The goal is to help you form a repeatable approach that survives core changes and game switches without constant tweaking. With a few rules of thumb, you can get sharp images and stable performance while keeping aspect integrity intact.

Key takeaways

  • Integer scaling preserves pixel edges and reduces shimmering across cores.
  • Aspect integrity avoids stretching and keeps sprites proportional and readable.
  • Custom viewports balance borders against clarity on fixed 640 by 480 screens.
  • Fractional scaling is workable when mild blur is acceptable to fill space.

Concepts and definitions

Integer scaling means enlarging a game’s native resolution by whole-number multiples to keep pixel edges aligned. On a Miyoo Mini display at 640 by 480, Game Boy’s 160 by 144 image cleanly scales at 3x or 4x vertically, creating sharp lines with predictable borders. The tradeoff is unused screen space when the integer multiple does not perfectly fit both dimensions. Fractional scaling fills more area but can introduce shimmer during scrolling because sampling does not land on exact pixel boundaries. RetroArch provides Integer Scale and Aspect Ratio controls so you can choose precision or fill based on the core and game. Understanding both settings prevents conflicts where one setting silently overrides the other and produces stretched results.

Aspect ratio describes the proportional relationship between width and height, such as 4:3 for many 8 and 16 bit consoles. Preserving the original aspect avoids distortion that makes circles appear oval and sprites look wider or thinner than intended. On Miyoo RetroArch, setting Aspect Ratio to core provided then toggling Integer Scale often yields accurate geometry with small side borders. The main limitation appears when a system’s vertical count does not divide neatly into 480, forcing either top and bottom borders or a fractional step. When overlay bezels are used, those borders can enhance presentation by framing the gameplay region. You can also adjust viewport position to center or align the image within that frame.

Viewport refers to the rectangle where RetroArch draws the game after scaling. For integer scaling, the viewport size is determined by the chosen multiple and the source resolution. For example, SNES commonly renders around 256 by 224, which fits vertically at 2x or 3x on 480p with different amounts of unused rows. Choosing 3x uses 672 vertical pixels, so it exceeds the display and is not possible. In that case 2x yields 448 pixels high and leaves 32 pixels distributed as borders. This controlled padding prevents fractional interpolation blur. When you require a bit more fill, a custom viewport can stretch horizontally within 4:3 while keeping the vertical integer, which often looks cleaner than full two dimensional fractional scaling.

Frameworks and decision lenses

A practical decision lens starts with the display’s native resolution, then the core’s nominal game resolution, and finally your tolerance for borders. First, confirm the handheld’s panel size so you know the vertical budget. Second, check the system’s typical vertical lines from core documentation or a quick on screen display. If the largest integer multiple does not exceed 480, use it and accept the remaining border. If it exceeds 480 by a small margin, consider a hybrid approach that keeps a vertical integer but allows a fractional horizontal step inside a fixed aspect. This keeps text crisp along scanlines while filling more width. Reserve full fractional scaling for cases where visual artifacts are minimal or bezel overlays justify the fill.

When speed matters, prefer integer scaling because it is computationally simple and reduces sampling overhead. RetroArch’s software path handles this well on modest hardware, which helps handhelds maintain stable frame pacing. Fractional methods combined with heavy shaders may cost extra cycles and can expose timing jitter, especially on action heavy scenes. The counterpoint is that some subpixel interpolation looks fine on small screens at viewing distances of 30 to 40 centimeters. If you choose fractional scaling, keep shaders light and disable costly effects like multi pass CRT masks. You can revisit performance tuning with the Quick Menu frame time graphs when experimenting. Always prioritize consistent frame delivery over chasing marginal extra fill.

Aspect policies benefit from a small table that maps common systems to integer options. Treat these as starting points and refine per core. If your game toggles high resolution modes or alternate line counts, you might see occasional letterboxing shift. In those cases, lock vertical integer and allow tiny horizontal adjustments while retaining the core’s aspect. This stabilizes text rendering on RPGs where readability matters. Remember that overlay choice influences perception of borders. A subtle bezel turns empty space into deliberate framing, which can make conservative scaling feel intentional rather than wasteful. Use overlays that fade near the gameplay area, preserving focus without adding busy patterns that distract during scrolling.

Pitfalls, limitations, and edge cases

The most common mistake is enabling integer scaling and then forcing a mismatched aspect that stretches the image. RetroArch prioritizes certain settings depending on the core and driver, so conflicts produce surprising results. A safer flow is to set aspect to core provided first, then enable integer scale, and only then consider a custom viewport. Another pitfall involves relying on heavy shaders to hide fractional blur. On modest hardware, multi pass effects can introduce frame pacing jitter that feels worse than narrow borders. If you experiment with shaders, start with single pass lightweight options and measure stability. The best rule is simple geometry first, then gentle appearance tweaks that do not alter sampling math.

High resolution modes and interlaced outputs can break assumptions about integer multiples. Some SNES titles and certain 32 bit systems switch line counts in menus or special scenes, which can cause sudden jumps in letterboxing. When that happens, focus on vertical stability for gameplay and accept less perfect fill in menus. Another edge case is handheld rotation or tate modes for vertical shooters. In those scenarios, recalculate integer choices after rotation and test whether overlays still align with the viewport. If your overlay includes a shadow near the gameplay area, verify it does not encroach after aspect changes. Keep a per game override handy for outliers that ignore core norms.

User interface clutter can mask good scaling decisions. On screen notifications, achievements popups, or busy bezels draw attention to borders that would otherwise fade away. Trim notification duration and opacity in RetroArch so the focus stays on the playfield. When using overlays, ensure their inner edge remains neutral and avoids high contrast near the viewport boundary. If you are tuning many systems, document your integer picks and aspect choices in a simple note for future you. This reduces rework when updating cores or firmware. Pair that documentation with a clean Quick Menu habit so you can toggle integer scale and aspect in seconds during testing sessions.
