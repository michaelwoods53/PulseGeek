FAT32 vs exFAT for Miyoo SD Cards: Pick Safely

By Jared Butler • Published March 9, 2026

Choosing between FAT32 and exFAT on a Miyoo SD card comes down to reliability, file size needs, and cross device behavior. The format you pick shapes how RetroArch writes saves, indexes playlists, and recovers from power loss. I will weigh data integrity against convenience and explain where each file system helps or hinders typical handheld use. If you mainly store compact ROMs and value fewer surprises, one option stands out. If you push large PS1 or disc images and transfer files from modern desktops, the other may be justified with safeguards. The goal is not ideology but reducing failure modes while keeping workflows smooth.

Key takeaways

  • Prioritize reliability on Miyoo and default to FAT32 for daily play.
  • Use exFAT only when single files must exceed 4 gigabytes.
  • Reduce corruption risk with safe ejects and periodic card checks.
  • Match allocation size to workload to avoid needless write amplification.
  • Back up saves and states before reformatting or switching file systems.

Evaluation criteria and how to weigh them

Start with reliability as the primary criterion because handhelds see frequent sleep toggles and abrupt power offs. FAT32 uses a mature allocation scheme with widely tested repair tools, which often recovers directory issues after minor corruption. exFAT improves throughput and large file handling yet historically suffers more from unsafe removals on embedded devices. On Miyoo, where RetroArch writes small metadata and save files often, consistency beats peak speed. A practical rule is to choose the format that degrades gracefully when something goes wrong. If you must stretch capacity or speed, offset that choice with backups and conservative settings. Reliability is not only about file system design but also about the surrounding habits that stress or protect it.

Next consider file size limits because they gate which ROM images or archives you can keep intact. FAT32 caps single files at 4 gigabytes minus one byte, which is fine for most 16-bit and many PS1 titles but blocks some large disc images and compressed sets. exFAT removes that ceiling, letting you store multi-gigabyte CHDs or large eboots in one piece. The tradeoff is a higher sensitivity to sudden power loss on certain low-cost controllers, which can damage directory structures. If your library includes multi-disc PS1 dumps or multi-gigabyte DVD era assets, exFAT may be necessary, but plan for extra care and redundancy. Always check actual image sizes before assuming the limit is a blocker.

Compatibility across devices matters because you will move the SD card between Miyoo and a computer to manage content. FAT32 reads and writes across nearly all operating systems and adapters with minimal drivers, ensuring quick access on older laptops or game consoles. exFAT is broadly supported on modern Windows, macOS, and many Linux distributions when the proper packages are installed, though some older readers and firmware still fail to handle it correctly. If you use shared environments like school or work machines, FAT32’s ubiquity reduces friction. When you rely on current desktops and verified adapters, exFAT can be fine, but test mounts and eject behavior before trusting it. Compatibility reduces surprise downtime during maintenance.

Deep dives by attribute with tradeoffs

Reliability under imperfect shutdowns is the most practical concern for a handheld that sleeps and wakes often. FAT32’s directory structure and allocation behavior tend to leave fewer half committed states when writes are interrupted, which improves repair outcomes after an accidental battery drop. exFAT has matured on modern OSes, yet some low cost card readers and device firmware still handle metadata flushes less conservatively. The why is simple: embedded controllers vary and Miyoo devices are not servers with journaling file systems. If you choose exFAT for large files, mitigate risk with frequent save backups and strict safe eject habits. FAT32’s strength is not magic reliability, but predictable failure modes that tools can diagnose quickly.

File size limits decide whether you split or compress assets. FAT32’s 4 GB ceiling blocks a handful of PS1 or multi disc images that exceed it in raw or untrimmed form. You can often convert to CHD to reduce size or split archives into multi part sets, though that adds steps when managing playlists. exFAT removes the ceiling so you can keep a single large image intact, which simplifies library organization and reduces join operations during transfers. The tradeoff is exposure to corruption if transfers are interrupted. A good pattern is to prefer FAT32 if your actual library fits and only reach for exFAT when two or three specific titles truly require it.

Performance varies more with SD card quality than with file system choice, but patterns differ. FAT32’s overhead is modest for small metadata updates like RetroArch playlists and save files, which keeps menus responsive. exFAT can edge ahead on large sequential copies from a desktop, cutting minutes off a big refresh day. On device, load times for typical 8 or 16 bit games often converge because bottlenecks sit in core initialization and compressed assets. If you transfer frequently, exFAT’s throughput may save time, yet weigh that against the extra caution it demands. If you mostly play and infrequently sync, FAT32’s steady behavior will likely feel smoother over months of use.

Fit by scenario with explicit recommendations

If you value stability and your ROMs stay under 4 GB, choose FAT32 and pair it with disciplined backups. This combination tolerates the occasional unsafe power off better and keeps repair paths simple if the card misbehaves. To further reduce risk, enable automatic resume behavior in RetroArch so save states are current, then periodically copy the save folders to a computer. For continuity between play sessions without manual steps, you can also enable autosave and autoload. The theme is boring reliability. It is cheaper to prevent surprises than to recover after a directory goes missing.

If you have a handful of multi gigabyte disc images that cannot be trimmed under 4 GB, pick exFAT and tighten your process. Always eject safely on both the Miyoo and your desktop, avoid cheap adapters, and verify transfers before deleting originals. Keep a weekly copy of saves and states on a computer so a rare hiccup does not cost progress. When planning capacity upgrades, skim practical advice like best microSD advice on speed and capacity to avoid cards with inconsistent controllers. exFAT can work well when disciplined handling offsets its sensitivity to interruptions.

If you still cannot decide, run a short trial on a spare card with a realistic slice of your library. Use the same folder layout, play for a week, and compare transfer patterns as well as any anomalies in RetroArch scanning. Keep notes on save behavior, boot times, and whether playlists ever rebuild unexpectedly. For broader save hygiene, adopting workflows like safely copying saves and states to a computer gives you a safety net independent of file system choice. Decision clarity often emerges from hands on friction rather than theory, and a pilot reduces uncertainty.

Frequently asked questions

Can I switch from exFAT to FAT32 without losing data?

No. Reformatting erases data. Back up the SD card to a computer, verify the copy, then format to FAT32 and restore files afterward.

Does a 128 GB card work with FAT32 on Miyoo?

Yes. You can format 128 GB and larger cards to FAT32 with proper tools. The 4 GB limit applies to single files, not total capacity.

Which allocation unit size should I use?

For mixed ROM sets, 32 KB clusters balance small saves and larger images. If most files are tiny, 16 KB may reduce slack space slightly.

How do I reduce corruption risk on exFAT?

Always use safe eject on both devices, avoid low quality readers, and keep regular backups of saves and states before large transfers.

Looking ahead

The best outcome is boring predictability where saves persist and transfers feel routine. Whichever format you choose, standardize on safe ejects, occasional integrity checks, and a simple backup script so one mishap never costs progress. As your library evolves, revisit the few titles that push past 4 GB and adjust formats only when evidence demands it. If you extend into features like achievements or netplay, confirm how new cores write data and whether folder paths remain consistent. With a measured process and periodic review, your Miyoo stays dependable, and file system debates become background noise to steady play.
