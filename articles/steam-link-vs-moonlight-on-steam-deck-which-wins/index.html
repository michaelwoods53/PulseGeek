Steam Link vs Moonlight on Steam Deck: Which Wins?

By Jacob Reed • Published February 28, 2026

Choosing between Steam Link and Moonlight on Steam Deck hinges on latency, codec support, and control behavior under real LAN conditions. This comparison treats the decision like a build sheet, ranking what matters for smooth gameplay and reliable inputs. I focus on picture stability, encoder support from your host PC, and the setup steps that tend to introduce friction. By the end, you will know which option fits your network, GPU, and tolerance for tuning, and you will understand why that choice holds over time.

Key takeaways

  • Moonlight often edges latency when the host GPU supports NVENC well.
  • Steam Link integrates controller prompts and desktop navigation more smoothly.
  • Codec flexibility favors Moonlight for HEVC and AV1 on capable hosts.
  • Setup speed and simplicity usually go to Steam Link on Deck.
  • Evaluate your LAN stability and encoder support before committing.

Evaluation criteria and how to weigh them

Start with latency as the primary criterion because input delay shapes playability more than visual polish. On a wired LAN, aim for a glass to glass delay that feels under roughly a frame or two at 60 fps, which corresponds to a subjective threshold rather than a fixed number. For example, a fast-paced shooter exposes tiny encoder and decoder delays, while a turn-based game tolerates more slack. The tradeoff is that chasing the lowest delay can reduce picture stability if you undershoot bitrate or pick a codec your hardware cannot sustain. Weight latency highest for competitive games and slightly lower for slower genres, but never ignore it because spikes often hide configuration issues.

Next, weigh codec support because the encoder you can actually sustain beats the one that looks best on paper. If your host GPU supports NVENC or AMF at 1080p60 comfortably, HEVC can deliver cleaner edges at a lower bitrate than H.264, especially on detailed scenes. However, some networks or decoders handle H.264 more predictably at fixed frame pacing. AV1 is promising on newer GPUs, yet early decode paths or handheld thermals can add variability. A practical rule is to pick the codec that your host encodes in real time with headroom and your Deck decodes without throttling. Revisit this choice when drivers and firmware improve on both ends.

Finally, consider control integration and setup complexity because convenience affects daily use more than a one time benchmark. Steam Link benefits from native prompts and Big Picture navigation that reduce friction when launching games or adjusting volume. Moonlight, paired with Sunshine, grants granular control over bitrates and encoder flags, which suits tinkerers who want per game tuning. The tradeoff is time spent configuring host permissions, firewall rules, and game profiles. Assign moderate weight to integration if family members or guests will use the Deck, and heavier weight to configurability if you routinely test codecs, swap displays, or push higher refresh rates.

Deep dives per attribute with tradeoffs and concrete examples

Latency depends on encode speed, network jitter, and decode timing on the Deck. If your PC’s GPU has abundant encoder headroom, Moonlight paired with Sunshine can feel slightly snappier because it allows tuning smaller buffers when the network is clean. For instance, on a wired gigabit link with no background traffic, a lean buffer and HEVC at modest Mbps respond crisply to camera pans. The risk is that an occasional spike will show as a hitch if buffers are too aggressive. Steam Link tends to absorb minor jitter with steadier pacing, which feels safer on busy home networks where other devices contend intermittently.

Codec choice changes both clarity and thermals. HEVC generally delivers sharper results per megabit than H.264 for fine textures like asphalt or foliage, while AV1 can surpass both on the newest hardware. Moonlight exposes those options readily and can target AV1 when the host supports it. Steam Link supports mainstream codecs reliably but offers fewer toggles to force edge combinations. A caution is that higher compression complexity can raise host load, which in turn increases input delay during spikes. Measure host encoder utilization and ensure cooling keeps clocks stable before assuming a newer codec will always look better in motion.

Controller mapping and on screen prompts influence comfort more than many expect. Steam Link mirrors Steam’s input layers and Big Picture navigation, which keeps glyphs correct and menus predictable across titles. Moonlight accepts flexible mappings and can mimic gamepads well, but certain games may require per title tweaks to align dead zones or paddles. If your setup relies on Deck specific shortcuts or chorded actions, Steam Link reduces friction when switching games quickly. If you need custom profiles that carry unusual bindings or want raw mouse input from the couch, Moonlight’s flexibility becomes a strength, though it asks for a little setup time up front.

Setup and maintenance should scale with your patience for tinkering. Steam Link pairs quickly and keeps most configuration within Steam’s interface, which suits a living room where others may use the Deck without guidance. Moonlight requires installing Sunshine on the PC, allowing permissions, and confirming firewall rules, but then unlocks rich controls for bitrate targets, encoder selection, and per game overrides. If you value repeatable simplicity, default toward Steam Link and spend effort optimizing your host PC’s background tasks instead. For enthusiasts who iterate on settings weekly, Moonlight’s knobs repay the time by smoothing edge cases and enabling fine tuning across genres.

Fit by scenario with short, explicit recommendations

For a wired LAN with a modern NVIDIA GPU and stable drivers, choose Moonlight for the slight latency edge and codec breadth. Use HEVC at a bitrate your encoder holds without spikes, and reserve AV1 for hosts that maintain real time encode at your target resolution. If you notice occasional stutter during busy scenes, nudge buffer sizes upward before dropping resolution. As a sanity check, confirm frame times on the host are even and background tasks are quiet, because host load masquerades as network trouble when spikes align with encoding.

For mixed households or frequent Big Picture navigation, use Steam Link for smoother controller prompts and consistent menus. Pairing inside SteamOS shortens the path from wake to gameplay, which makes it friendlier when multiple people use the Deck. If visual clarity needs a boost, raise bitrate modestly and let Steam Link’s pacing ride through light congestion. When input feels sluggish, look to the host PC first and ensure GPU drivers, power plan, and focus mode keep encoder clocks steady rather than chasing codec changes prematurely.

For tinkerers on wired networks who want per game profiles and granular control, Moonlight with Sunshine is the better fit. Allocate time to set firewall rules and test codecs at your native display resolution, then freeze settings that keep frame pacing stable. If your results vary by title, scan for game specific overlays or capture hooks that interact with encoders and disable them. Conversely, if your goal is an appliance like experience that always boots into a familiar interface, favor Steam Link and optimize your LAN using a clean switch and wired connections to both host and Deck dock.

Looking ahead

Driver updates and new encoder hardware will keep shifting the small gap between Steam Link and Moonlight, so plan to retest after significant changes. Build a short checklist that validates wired paths, confirms encoder headroom, and spot checks two genres you play most. Keep notes on bitrate, codec, and buffer settings that produced stable frame pacing, then reuse those baselines when you troubleshoot new titles. If a future update introduces AV1 decode improvements or controller refinements on Deck, revisit your pick with the same criteria used here, and adjust only when the change proves consistent across several sessions.
