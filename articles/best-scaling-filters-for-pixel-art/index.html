Best Scaling Filters for Pixel Art

By Logan Pierce • Published January 21, 2026

Choosing scaling filters for pixel art is a timing decision as much as a taste call. I prioritized methods that preserve edges, avoid ringing, and behave predictably across displays. Each pick uses a clear example, then weighs performance and artifact risks. You will see how scaling and filters interact, where integer steps matter, and when hybrid approaches win. The goal is a repeatable path that translates across emulators and backends without guesswork.

Key takeaways

  • Prioritize integer ratios or edge-aware filters to retain pixel intent.
  • Check ringing and moiré after scaling, especially with sharp kernels.
  • Combine modest sharpening with gentle smoothing only when necessary.
  • Match backend and shader format for stable performance and output.
  • Test different masks or scanlines last to fine tune perceived clarity.

1) Nearest Neighbor

Nearest Neighbor is the baseline for pixel art because it replicates source pixels exactly, preserving hard edges without averaging. For a 256 by 240 game scaled to 5x, each source pixel becomes a perfect 5 by 5 block, keeping sprite outlines crisp and readable. On handheld or docked setups, this purity helps UI fonts and tile seams stay consistent across scenes. The tradeoff appears when the display cannot use an exact integer step, such as 1080p targets from 240p sources, where a fractional scale forces either uneven borders or a second pass that introduces shimmer on scrolling backgrounds. If you must fill the screen, add small black borders to retain whole-number multipliers rather than stretching. This method is nearly free on the GPU, so it is ideal for low power devices and quick comparisons.

Section highlights

  • Nearest keeps edges exact but demands whole number scaling.
  • Prefer small borders over fractional stretch to avoid shimmer.

2) Integer Scaling

Integer scaling enforces whole-number magnification so each source pixel maps to uniform blocks, preventing uneven columns or rows. A 4x scale of a 320 by 240 frame to 1280 by 960 keeps every tile aligned, then letterboxes within a 1080p display. In emulators like RetroArch, combine integer scale with aspect correction to avoid stretch while maintaining the original pixel grid. The compromise is screen real estate; you give up edge to edge fill for stability and clarity, which matters more for text-heavy RPGs or dense platformers. If you still want near full screen, consider a small upscale to the nearest integer plus a mild post pass such as limited radius sharpening. For a broader foundation on scaling tradeoffs and latency impacts, the evergreen reference on emulator concepts provides context in plain language across basics, latency, and shader choices.

Section highlights

  • Whole-number steps keep pixels uniform and prevent uneven columns.
  • Accept letterboxing to trade small borders for consistent clarity.

3) xBRZ

xBRZ is an edge-aware scaler that expands pixel art while smoothing diagonals and curves, reducing stair-stepping on character outlines. Upscaling SNES sprites 4x with xBRZ produces readable faces and rounded helmets that look hand drawn at a glance. It excels on cartoon assets with large color blocks and gentle gradients. The cost is a softened look that can blur intentional dither patterns or break the chunky charm of strict pixel grids. On low resolution fonts, legibility can suffer if the filter overblends strokes. To mitigate, pair xBRZ with integer scaling where possible, then adjust sharpness only slightly to recover edges without reintroducing jagged artifacts. If you plan to mix shaders, see a deep dive on scaling, mask tuning, and pipeline order so the scaler feeds clean data into later passes.

Section highlights

  • xBRZ restores curves but softens deliberate dithering and micro detail.
  • Use integer steps then light sharpening to regain edge definition.

4) HQx

HQ2x and HQ3x use pattern detection to interpolate smoother edges while retaining broad pixel regions, making them effective for low color sprites. For 2x to 3x upscales of 8-bit titles, outlines become less jagged and gradient areas blend more naturally than with nearest neighbor alone. However, these filters can misinterpret fine checkerboard shading as gradients, washing out intentional texture. They also have fixed scale factors, which limits flexibility when targeting modern resolutions. If you need freedom beyond 2x or 3x, consider ScaleFX or xBRZ that adapt to larger steps. When sharp UI is crucial, reserve HQx for backgrounds and use per-viewport overrides if your emulator supports them. For a comparison mindset on balancing clarity and comfort, this related guide on sharpness versus smoothing strategies outlines when softness helps readability.

Section highlights

  • HQx smooths edges at 2x or 3x but blurs dithering.
  • Choose adaptive scalers for larger steps or mixed resolutions.

5) ScaleFX

ScaleFX grows features by analyzing patterns across neighboring pixels, aiming to retain shapes without heavy blur. On Genesis platformers at 3x to 6x, it often keeps corners tight while smoothing only the necessary diagonals. This balance works well for tiles with both fine texturing and clear silhouettes. The tradeoff is computational cost and occasional edge overshoot that can create halos on high contrast borders. Limit the radius or choose a lighter variant when the GPU budget is tight. For users tuning pipelines, mind the pass order so ScaleFX precedes any scanline or mask effect. If you plan to change graphics backends, this comparison of Vulkan versus OpenGL for shader performance can help set expectations for throughput and compatibility across devices.

Section highlights

  • ScaleFX preserves shapes while moderating blur on diagonals.
  • Watch for halos and limit radius to control overshoot.

6) FSR with Modest Sharpening

FidelityFX Super Resolution in its spatial form helps when fractional scaling is unavoidable, letting you upscale then add restrained sharpening to recover perceived detail. On 720p handheld screens from 240p sources, a fractional step plus FSR sharpening near the low end of its scale can stabilize edges without harsh halos. The drawback is increased ringing on high contrast UI lines and possible flicker during scrolling if the sharpness is set too high. Keep sharpening conservative and test with panning scenes. If your emulator offers separate viewport and menu scaling, ensure only the game frame receives the filter. For deeper color accuracy while tuning perceived detail, consult guidance on SDR and HDR setup for correct brightness and tone mapping so sharpening does not exaggerate clipped highlights.

Section highlights

  • Use light FSR sharpening after fractional steps to lift detail.
  • Check for ringing and scrolling flicker before finalizing settings.

7) Lanczos Resampling

Lanczos is a sharp resampling kernel that preserves edge contrast well, making pixel boundaries appear crisp at non-integer sizes. For a 240p source scaled to 1080p, Lanczos can keep sprite outlines distinct compared to bilinear. The downside is ringing, visible as faint halos around high contrast edges, and moiré in fine textures. To minimize artifacts, reduce the lobes or blend with a small percentage of bilinear, effectively damping the kernel. If you are noticing patterned interference in tiled backgrounds, this explainer on ringing and moiré mechanisms can help diagnose whether the kernel or the content is at fault. Lanczos is compute friendly on most GPUs, but test at your target refresh rate because subtle ringing can become distracting during fast scrolls or flashing effects.

Section highlights

  • Lanczos keeps edges crisp but increases haloing and moiré risk.
  • Tame lobes or blend bilinear to damp ringing artifacts.

8) Mitchell–Netravali

The Mitchell–Netravali family strikes a middle ground between sharpness and smoothness, making it suitable when Lanczos is too aggressive but bilinear feels blurry. On 1.5x to 2.5x scales of pixel art, it maintains readable silhouettes while hiding minor aliasing along diagonals. Parameter choices matter; opting for softer coefficients reduces halos at the cost of slight blur. Use this kernel when you must fill a display without integer steps and want a predictable compromise. It will not preserve dither as faithfully as nearest neighbor and can soften small UI glyphs. In those cases, consider layering a mild sharpening pass after this resample, while keeping a close eye on ringing. For shader order guidance, see a primer on structuring pipelines and dependencies so post effects do not amplify interpolation artifacts.

Section highlights

  • Mitchell–Netravali balances crisp edges with reduced haloing.
  • Tune coefficients and add only light sharpening if needed.

9) Bilinear or Bicubic

Bilinear and bicubic are standard smoothers that hide stair-steps at non-integer sizes, often used as a last resort when fill is required. On 1440p monitors displaying 240p content, bilinear softens jaggies but sacrifices the crisp grid that defines pixel art. Bicubic is sharper than bilinear but can still blur thin text and HUD lines. The gain is predictability and speed across all hardware. If you adopt one of these, counterbalance with a tiny sharpening pass or overlay subtle scanlines to restore perceived structure. Be cautious, though, as too much sharpening creates halos that defeat the purpose. For a practical reference on when softening aids comfort versus clarity, this comparison of sharpening and antialiasing gives thresholds and pairing advice useful for mixed content.

Section highlights

  • Bilinear and bicubic smooth quickly but reduce pixel grid definition.
  • Apply minimal sharpening or scanlines to regain perceived structure.

10) Scale2x and EPX

Scale2x and EPX use straightforward rules to expand edges and fill gaps, producing believable shapes without heavy smoothing. For GBA sprites at 2x, these methods connect diagonal pixels cleanly and keep color boundaries intact. They are lightweight and easy to run on modest hardware, making them good defaults for portable emulation. The limitation is rigidity; beyond 2x or 3x, their assumptions break down, and fine dithering can be misinterpreted. If you must target larger displays, consider chaining a 2x EPX with an integer upscale to reach your resolution while preserving structure. When you want to add presentation flair after scaling, review curvature and bezel tradeoffs so decorative effects do not undermine clarity or performance budgets.

Section highlights

  • Scale2x and EPX are fast and connect diagonals convincingly.
  • Chain with integer steps for larger displays without distortion.

11) CRT-aware Prescaling

CRT-aware prescaling means upscaling to a clean integer before applying scanlines or masks, so the shader operates on stable blocks. For a 240p game, scale to 5x or 6x first, then let the CRT pass add phosphor simulation and beam width. This sequence respects the pixel grid while improving perceived detail and motion. The tradeoff is complexity and GPU cost, plus the risk of moiré if masks resolve poorly at your panel resolution. Keep mask strength conservative and preview multiple mask densities to fit your display’s subpixel layout. For fine tuning mask and scanline balance, this guide on dialing mask intensity and scanlines walks through parameters to avoid overbloom. If you are mixing overlays, test order and output size carefully to prevent compounding artifacts.

Section highlights

  • Prescale to clean integers before adding scanlines or mask effects.
  • Use modest mask strength to prevent moiré on modern panels.

Looking ahead

  • Audit a few favorite scenes with scrolling to validate filter choices.
  • Save presets per system, then fine tune sharpening for HUD legibility.
  • Measure GPU headroom while testing to keep frame pacing stable.
