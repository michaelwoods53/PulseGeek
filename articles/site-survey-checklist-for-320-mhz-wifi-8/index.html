Site Survey Checklist for 320 MHz WiFi 8

By Rachel Morgan • Published January 13, 2026

You are here to run a precise site survey for 320 MHz WiFi 8 and exit with defensible decisions. The checklist assumes indoor enterprise spaces, a dual 6 GHz capable analyzer, and at least one AP that can broadcast wide channels for test purposes. We will move from planning through preparation to execution, then validation and optimization so each step narrows uncertainty. Expect practical thresholds, repeatable walk paths, and notes on when to defer 320 MHz due to interference or client limits. If you follow the sequence, your documentation will stand up to peer review and your network will be easier to iterate.

Key takeaways

  • Start narrow, measure SNR, then justify 320 MHz with hard evidence.
  • Record AP power, width, and client support to bound expectations.
  • Use repeatable paths and timestamps to compare survey variants.
  • Flag DFS occupancy and AFC limits before enabling wide channels.
  • Validate roaming and MLO behavior with client logs and pcap traces.

Quick start checklist

  1. Define goals: document throughput, latency, and roaming targets per area.
  2. Baseline narrow: survey 80 or 160 MHz to gauge SNR and noise.
  3. Stage a test AP: enable 320 MHz on one AP with fixed power.
  4. Walk planned paths: capture RSSI, SNR, channel utilization, and airtime.
  5. Validate clients: test at least two WiFi 8 devices with MLO on and off.
  6. Compare logs: review maps, pcaps, and counters against targets.
  7. Decide width: keep 320 MHz only where evidence meets thresholds.

Plan the work

Start by fixing measurable objectives, because 320 MHz doubles capacity only when signal and airtime remain stable. Write down target throughput per user, acceptable latency under load, and minimum SNR at the edge of each zone. For example, a design might require 1 Gbps aggregate in a conference room and under 25 ms one-way latency during peak. Make thresholds realistic by scoping client capabilities, since many devices support 6 GHz but not wide rates at range. Accept that aggressive targets will shrink viable coverage for wide channels. Defining these bounds early helps avoid overextending 320 MHz where noise or density would degrade experience.

Map constraints before touching tools, since regulations and construction shape usable spectrum. Identify DFS regions and any Automated Frequency Coordination requirements for outdoor extensions or window-adjacent areas. Note wall materials, ceiling height, and reflective surfaces that influence multipath and signal decay. A simple rule is to treat heavy masonry and metal shelving as SNR tax that widens cell edges only on paper. Document hours when measurement is allowed to match typical interference. These observations feed decisions on where 320 MHz is even credible, and where narrower channels will protect roaming and airtime.

Choose a repeatable route and timing, because comparability makes survey data useful. Sketch primary paths through corridors and high-density rooms with start and stop points, then estimate the walking speed and dwell times at corners. Use consistent timing like two-second pauses at labeled waypoints to anchor later comparisons against alternative channel widths. Plan at least two passes per area, one with narrow baselining and one with 320 MHz test conditions. Expect tradeoffs if occupancy changes between passes. Timestamped photos of AP locations and obstacles add context to later analysis and reduce debate about why readings differed.

Prepare environment

Stabilize test conditions first, because inconsistent transmit power or background noise will mislead conclusions. Fix AP transmit power on the 6 GHz radio, disable auto-channel temporarily, and lock a single SSID to the test band. Begin with 80 or 160 MHz to create a baseline, then stage one AP to 320 MHz for the second pass. Ensure neighboring APs use non-overlapping segments or remain narrow to limit artificial contention. If your controller shows channel utilization, clear historical stats to avoid conflating old bursts. The goal is isolation so any changes you measure map to channel width and not unrelated dynamics.

Prepare a survey kit that logs the right fields, since 320 MHz success hinges on SNR and airtime. Use a 6 GHz capable analyzer or laptop with a supported NIC that exports RSSI, noise, SNR, BSSID, MCS, spatial streams, and channel utilization. Add a second device for throughput tests to avoid adapter bottlenecks. Calibrate timestamps across tools to correlate maps, logs, and packet captures. A power bank and labeled floor plan help maintain a consistent pace. Verify that data storage will not fill mid-walk. These small checks prevent gaps that would otherwise force a redo and skew your comparison.

Capture a short spectrum sweep before walking, because hidden interferers can invalidate wide channels. Do a two-minute listen on your intended primary channel and adjacent segments to spot persistent spikes. Note any DFS activity or non-802.11 signatures that might be microwave leakage or building systems. If utilization idles above a modest threshold during quiet periods, expect contention to worsen under occupancy and consider narrowing. This early glance avoids wasting time proving what interference already predicts. It also informs which segments to avoid when you later evaluate puncturing or segmentation strategies during deployment.

To make your preparation reproducible, log current radio settings via CLI so the survey can be repeated later with the same parameters. The following commands use common Linux tools to capture adapter details and a quick passive scan artifact for the work ticket.

Execute steps

Run the baseline pass first, because you need a control to judge 320 MHz. Walk your planned route with 80 or 160 MHz active, hold pace steady, and log RSSI, SNR, MCS, retries, and utilization. Take short iPerf or HTTP pulls at marked points to observe application-level throughput without saturating airtime. Keep transmit power fixed to avoid masking how width changes SNR distribution. Save a map export and raw logs with timestamps. This evidence lets you confirm whether a later 320 MHz pass delivers higher rates without unacceptable frame loss or drops in SNR at the edges of the same path.

Switch a single AP to 320 MHz for a second pass so you test a realistic hop without network-wide risk. Repeat the exact walk path and measurements, then add a roaming exercise between two APs if available. Monitor channel utilization spikes and note any increases in retries or latency. If throughput climbs at close range but SNR falls quickly with distance, mark the practical edge for wide operation. This data helps decide where 320 MHz is viable and where segmentation or narrower widths keep experience consistent during peak occupancy or voice usage.

Include at least two client perspectives, because implementations differ. Use one modern laptop and one smartphone that both support WiFi 8, then repeat short active tests with Multi-Link Operation enabled and disabled if possible. Record association timing, negotiated MCS, and roaming behavior between the same AP pair. If one device underperforms or refuses 320 MHz at midrange, note the trigger distance and SSID settings. These edge cases justify mixed policies by area or by device class later. A second perspective reduces bias from driver quirks and makes your recommendations more robust during validation with stakeholders.

Validate results

Evaluate SNR and edge performance against the thresholds you set, because raw peak throughput alone can hide problems. Create a side-by-side map showing SNR contours for narrow and wide passes, then mark the minimum acceptable SNR boundary. If your design target fails beyond a short radius on 320 MHz, plan narrower widths there and reserve wide channels for close-in coverage. An effective heuristic is to maintain a margin above your minimum SNR to preserve MCS under normal contention. Accept cases where 320 MHz serves only certain rooms, and document that scope explicitly to avoid surprises later.

Correlate channel utilization and retries with user experience, since subframe losses reduce goodput even when PHY rates look strong. Compare utilization histograms from both passes at matching timestamps. If the wide channel shows frequent utilization spikes or elevated retries during light occupancy, expect worse behavior when the space fills. Prefer narrowing or segmenting channels rather than raising power, which inflates contention domains. Where utilization remains modest and retries low, 320 MHz can provide substantial headroom for short-range high-throughput tasks without harming neighboring cells.

Review client behavior logs to confirm roaming and MLO benefits materialize in practice. Align association times, MCS negotiation, and steering events to your waypoints. If MLO reduces handoff gaps or stabilizes rate selection, note the conditions that enabled it. When clients show erratic links or drop to lower rates at midrange, verify antenna orientation and environmental factors before discarding 320 MHz. Use this evidence to recommend policy granularity by area type, such as keeping wide channels in labs and studios while reserving office corridors for narrower widths to optimize overall airtime fairness.

Troubleshoot and optimize

Treat persistent retries and poor SNR as signals to right-size width, because more spectrum does not fix path loss. If edge SNR falls below your safe margin, reduce to 160 or 80 MHz in those areas and retest. Adjust transmit power conservatively, prioritizing symmetry between AP and client capabilities to avoid sticky roaming. Consider moving the AP or reorienting antennas in reflective spaces. The tradeoff is between headline rates near the AP and reliable service across the intended footprint. Document each change and its effect so your final plan links settings to observed improvements.

Address interference methodically to protect airtime. If you recorded recurring spikes in specific subchannels, steer away using channel selection that avoids those segments. Where rules allow, evaluate preamble puncturing to route around narrow interference while keeping usable portions active. If utilization remains unpredictable, adopt narrower widths to localize issues and contain co-channel interactions. Resist raising power to fight noise, as it usually amplifies contention and worsens roaming. This approach keeps the network stable while you investigate the root cause through additional spectrum sweeps or targeted off-hours tests.

Standardize policy by space type so changes scale. Define where 320 MHz is permitted, where 160 MHz is preferred, and where 80 MHz remains the norm. Set controller templates for transmit power, width, and minimum data rates that match each area profile. Train operations to collect quick SNR and utilization snapshots during incidents so deviations can be recognized quickly. Finally, schedule periodic revalidation after layout changes or occupancy shifts. This cadence ties planning, DFS awareness, and client behavior to ongoing coverage quality so future surveys build on known good patterns rather than restarting from zero.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need 320 MHz everywhere?

No. Keep 320 MHz where SNR margins, utilization, and client support meet targets. Use narrower widths elsewhere to maintain roaming stability and predictable airtime.

What if my analyzer cannot capture 6 GHz?

Use a supported 6 GHz adapter or analyzer. Without it, measurements will miss critical SNR and utilization details and any recommendations for 320 MHz will be speculative.

How long should each walk take?

Keep a consistent pace and dwell pattern that fits the space. Two passes per area with brief stops at waypoints are typically sufficient to compare widths reliably.

Should I raise transmit power to fix edge rates?

Avoid increasing power to compensate for poor SNR. Higher power enlarges contention domains and often worsens retries and roaming behavior.

When is puncturing worth testing?

Test puncturing when interference is localized and rules allow it. If usable subchannels remain clean and client support is present, it can preserve airtime without full narrowing.

Looking ahead

Use the survey artifacts to drive a phased enablement that respects evidence. Start by turning on 320 MHz only in the rooms that met your SNR and utilization criteria, then observe production behavior during normal occupancy. Capture the same short snapshots weekly to confirm stability and drift. If results remain strong, expand to adjacent areas while keeping corridors and transitional spaces narrower to protect roaming. Revisit your templates after firmware updates or layout changes. This rhythm keeps your network aligned with real client behavior and simplifies maintenance because each change traces back to a documented, repeatable measurement.
