Tune Physics Materials: Friction and Bounce That Feel Right

By Ethan Palmer • Published January 1, 2026

Our goal is to tune physics materials so friction and bounce feel deliberate during play. You will set guardrails, run small experiments, and adjust parameters with purpose across your engine of choice. The workflow assumes access to common features like colliders, rigid bodies, and per-material properties, plus a basic scene with movable shapes and stable gravity. We will reference Unity as a concrete example while keeping the logic portable to Unreal, Godot, or Box2D. By the end, you will transform vague “too slippery” notes into friction and restitution targets that hold up under iteration and stress.

Key takeaways

  • Establish friction and restitution targets before touching engine sliders.
  • Prototype with bounded ranges, then converge using repeatable tests.
  • Validate on varied speeds, masses, and contact angles for coverage.
  • Prefer stable timesteps and CCD when bounce must remain reliable.
  • Measure distance and time, not vibes, for material tuning decisions.

Plan the work

Start by defining the feel you want in measurable terms, because physics materials respond best to targets, not wishful tweaks. Translate “sticky wall runs” into friction that sustains a 35 degree climb at 3 m/s for 2 seconds, or “springy crates” into a drop from 2 meters that rebounds to 1.2 meters. These statements anchor friction and restitution against distances and times you can record with gizmos or debug logs. The tradeoff is initial setup time, yet the payoff is fast convergence. If uncertainty remains, design two or three bracketing goals so you can decide between them empirically without reworking the scene or resetting your expectations midstream.

Next, list a small set of canonical interactions that represent your game’s core contacts. Include sliding on floors, bouncing off angled ramps, and stopping against walls. Attach numbers to each case, such as acceptable slide distance after a 5 m/s run or minimum rebound height for a ball dropped from 3 meters. The reason is simple: friction balances tangential motion while restitution handles normal impulse transfer, and both respond differently to speed and angle. A compact suite of three to five cases keeps you focused. The limitation is coverage, so plan to extend cases later if new mechanics appear.

Finally, choose coarse parameter ranges to explore so you avoid wandering through noise. For dynamic friction, start around 0.2 to 0.6 for floor materials that allow controlled movement, with static friction 10 to 30 percent higher when you need stickier starts. For restitution, try 0.1 to 0.6 for believable bounce on wood or rubber analogs, noting that values near 1 can amplify numerical errors. These are starting windows, not universal truths. The mechanism is straightforward: you screen wide, then narrow. The edge case is stylized physics, where exaggerated values might be appropriate but will likely demand additional damping or a tighter timestep to maintain stability.

Prepare environment

Stabilize the simulation before touching materials, since friction and bounce only behave predictably on a steady foundation. Use a fixed timestep in engines that support it and ensure gravity, solver iterations, and collision layers are consistent across test scenes. For example, if you double solver iterations for ragdolls, your rebound heights may rise slightly due to tighter contact resolution. The practical choice is to standardize baseline settings, then tune materials. Also add simple instrumentation: a drop rig with exact heights, a ramp with a known angle, and a level runway. The tradeoff is reduced spontaneity, but the benefit is repeatability and cleaner comparisons across builds.

Create test assets that isolate variables, because mixed influences hide the effect of material changes. Use simple shapes like cubes and spheres with matched mass and scale. Apply one physics material at a time and avoid compounding by stacking materials through multiple colliders unless that mirrors production use. If the engine supports per-contact overrides, disable them for baseline passes. The reasoning here is controllability: you want to attribute an observed slide length or bounce height to the current material only. Edge cases include compound colliders for characters or vehicles, where distribution of mass and geometry will also shape contact response.

Document your test naming and logging so results are easy to compare. Prefix runs with material name, friction values, and restitution, and log measured outcomes like slide distance or peak height. Color-coded gizmos or debug lines make it quick to verify that start points and angles are correct across attempts. The why is momentum: small bookkeeping now compounds into faster iteration later, especially when others join the tuning. Execute steps

Implement material changes in small increments and observe results immediately, because rapid feedback keeps you on track. In Unity, set dynamicFriction, staticFriction, and bounciness on a PhysicMaterial, then assign it to relevant colliders. Keep changes within your planned ranges and update one axis at a time. If your engine supports combine modes, test average versus minimum or maximum since the counterpart material at contact can sway the outcome. The tradeoff is slower single-variable experimentation, yet it avoids false correlations. Validate results

Validate by measuring outcomes against your initial targets, since numbers settle debates. For sliding tests, record stop distance after a timed push at a set speed. For bounce, compute rebound ratio by dividing peak height by drop height. A simple rule of thumb: if measured rebound is 0.6, your effective restitution under current conditions is roughly 0.6, assuming minimal energy loss elsewhere. Differences between theory and practice can stem from solver iterations, shape convexity, or contact smoothing. The why is clarity: measuring turns opinions into adjustments. For a deeper background on collisions, rigid bodies, and stability, reference the broader overview of collisions, bodies, joints, solvers, and stability.

Compare material combinations to ensure interactions remain believable when different surfaces meet. A rubber ball on concrete should rebound higher than on carpet, even with identical drop heights. If your engine offers combine modes for bounce and friction, average often yields intuitive middle-ground outcomes, while maximum can exaggerate the highest of the two. Choose based on your design intent and consistency needs. The tradeoff is predictability versus flair: average is safer for systemic play, maximum can highlight special surfaces. Measure both setups and pick the one that best preserves your intended rebounds and slide distances under mixed contacts.

Document final values and the test conditions that justify them so future changes do not regress behavior. Note the material pair, contact angle, speed, object mass, and measured results. When a designer later requests a slipperier floor or livelier crate, you can adjust friction or restitution with a clear expectation of what the numbers will do. This improves communication and speeds iteration across teams. Include a simple table with the chosen ranges and the intended uses to reduce ambiguity. Quick start checklist

  1. Lock the timestep: choose a fixed step and keep it consistent.
  2. Build simple rigs: create a drop tower, ramp, and flat runway.
  3. Set coarse ranges: pick starting friction and bounce windows to explore.
  4. Assign via code: apply materials predictably to all test colliders.
  5. Measure outcomes: log rebound height and slide distance for each run.
  6. Converge deliberately: adjust a single parameter until targets are met.

Troubleshoot and optimize

If sliding or bouncing feels erratic, first verify timestep and collision detection settings, because numerical issues can masquerade as bad materials. Fixed timesteps reduce variability, and continuous collision detection helps when fast objects tunnel and skip contacts altogether. If you must keep variable timesteps, clamp frame-to-frame deltas and increase solver iterations moderately. The tradeoff is performance cost, but you gain repeatability. Another edge case involves very light or heavy masses that magnify rounding errors or overwhelm impulse limits. Scale your masses into a reasonable band and adjust gravity if needed so contact impulses resolve within stable ranges for the engine’s solver.

When wall friction stops movement too abruptly, reduce static friction or change friction combine modes to avoid extreme pairings. Alternatively, add a small tangential damping term through drag or a custom material that models directionality, which is useful for ladders or conveyor belts. For bounce that dies too quickly, raise restitution slightly and check if angular velocity is stealing energy via spin. If the engine supports per-axis or per-contact refinements, use them sparingly so material behavior remains comprehensible. Keep notes for exceptions, since special materials can erode mental models if they proliferate without clear naming and documentation.

For tuned behavior under production conditions, profile costs of solver iterations and continuous detection, then choose the cheapest path that preserves your targets. Consider switching to average combine modes for widespread surfaces and reserving maximum or minimum for set pieces where a distinct feel is desired. If you are deciding between rigid body movement and alternative control models, review movement systems to ensure material tuning aligns with character or vehicle handling. Frequently asked questions

Why does my object slide forever even with high friction?

Check that both contacting materials have appropriate friction and that combine mode is not set to minimum. Verify the timestep is fixed and mass is within a reasonable band. Confirm the collider is not marked as a trigger.

Why is my bounce inconsistent across frame rates?

A variable timestep can alter impulse resolution. Use a fixed timestep or clamp delta time. Consider increasing solver iterations and enable continuous collision detection for fast bodies to avoid missed contacts between frames.

Should static friction always be higher than dynamic friction?

Usually yes, because starting motion typically requires more force than maintaining it. Exceptions exist for stylized effects or directional surfaces. If you invert them, document the intent and test stability under ramps and slopes.

Why does increasing restitution cause jitter at rest?

High restitution can amplify tiny penetrations into repeated bounces. Lower bounce on resting surfaces, increase sleep thresholds, or add slight damping. Ensure penetration resolution and solver iterations are adequate for your shapes.

Do different shapes need different material values?

Often yes. Spheres roll and transfer energy differently than boxes or capsules. Start with shared values, then offset per shape to hit targets. Validate with identical tests so changes reflect geometry effects, not random noise.

Looking ahead

You now have a repeatable method to set friction and bounce that match explicit targets, with guardrails for stability and measurement. Extend your suite with corner cases like wet surfaces, conveyor belts, and steep ramps that stress tangential forces. Then roll those values into production prefabs and document intended uses so designers can pick materials like they pick colors. As systems evolve, revisit ranges and tests to confirm your choices still hold under new mechanics. With small, durable steps, your physics tuning becomes a predictable craft rather than a guessing game, and players feel the difference immediately in every contact.
