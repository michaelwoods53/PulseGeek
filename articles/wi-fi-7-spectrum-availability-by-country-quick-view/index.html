Wi‑Fi 7 Spectrum Availability by Country

By Allison Grant • Published January 29, 2026

Use this quick view to assess Wi‑Fi 7 spectrum, availability, and country constraints without reading every regulation. We chose items that repeatedly affect deployment choices across bands, then added decision cues you can apply today. Each point translates policy into practical steps for planning and operations.

Key takeaways

  • Availability differs by band and power class, not just by country.
  • AFC readiness controls outdoor and standard power use in 6 GHz.
  • PSC alignment speeds discovery and reduces roaming misses in 6 GHz.
  • Regional rules change channel width choices and reuse strategies.
  • Certification timing lags can limit device features after spectrum opens.

1) Availability tiers that actually matter

The most reliable way to read a country matrix is to group availability into tiers that mirror deployment choices. A practical set is Open, Limited, and Constrained. Open means 2.4 and 5 GHz are routine and 6 GHz Low Power Indoor is allowed across broad channels. Limited means 6 GHz is partially open, often with reduced channel widths or building attenuation assumptions. Constrained means the 6 GHz band is closed or experimental, so Wi‑Fi 7 plans rely on 2.4 and 5 GHz. For example, planning a university network in a Limited region might prioritize 80 MHz in 5 GHz while staging 6 GHz for indoor only. The tradeoff is that coarse tiers hide national nuance like sub‑band exclusions or temporary notices, which you must confirm against regulator bulletins before finalizing channel plans.

These tiers also help scope feature expectations for 802.11be Extremely High Throughput features without overpromising. In Open environments, you can assume wide channels and predictable Preferred Scanning Channels which enable fast discovery. In Limited cases, you might cap channel width and emphasize spatial reuse or multi‑link operation to offset narrower spectrum. In Constrained countries, focus on clean 5 GHz reuse and load management while you track filings. As you refine, use a structured checklist to ask whether Low Power Indoor is permitted, whether standard power requires coordination, and which sub‑bands are off limits. The compromise is simplicity versus precision, but the approach reduces rework during early design.

Section highlights

  • Map countries into open, limited, and constrained to guide designs.
  • Verify sub‑band exceptions and notices before final channel selection.

2) Six gigahertz LPI versus standard power

The decisive question in 6 GHz is whether Low Power Indoor is available and whether standard power needs Automated Frequency Coordination. LPI enables indoor deployments without external coordination but limits EIRP and disallows outdoor use, which still delivers high throughput in controlled spaces. Standard power supports larger cells and some outdoor links but typically requires AFC authorization to protect incumbents. A stadium example shows the split. Suites and concourses can thrive on LPI with dense AP placement while adjacent outdoor plazas may demand standard power coordinated through an AFC system. The tradeoff is operational overhead. LPI simplifies turn‑up with lower range while standard power grants coverage at the cost of coordination workflows and possible denial areas where incumbent services need protection.

When planning, create two RF designs that share structure but diverge on power class so you can swap if policy shifts. In the LPI design, assume shorter cell radii, more APs, and strict building loss models that favor 80 or 160 MHz where feasible. In a standard power design, model AFC grant boundaries and minimum separation to incumbents, then tune channel reuse. For fine discovery behavior, align beacons to Preferred Scanning Channels so clients find 6 GHz quickly. Expect to update designs as regulators clarify test methods or extend indoor allowances.

Section highlights

  • Choose LPI for simple indoor throughput, standard power for larger cells.
  • Model AFC boundaries and maintain PSC alignment for faster discovery.

3) AFC readiness and operational impact

A country matrix is only useful if it tracks whether AFC is available for standard power and what that means for operations. AFC adds a request and grant loop where access points provide location and parameters to receive a permitted power and channel list. In practice, this drives provisioning changes, inventory choices, and back‑office integrations. For instance, an outdoor retail campus might stage APs with GPS or survey coordinates and integrate the controller with an AFC provider. The upside is access to higher power where allowed. The tradeoff is latency to approval and occasional denials near incumbents, which require fallbacks to LPI indoors or alternate channels. For deployment planning, document grant refresh intervals and the retry posture when responses are delayed or revoked.

Operational teams benefit from a visible state machine that shows each AP’s coordination status. Track states like Unregistered, Pending Grant, Granted, and Revoked, then alert on transitions that reduce coverage. Inventory should separate LPI‑only radios from standard‑power‑capable models to avoid misplacements.   • Separate LPI‑only and standard‑power inventory to avoid misplacements.

4) Channel width and PSC alignment

Country differences ripple into viable channel widths and discovery behavior, so you should plan widths and beacon placement together. In 6 GHz, clients scan Preferred Scanning Channels to find APs quickly, then learn about wider channels through signaling. If a region restricts certain sub‑bands, you might still deploy 80 MHz by stitching permitted segments, but discovery will suffer if beacons are not on PSC points. A corporate campus example illustrates this. Two 160 MHz blocks produced high throughput but intermittent roaming until PSC alignment corrected initial probe hits. The downside to wide channels is reduced reuse in dense buildings, which can raise contention. In Limited countries, capping at 80 MHz with careful PSC placement often improves median rates without sacrificing stability.

Implement a rule of thumb that each SSID beacons on PSCs first, then evaluate wider operation through measurements rather than assumptions. In dense floors, map 40 or 80 MHz reuse patterns and ensure client capability distribution justifies the width. In regions where 6 GHz is unavailable, translate the same discovery discipline to 5 GHz by minimizing non‑PSC beacons and avoiding DFS turbulence when possible.   • Beacon on PSC first, then validate wider operation with measurements.
  • Prefer 80 MHz in dense spaces to balance reuse and stability.

5) Incumbent protection and indoor bounds

Country matrices often compress a complex idea into a simple icon: protect incumbents. In practice, that means honoring indoor limits, observing exclusion zones, and staying within emission masks. Indoor rules usually assume building attenuation to shield incumbents, while outdoor use needs coordination or remains disallowed. A hospital example shows the nuance. Radiology suites with heavy walls support LPI well, yet rooftop links risk excessive egress. The tradeoff is that conservative assumptions reduce surprise interference but can raise cost through more APs and tighter reuse. When countries publish special testing or mask updates, validate that firmware and regulatory databases on your APs reflect those changes so your emissions remain compliant as definitions evolve.

Because rules vary across regions, teams should maintain a small playbook that maps indoor constraints to design choices, such as lowering maximum EIRP, preferring internal antennas, and disabling outdoor profiles when a site sits near protected services. Where rules are explicit about spectral masks or adjacent channel limits, double‑check device conformance in release notes.   • Translate indoor limits into EIRP, antenna, and profile choices.
  • Verify firmware reflects current masks and any sub‑band exceptions.

6) Certification and device enablement lag

A country can legalize spectrum while devices still ship with disabled features, so plan for certification and firmware lag. Regulatory databases, SAR evaluations, and conformity assessments must align before vendors enable new bands or power classes. A common example is a country that opens 6 GHz LPI, yet enterprise APs require a compliance update and client drivers need region files before the band is usable. The benefit of waiting for approvals is predictable behavior and fewer audit risks. The tradeoff is delayed throughput gains. To mitigate, run pilot areas with staged firmware and monitor client populations to confirm that enabling 6 GHz helps more than it harms. Keep change windows ready because enablement often arrives in batches.

Procurement should specify support for target bands and note that availability depends on both national rules and device certifications. Track enablement at three layers: hardware capability, regulatory domain mapping, and operational software toggles. Where a vendor documents timelines for new band activation, schedule lab trials ahead of campus‑wide rollouts. For broader context on the 802.11be amendment and how features integrate with the base standard, use this clause‑aware standard overview to align expectations with what firmware can lawfully expose. This avoids promising 320 MHz or certain EHT features in regions where the spectrum or certifications do not yet support them.

Section highlights

  • Plan for lag between regulation, certification, and firmware enablement.
  • Stage pilots and track hardware, domain, and software readiness.

7) Multinational roaming and policy alignment

Organizations with cross‑border footprints should design for policy variance rather than assume uniform behavior. The safest posture keeps SSID sets stable worldwide but varies band enablement and channel widths per country. A practical example is a manufacturing group that disables 6 GHz in countries without LPI while running it in offices where it is allowed, keeping authentication and QoS identical. The upside is consistent user experience with country‑specific RF layers. The limitation is extra template complexity and the need for thorough validation. Ensure roaming lists avoid channels that are illegal in the destination country and keep country codes accurate to prevent clients from advertising unsupported capabilities.

Operational alignment includes platform guardrails that prevent accidental use of forbidden bands after config replication. Implement checks that block 6 GHz profiles from applying in disallowed regions and log any attempted overrides. Where DFS applies, pre‑test radar detection to prevent roaming interruptions and maintain channel availability maps.   • Keep SSIDs consistent while varying band and width by country.
