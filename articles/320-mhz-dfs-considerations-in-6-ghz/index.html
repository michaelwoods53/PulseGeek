320 MHz DFS Considerations in 6 GHz

By Rachel Morgan • Published March 1, 2026

Planning 320 MHz operation in 6 GHz demands rethinking DFS, because classic radar detection applies to 5 GHz while 6 GHz relies on other controls. The key is translating DFS-style risk management into AFC, indoor power rules, and client discovery behavior. That translation matters since 320 MHz magnifies both capacity wins and exposure to unclean spectrum. We will outline how AFC constrains standard power outdoors, why Low Power Indoor limits change link budgets, and how Preferred Scanning Channels affect association time. We will also examine puncturing and Multi Link Operation as tools that patch gaps but shift airtime math. By aligning expectations with real constraints, you avoid wide channel settings that look fast on paper yet degrade fairness and roam stability.

Key takeaways

  • 6 GHz lacks DFS, so 320 MHz planning centers on AFC and LPI.
  • Preamble puncturing salvages width but reduces effective spectral efficiency.
  • Client discovery on PSC channels impacts association time and roaming.
  • Wide lanes amplify noise floor sensitivity and require careful density.
  • MLO stabilizes throughput but depends on both links staying viable.

DFS in 6 GHz: what changes for 320 MHz

The first principle is that 6 GHz does not use DFS, yet planners still face incumbent coexistence through different mechanisms. Standard power devices depend on an Automated Frequency Coordination service that authorizes channels and power, which can exclude parts of a 320 MHz span. Low Power Indoor operation avoids AFC but uses stricter power spectral density, so link budgets shrink and retries climb in marginal rooms. The practical impact is that wide channels turn small margins into big airtime taxes. For example, a lightly attenuated neighbor can push contention enough to erase headline PHY gains. Treat 320 MHz as a performance multiplier that also multiplies exposure, and ask whether your space offers consistently clean 6 GHz across the full bonded width.

The second concept is discovery. 6 GHz clients typically probe or listen on Preferred Scanning Channels to reduce dwell time, then evaluate beacons and Reduced Neighbor Reports to find full service sets. If your 320 MHz primary lands outside a PSC, some clients delay association or miss it during fast roams. A simple mitigation is selecting a primary that aligns with a PSC while preserving clean bonding. The tradeoff appears when the cleanest center frequency does not map neatly to discovery norms. In that case, weigh slightly less clean RF on a PSC primary against slower discovery on a quieter non PSC primary. The decision hinges on your roam frequency, device mix, and tolerance for first join latency.

A third shift involves puncturing and Multi Link Operation. Preamble puncturing lets an AP signal unavailable subchannels inside a bonded width, allowing transmission on the remaining parts. This salvages airtime when a slice is busy but reduces effective spectral efficiency and complicates scheduling. MLO can pair 6 GHz with 5 GHz or another 6 GHz link to balance flows, smoothing out traffic when one link sees interference. The limitation is that both features lean on client support and vendor implementations, which vary. Plan for graceful degradation rather than assuming perfect salvage. If your baseline design only works when puncturing and MLO are active, it will likely underperform in mixed fleets or after firmware changes.

Planning framework for wide 6 GHz channels

Start with a cleanliness threshold rather than a throughput target. A workable rule is reserving a margin where your measured noise floor and co channel duty leave at least one third airtime headroom during peaks, because 320 MHz inflates collision domains. Validate by sampling busy hours using AP counters and a spectrum view, then stress test with parallel large flows. When that headroom is not available across the full width, step down to 160 MHz and add spatial reuse through more cells. The tradeoff trades single link rate for efficiency and fairness, which often raises net capacity. Use wide channels only where the spectrum is consistently calm and where client support is broad enough to benefit.

Next, align primaries with discovery and with a bonding map that avoids partial overlaps. Build your channel plan so PSC aligned primaries are used for high mobility SSIDs, while stationary or IoT SSIDs can tolerate non PSC primaries if the span is cleaner. Cross check against a region agnostic 6 GHz layout to confirm your 320 MHz grouping does not straddle known hot zones. This avoids brittle designs that look fine in lab baselines but erode under mixed clients and building acoustics.

Finally, assign roles to puncturing and MLO that match their limits. Configure puncturing policies to prefer stable subchannel masks rather than chasing short spikes, since constant mask changes increase management overhead and client confusion. Treat MLO as a stabilizer that pairs a wide 6 GHz link with a narrower anchor when density or interference rises. The risk is starving latency sensitive frames if scheduling assumes consistent dual link quality. To reduce that, cap queue depths per link and set thresholds that revert to single link when loss or contention exceed a strict bound. This keeps behavior predictable across firmware updates and heterogeneous devices.

Examples and short scenarios

Consider an open office with moderate AP density and mixed clients. Spectrum scans show occasional external energy in one 80 MHz slice within your preferred 320 MHz block. Leaving 320 MHz intact invites frequent puncturing, which complicates scheduling and reduces net efficiency. A better approach sets the primary on a PSC within the cleanest half and evaluates 160 MHz during busy hours. If client distribution favors laptops with strong 6 GHz radios, 160 MHz often equals or beats a punctured 320 MHz in delivered throughput. The why is simple: fewer collisions, steadier MCS, and faster discovery outweigh headline PHY rates. Reserve 320 MHz for a smaller quiet zone such as executive rooms.

Now evaluate a stadium concourse with short dwell users and bursty uplinks. The wide collision domain of 320 MHz increases backoff times and punishes latency sensitive control frames. Even with AFC clearance, transient noise from broadcast gear or cameras can intermittently foul subchannels, pushing constant mask changes. A design that fixes 80 or 160 MHz with more cells and directional antennas performs more predictably. If the venue insists on showcasing top line rates, pair an MLO SSID where the 6 GHz link is wide only during off peak and the 5 GHz anchor carries retries when contention spikes. Set strict thresholds so the system falls back quickly when quality dips.

For a small lab with controlled RF and a homogenous device fleet, 320 MHz can shine. Here, AFC authorizations are stable, walls help isolate cells, and administrators can align primaries to PSCs without sacrificing cleanliness. Enable 320 MHz on a subset of APs that serve bandwidth heavy tasks while keeping a conservative 160 MHz SSID for roaming and collaboration areas. Document expected behavior and retest after firmware updates, because vendor changes to puncturing heuristics or MLO scheduling can shift performance. Plan for incremental trials where you expand wide channels only after verifying airtime headroom and roam outcomes.
