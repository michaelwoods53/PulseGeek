Shader Performance on Miyoo: Which to Use

By Austin Price • Published January 14, 2026

Picking the right shader on Miyoo is a balance between performance, style, and input latency. This list focuses on lightweight effects that preserve smooth frame pacing and responsive controls while still improving clarity. Items were chosen by how reliably they hold target framerate, how they interact with scaling, and how easy they are to configure. You will see practical guidance on which shader to use, what a safe baseline looks like, and how to avoid common stutter pitfalls.

Key takeaways

  • Start with no shader to validate performance, pacing, and latency.
  • Prefer single pass shaders that maintain 60 FPS on Miyoo.
  • Use integer scaling and sharp-bilinear to reduce shimmer.
  • Light CRT and LCD looks beat heavy multi pass pipelines.
  • Test with frame pacing and audio sync to catch regressions.

1) Start without shaders to set a baseline

Begin with no shader to confirm performance headroom and stable latency, because every effect adds cost that obscures root causes. For example, disable video_shader_enable in RetroArch and verify your core holds its native target, like 60 FPS for most 2D systems, while input feels crisp and audio remains free of crackle. This establishes whether any micro-stutter stems from sync or rendering instead of post-processing. The tradeoff is aesthetic loss, since raw output can look jagged or soft on modern displays. Still, the clarity of a clean test is invaluable. Once baseline stability is proven, you can layer a single pass shader confidently, knowing any regression likely comes from that change. Use this step to also check that vertical sync and frame pacing are correct before styling.

A baseline test pairs well with checking frame pacing and sound alignment to avoid chasing ghosts. Run a game that normally exposes issues, pause on a scrolling scene, and observe whether motion is even and audio remains steady. If pacing falters or tearing appears, address sync before adding any shader. A good reference is to review guidance on frame pacing fixes that covers vsync, FPS limits, and audio sync ordering to reduce stutter risk, such as the article on mastering frame pacing steps. The tradeoff is spending time upfront instead of tweaking visuals immediately, but it prevents masking sync defects under cosmetic changes that would later complicate troubleshooting.

  • Validate framerate, latency, and audio before adding any visual effect.
  • Fix pacing issues first to avoid masking problems with shaders.

2) Use sharp-bilinear for clean integer scaling

Sharp-bilinear is a strong first shader because it keeps edges crisp while staying nearly as fast as no effect. The idea is simple: do integer scaling to avoid shimmering and apply a tiny linear blend on the fractional remainder so vertical or horizontal scrolls look steady. For example, set integer scale on and use a single pass sharp-bilinear preset that produces clean pixel edges on handheld screens without ringing. The tradeoff is that extreme upscales or unusual aspect ratios may still show slight blur on non-integer axes. Why it works is efficiency: the filter performs minimal sampling per pixel, so GPU cost remains low and input latency impact is small, making it reliable for most 8-bit and 16-bit cores where consistent 60 FPS matters.

Testing sharp-bilinear is straightforward and reveals whether your scaling path is bottleneck free. Load a sprite-heavy scene with horizontal panning, then toggle integer scaling and the shader on and off to inspect shimmer, especially on repeating patterns like brick walls or text. If you notice timing hiccups or inconsistent frame delivery, revisit sync and limiter settings to stabilize cadence with safe limits explained in the guide on reliable FPS limiting. The tradeoff is that sharp-bilinear does not add CRT-like warmth or LCD grid texture, so if you want a nostalgic look, choose a lightweight mask next, knowing your scaling foundation is solid and low risk.

Section highlights

  • Sharp-bilinear preserves edges while remaining nearly costless to render.
  • Confirm integer scaling to stop shimmer before trying aesthetic masks.

3) Pick zfast CRT mini for a light CRT look

Choose a one pass zfast CRT variant when you want gentle scanlines without tanking performance. For instance, presets labeled zfast-crt or guest mini tuned for low-power hardware can provide subtle line structure and gamma shaping while staying close to the cost of simple filters. Try a conservative strength so dark scenes remain readable and bright areas do not band. The tradeoff is authenticity versus speed: heavy masks with subpixel shadow apertures look richer but can exceed budget on handheld GPUs. The strength here is predictable overhead, which makes these presets dependable across cores. You get a classic flavor with minimal configuration and only a small latency impact compared with multi pass pipelines that mix bloom, curvature, and complex mask math.

Evaluate the visual impact on high contrast content and scrolling text to check for moiré and flicker. If the mask introduces uneven brightness or amplifies dithering, dial down line intensity or switch to a lighter profile. Keep vsync steady and do not push frame delay aggressively while testing a new mask, since the combined timing can nudge audio buffering into crackle on tight budgets. If tearing or cadence oddities appear while experimenting, compare the advice in the piece on choosing the right vsync option to keep latency manageable and visuals stable. The tradeoff is that faint masks may look too subtle outdoors or on dim screens, so pick a middle ground you can see in various lighting conditions.

Section highlights

  • Use single pass CRT variants for gentle lines and steady framerate.
  • Tune line strength to avoid moiré, flicker, and brightness loss.

4) Choose LCD grid lite for subtle subpixel texture

Lightweight LCD grid shaders add a faint subpixel texture that reduces flatness without heavy cost. A one pass lcd-grid-lite or similar preset can simulate pixel structure gently, which helps soften banding and gives sprites a cohesive look at modest scale factors. For example, enable the grid at low opacity and pair it with integer scaling so the pattern aligns predictably as the camera moves. The tradeoff is potential moiré on diagonal lines if grid size mismatches the output resolution. Keep opacity and cell size conservative, and avoid curvature or bloom layers that balloon GPU work. The benefit is consistency across systems, since the shader does not depend on core resolution tricks or overscan and barely affects input timing.

To verify stability, watch a diagonal scrolling scene where patterns quickly reveal misalignment. If you see interference patterns, slightly adjust grid scale or disable the effect on that core. Shimmer often signals fractional scaling, which sharp-bilinear can mitigate, but start by ensuring integer scale is active to prevent compounding artifacts. If audio clicks appear when toggling the grid, that points to tight latency margins rather than the grid math itself. Reassess sync and buffering using practical tuning steps explained in the guide to a safe performance stack, then return to visual polish. The tradeoff is acceptance that perfect subpixel mimicry is unrealistic on low power devices, so prioritize a subtle texture that stays out of the way.

Section highlights

  • Light LCD grids add texture cheaply when scaled at integer factors.
  • Reduce opacity or size if diagonal moiré appears during scrolling.

5) Keep to one pass presets to protect latency

Favor one pass shaders because each additional pass compounds cost and increases the chance of missing frame deadlines. On Miyoo-class hardware, a single pass often keeps you at full speed while two or more can introduce micro-stutter on busy scenes. For example, choose sharp-bilinear or zfast CRT in a single pass rather than stacking bloom, curvature, and mask layers. The tradeoff is less cinematic flourish, but you protect responsiveness and audio stability. Technically, fewer passes mean fewer texture samples and lower bandwidth per frame, which reduces worst-case spikes. That steadier envelope gives frame pacing features a fair shot at delivering even cadence without relying on frameskip. It also makes runahead or frame delay adjustments less risky if you decide to trim latency later.

When assessing whether a second pass is feasible, test the heaviest scene you play and watch for small jitters that appear every few seconds. Those spikes often correspond to memory or sampling bursts when effects overlap. If you must add a second layer, keep it mathematical simple and avoid dependent texture lookups. Also confirm vsync and limiters are not fighting by matching the output target with core timing. If uncertainty remains, compare options in the overview about picking the right vsync method under different cores, such as the discussion in which vsync choice fits latency goals. The tradeoff is accepting modest visuals in exchange for predictable control feel during demanding moments.

Section highlights

  • Single pass shaders minimize sampling cost and reduce timing spikes.
  • Test worst cases before adding any second layer or effect.

6) Avoid curvature and bloom on low power hardware

Disable screen curvature and bloom because they tend to be the largest cost multipliers relative to visual value on a small display. Curvature warps geometry and forces more texture lookups, while bloom blurs then blends scenes, stressing bandwidth. For example, a two pass CRT pipeline with curvature and glow might look impressive in screenshots but can subtly desynchronize frame delivery during action. The tradeoff is losing spectacle, yet you gain consistent input feel and a quieter cooling profile. The why is straightforward: these effects scale with resolution and scene brightness, producing variable GPU load that confuses pacing. On handhelds, that variability is more harmful than a constant low overhead effect that preserves timing predictability across bright and dark content.

If you still want a hint of depth, simulate it cheaply by adjusting gamma or applying a light vignette at low intensity within a single pass preset. Watch HUD elements and high contrast UI for ringing or delayed response when toggling heavy effects. If audio buffers start to underrun while bloom is active, the pipeline is too costly, and you should revert to a leaner look. For a reliable tuning order that preserves stable cadence, revisit a practical stack focused on sync and buffering prior to visuals, like the safe path laid out in the article on stutter-free tuning. The tradeoff is stylistic restraint, but handheld screens rarely showcase elaborate post effects as well as a steady, clean image.

  • Curvature and bloom create variable load that disrupts steady cadence.
  • Prefer gamma tweaks or vignette within a single efficient pass.

7) Save per core presets and keep toggles simple

Create per core shader presets so each system uses a stable, tested configuration that respects its timing. For example, save sharp-bilinear for 8-bit cores and a light zfast CRT for 16-bit cores, both in single pass form. This avoids global settings that might fit one core but stutter in another with different output resolution or latency budget. The tradeoff is managing a few more files or menu entries, but you gain reproducibility and faster switching during play. The why is alignment: core-native timing and resolution interact with scaling and shader cost, so keeping presets scoped ensures consistent pacing. Document which effects are safe and the conditions they were tested under, like integer scaling settings and whether runahead or frame delay is active.

Applying presets through a config file reduces menu friction and prevents accidental multi pass layering. The goal is a minimal profile that can be toggled quickly for A/B testing on a demanding scene. Keep the preset readable so you can adjust a value rather than stack another pass. The tradeoff is less spontaneity, but a small stable set of profiles makes it easy to share and reproduce results.

Frequently asked questions

Do shaders increase input lag on Miyoo?

They can, especially multi pass effects. Single pass options like sharp-bilinear or light zfast CRT usually add negligible delay if the device maintains framerate. Test worst case scenes to confirm no dropped frames.

Which shader is safest for performance?

No shader is safest. Among effects, single pass sharp-bilinear is a reliable starting point, followed by light CRT or LCD grid presets. Avoid curvature and bloom if you notice any pacing instability.

Should I use integer scaling with shaders?

Yes. Integer scaling reduces shimmer and makes mask patterns stable during scrolling. If aspect constraints force fractional scale on one axis, keep the shader lightweight and consider sharp-bilinear to smooth only the remainder.

Why does audio crackle when I enable a shader?

Crackle often indicates missed frame deadlines or tight buffers. Reduce shader cost, verify vsync and framerate limits, and increase audio buffer slightly. If crackle persists, test again with the shader off to isolate the cause.
