Key takeaways

  • Wide channels amplify interference and require disciplined placement and power control.
  • Client drivers and antennas often cap real throughput more than router silicon.
  • Dual 6 GHz radios enable cleaner roles for backhaul and client access.

1) Dual 6 GHz radios for clean backhaul

Prioritize routers with two discrete 6 GHz radios when targeting 320 MHz because role separation reduces self-interference and keeps client airtime stable. Consider a setup where radio A anchors a 320 MHz dedicated backhaul to a satellite while radio B serves clients at 160 MHz during peak hours. In testing, this pattern preserved modulation on client links while the backhaul absorbed high burst throughput. The tradeoff is cost and power draw, plus more tuning tasks like aligning channel groups and transmit power. In small dwellings, a single 6 GHz radio may outperform dual units due to lower noise and simpler steering. Use dual 6 GHz only when you can maintain distance or attenuation between nodes to prevent coupling that flattens SINR.

This design benefits from channel planning across the U-NII bands, particularly when your environment has partial incumbents or neighbors that constrain edges of the spectrum. For example, you might pin the backhaul on a high channel block that stays quiet and allow the client radio to use dynamic 160 MHz with puncturing to navigate local interference. The downside is fragmentation if clients that advertise 320 MHz cannot maintain that width under load. You must also confirm your router firmware supports independent channel widths per radio. If not, both radios can inherit suboptimal settings, causing wider channels to collide with client retries and reduced effective throughput.

2) 320 MHz with pragmatic fallbacks

Adopt 320 MHz only with guardrails that define when to retreat to 160 MHz or narrower, because graceful degradation saves sessions during contention. A workable rule is to fix thresholds for retry count, airtime utilization, and MCS collapse. For instance, when aggregated retries exceed a safe range during busy periods, dropping to 160 MHz can regain SNR margins and reduce collisions. The limitation is that frequent flips confuse clients and erode throughput averages. You need timers and hysteresis so changes feel deliberate, not jittery. Also, wide channels increase noise bandwidth, so uplink from low-power phones is more fragile. A narrower channel often restores reliability for uploads like video calls while costing peak download speed that only a few clients can actually use.

3) Client reality check before 320 MHz

Validate that your clients can benefit from 320 MHz because device tiers and drivers often limit realized rates more than router silicon. Picture a home office with several 2x2 laptops and many 1x1 phones. The laptops may reach high MCS at 160 MHz while phones stall uplink on 320 MHz due to low transmit power and antenna constraints. The tradeoff is clear. Chasing headline PHY rates for a minority of devices can degrade experience for the majority. Inventory radios and drivers, then map expected PHY to throughput under typical RSSI. When in doubt, try staged rollouts to a subset of rooms. If the 320 MHz segment creates more retries than wins, pare back and spend effort on placement and attenuation instead of width.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need 320 MHz if most clients are 1x1?

No. Most 1x1 phones gain little from 320 MHz and may upload less reliably. Start at 160 MHz for access and consider 320 MHz only for a dedicated backhaul where signal quality is consistently strong.

Will 320 MHz hurt coverage in larger homes?

It can. Wider channels increase noise bandwidth and reduce SNR margins, which makes uplink more fragile. For larger spaces, plan more nodes at moderate power or keep access at 160 MHz while reserving 320 MHz for backhaul.

How do I know when to step down from 320 MHz?

Use thresholds for retries, airtime utilization, and MCS drops. If these exceed your baseline for a sustained period, step down to 160 MHz and recheck stability and latency before widening again.

Does MLO always improve 320 MHz performance?

Not always. MLO can shift some traffic to a cleaner link, but if both links are congested or poorly placed, benefits shrink. Gains depend on client capability and careful channel planning across bands.
