How to Measure MLO Performance in EHT Networks

By Courtney Lawson • Published March 4, 2026

Measuring MLO in EHT networks starts with a narrow goal and clean controls. Define whether you care about aggregate throughput, latency under load, or resilience when one link degrades. Your plan should map KPIs to mechanisms, which means relating multi‑link aggregation and scheduling to observable counters, streams, and captures. Assume you can use iperf3, a packet capture tool, and an EHT capable AP and client with MLO enabled. Include a baseline single link reference so improvements are attributable to multi‑link operation rather than noise. This article assumes lab access or a quiet window on a production cell, with adjustable transmit power and channel choices. With a structured approach, you can measure MLO performance credibly and avoid misleading numbers.

Key takeaways

  • Design scenarios that isolate MLO gains against a single link baseline.
  • Control RF, client state, and traffic patterns to keep runs comparable.
  • Track throughput, latency, and loss with synchronized clocks and notes.
  • Validate multi‑link activity using captures and device counters together.
  • Diagnose deviations by correlating airtime, retries, and CPU bottlenecks.

Plan the work

Start by defining the question your MLO test must answer, then pick matching KPIs. If you want to confirm aggregate throughput, choose TCP goodput at the application layer and include retry and airtime counters to interpret changes. If your aim is latency stability, combine one sustained flow with short probe bursts and record percentile delay. Tie each KPI to a scenario like dual band aggregation across 5 and 6 GHz, or rapid steering during interference. The tradeoff is scope creep, which dilutes test time and weakens conclusions. Limit to two or three scenarios and one baseline per scenario. Planning tightly aligns measurement to mechanism, making results trustworthy instead of anecdotal.

Next, document assumptions about devices, firmware, and features that affect multi‑link behavior. Record AP model and software build, client driver version, channel widths, and whether features like OFDMA or power save are on. Write planned settings so you can revert or repeat. Include a change log template with time, setting, and rationale. The edge case to consider is hidden toggles that vendors expose under advanced tabs or CLI, which can skew MLO scheduling or link selection. Ask how each setting could bias results and decide whether to freeze it or vary it intentionally. This discipline protects against misattributing gains to multi‑link when a background feature did the heavy lifting.

Finally, define acceptance thresholds that distinguish noise from signal. For throughput, a rule of thumb is to require repeated improvements across three runs within a narrow variance band, such as under 5 to 10 percent spread between trials. For latency, compare not only the mean but the 95th percentile, since tail delay reveals scheduling dynamics. Establish abort conditions like excessive retries or CPU saturation that invalidate a run. The tradeoff is time, as more repeats and strict thresholds slow progress. The payoff is confidence that observed improvements reflect real MLO benefits rather than luck or transient RF. Clear thresholds make your stop or proceed decisions defensible.

Prepare environment

Create a stable RF environment so differences come from MLO, not interference. Choose clear channels in 5 and 6 GHz if your region allows, and set transmit power low enough to keep test devices close while minimizing reflections. Position the AP and client with line of sight at a fixed distance, and mark placements to maintain geometry across runs. If field noise intrudes, schedule runs during quiet periods and log detected neighbors. The limitation is real spaces rarely stay still, so capture a spectrum snapshot before and after each run to catch surprises. Environmental control reduces random loss and makes trends attributable to multi‑link scheduling rather than chaotic RF shifts.

Standardize traffic generation and clock synchronization across tools. Use iperf3 with fixed parameters for stream count, window size, and test duration, and sync clocks using NTP or a manual reference so timestamps align with captures. Place the iperf server on wired backhaul to prevent uplink bottlenecks, and confirm the host has CPU headroom. The edge case is virtual machines that throttle timers or networking under load. Prefer bare metal or allocate dedicated cores. Consistent traffic and time alignment let you compare trials fairly and correlate events like steering or link pauses to observed metrics without guesswork.

Enable observability on both AP and client, aiming for counters and captures that reveal multi‑link state. Collect per link RSSI, MCS, retries, and airtime if exposed, and prepare a packet capture setup on a monitor interface or adjacent sniffer that supports EHT. Avoid mirroring across a switch that hides wireless metadata. When capture tools fall short, compensate by reading device logs for link switches or MLD identifiers. A tradeoff exists between deep capture and privacy or storage constraints, so rotate files and filter to test MACs. Sufficient visibility lets you confirm when both links are active and how schedulers split traffic.

Execute steps

Execute tests in a strict order that builds credible comparisons. Begin with a single link baseline using the strongest band at a chosen width, then repeat with the alternate band. Next, enable multi‑link operation and run the same traffic pattern, keeping every other variable constant. For resilience, introduce controlled impairment on one band, such as reduced power or a known interferer, while observing aggregate throughput. The tradeoff is time spent resetting conditions, but this structure prevents conflating gains with drift. When relevant, include an upstream bottleneck check by running a parallel wired test. Orderly execution establishes causality between MLO and observed performance rather than relying on anecdotes.

To keep runs consistent, document an execution script with exact parameters and durations. Record iperf3 flags, stream counts, and whether you measure uplink, downlink, or bidirectional flows. Store a run sheet with start times, test IDs, and notes on any deviations. If the device supports toggling features like OFDMA or specific MLO policies, change only one variable per batch. The limitation is human error during repetitive sequences, so use simple automation where possible. This operational discipline ensures you can reproduce outcomes and explain differences across days or firmware updates without resorting to speculation.

Validate results

Validation means confirming that both links were active and contributed as expected. Inspect captures for multi‑link device identifiers and per‑link sequence behavior, then compare with AP or client counters that report per‑band airtime and retries. If aggregate throughput rises while one band shows minimal airtime, you may be seeing scheduling overhead without true aggregation. Conversely, balanced airtime with rising retries suggests interference rather than MLO efficiency. The limitation is inconsistent counter availability across vendors, so correlate multiple signals rather than relying on a single metric. Cross‑checking artifacts proves that observed gains map to real multi‑link use, not coincidental changes elsewhere in the stack.

Compare measured results against the baselines using consistent statistics. For throughput, compute median and interquartile range across three or more runs to limit outliers. For latency, inspect 95th or 99th percentile to spot tail regressions that averages hide. Where differences approach variance bands, do another batch to tighten confidence. If your measurements disagree with expectations, revisit environmental notes and configuration diffs before re‑testing. The tradeoff is extra time spent, but it is cheaper than drawing poor conclusions that misguide deployment settings. Statistical discipline helps distinguish meaningful multi‑link improvements from random drift or measurement artifacts.

When ambiguity remains, augment tests with a focused variant rather than more of the same. For example, keep total offered load fixed while disabling one band to see the direct impact on throughput and tail delay. Alternatively, constrain MLO policy to a single TID and verify scheduler behavior on that stream. Each variant should answer one uncertainty and then be retired. The risk is chasing edge cases that matter only in the lab, so prioritize variants that mirror real client mixes and bandwidth demands. Targeted validation trims uncertainty while keeping your measurement program aligned with production realities.

Troubleshoot and optimize

When results underperform, break the problem into RF, device, and traffic causes. If retries spike on one band, reduce channel width or adjust power to improve SNR, then rerun. If CPU or thermal limits appear on the client, cap streams or lengthen duration to stabilize bursts. For traffic mix, vary the number of parallel flows and note scheduler responses. The edge case is asymmetric backhaul that caps overall throughput, so verify wired capacity with a direct test first. Systematically isolating each layer shortens time to a fix and avoids blind tuning. Effective troubleshooting turns disappointing trials into actionable guidance for production settings.

For ongoing tuning, capture lessons in a playbook that maps symptoms to actions. Document when to enable or relax features like OFDMA scheduling, power save parameters, or link policies that favor reliability over peak rates. Where documentation is unclear, run small A/B tests under the same harness and compare numbers. The tradeoff is that aggressive tuning can reduce interoperability in mixed device fleets. Prefer changes that are reversible and validated across multiple client types. A living playbook helps teams repeat good outcomes as firmware evolves, while keeping risk contained through controlled, measured adjustments.

Frequently asked questions

How do I verify that both links are active during a run?

Check for multi link identifiers in captures and compare per band airtime or retries from AP or client counters. If one band shows negligible airtime, the scheduler may be idling that link.

Why does throughput rise but latency tails get worse with MLO?

Aggregation can increase queue depth across links. Measure 95th percentile latency and review per band retries. Tuning stream count or enabling scheduler options that prioritize smaller frames can help.

What iperf3 settings should I change first when results are flat?

Increase stream count modestly after confirming CPU headroom, then extend duration to reduce variance. Change one parameter at a time and keep a single run sheet for traceability.

How many repeats are enough for confidence?

Run at least three trials per scenario and compare medians and interquartile ranges. If variance remains high, add runs or stabilize the environment before drawing conclusions.

Could wired backhaul limit my measured gains?

Yes. Verify wired path capacity with a direct test. If the backhaul saturates below expected aggregate throughput, upgrade or bypass it before interpreting MLO results.
