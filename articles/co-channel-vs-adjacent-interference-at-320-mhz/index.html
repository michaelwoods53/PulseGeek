Co-Channel vs Adjacent Interference at 320 MHz

By Rachel Morgan • Published January 9, 2026

Ultra wide 320 MHz channels deliver throughput, yet interference defines whether that speed is usable. Here we separate co-channel behavior from adjacent-channel effects at 6 GHz, then map both to practical design choices. The focus is on WiFi 8 planners balancing 320 MHz benefits against real client densities and control of reuse domains.

Key takeaways

  • Co-channel overlap at 320 MHz raises contention more than noise floor.
  • Adjacent leakage depends on spectral masks and receiver selectivity limits.
  • Preamble puncturing reduces overlap but increases scheduling complexity.
  • Use spatial reuse, TPC, and careful placement to bound interference.

Concepts and definitions

Co-channel interference occurs when two access points or clients share the same 320 MHz channel and contend for the medium, which elevates airtime competition rather than raw noise. The key effect is increased backoff and reduced transmission opportunities, especially for legacy devices that cannot coordinate with modern scheduling. For example, two APs on an identical primary channel with overlapping cells will trigger more clear channel assessments and longer queues. The tradeoff is that reuse can still work when cells are spatially separated by walls or distance, while a single open hall magnifies contention. Understanding this difference matters because contention collapses multi link operation gains by forcing links to idle, whereas pure noise would simply degrade throughput in a more linear way.

Adjacent-channel interference arises when energy from a 320 MHz transmitter leaks into a neighboring channel, or when receivers have imperfect selectivity and cannot fully reject near signals. At 6 GHz, spectral masks constrain out-of-band emissions, yet the very width of 320 MHz places edges close to neighbors, raising the risk of adjacent-channel power ratio issues. A scenario is two APs on side-by-side 320 MHz blocks in an open space where clients sit at the boundary. The benefit of side-by-side planning is more theoretical channel count, but the limitation is that practical receiver filtering and aggregate power can still raise error rates. Designers should treat adjacent placement cautiously unless walls or distance provide solid isolation that keeps effective interference below harmful thresholds.

Preamble puncturing lets radios carve out unusable subblocks inside a 320 MHz allocation, steering around known interferers while keeping the remainder active. This helps when a small portion of the band is noisy or reserved, which is more common in mixed environments and near incumbents. The tradeoff is scheduling fragmentation, because radios must coordinate resource units and clients must support the feature for benefits to materialize. A good rule of thumb is to puncture when the affected bandwidth is modest and localized, such as a narrow UNII segment, but to step down channel width when wide segments are impacted. This mechanism limits co-channel overlap by avoiding contaminated subcarriers, yet it cannot solve cases where entire reuse domains are colliding on the same primary.

Frameworks and decision lenses

A reliable lens is to evaluate domain size, isolation, and client mix before committing to 320 MHz reuse. Start with physical separation, because walls and distance reduce both co-channel contention and adjacent leakage more effectively than tweaks alone. For instance, a 25 to 30 meter separation with absorptive partitions often enables safe same-channel reuse, while open atriums rarely do. Next, examine client capabilities, since MLO devices tolerate contention differently than single link clients. The tradeoff appears when chasing headline throughput in a mixed estate, because one slow client can dominate airtime fairness. When isolation is weak or clients are uneven, prefer fewer reuse instances at 320 MHz, or limit width to 160 MHz on selected radios until measurements show acceptable airtime availability.

Control-plane settings become your second lens, with transmit power control and primary channel placement shaping who hears whom. Lowering EIRP on 6 GHz often pays off, shrinking contention domains while preserving close-in capacity for modern clients. Set primaries to avoid aligning at boundaries where crowds gather, reducing collisions on the portion most devices use for preamble detection. Examples and short scenarios

Office floor with mixed devices is a common test of 320 MHz realism. Imagine three APs along a corridor feeding glass meeting rooms and cubicles with partial absorption. Keeping two APs on the same 320 MHz domain with reduced EIRP can work if rooms sit between them and the third AP sits on a distant block. The tradeoff is conference rooms filling with BYOD devices that do not support MLO, pulling airtime toward one primary. In that case, moving only the third AP to 160 MHz can reduce adjacent pressure and stabilize retries. Event venue with line-of-sight seating shows the limits of adjacent placement. Two 320 MHz channels side by side along the bowl edge can degrade due to receiver selectivity when patrons sit at boundaries. A safer pattern is alternating same-channel sectors separated by aisles and elevation, with EIRP trimmed to keep cells tight. The downside is fewer distinct channels, but real airtime improves because devices see a single clear domain rather than two leaky neighbors. If wireless microphones or other incumbents appear in a slice, puncturing a subblock preserves service without a full width change. Reevaluate after doors open, because human bodies add absorption that may let you relax thresholds or expand a segment once the crowd settles.

Warehouse aisles with racking demonstrate when 320 MHz can shine. Tall metal shelving creates isolation that naturally bounds co-channel domains along each aisle, enabling repeated use of the same wide lane if primaries face down the aisles. The tradeoff is roaming behavior at aisle ends where signals converge, which can elevate contention unless power is shaped and APs are offset. In such settings, keep a clean primary away from aisle intersections and consider reduced widths only at crossings. This setting also benefits from strict change control, because moving one AP a few meters can undo isolation created by shelving. Logs that track retries and MCS drop events will confirm whether geometry or settings are driving improvements after tweaks.

Looking ahead

The next phase is to formalize validation loops that prove 320 MHz remains beneficial as the environment changes. Start by defining acceptable airtime utilization and retry ceilings for peak periods, then measure them after each change. A baseline could target sub 40 percent airtime during busy hours and stable MCS distributions across critical areas, recognizing that exact thresholds vary by application mix. The tradeoff is spending more time instrumenting, but it avoids reacting to single metrics like RSSI. This approach also lets you compare co-channel and adjacent risks over time, since rising reuse sensitivity often shows up as jitter and queue growth before obvious drops in link rate appear.

Plan for seasonal or event-driven variance that can flip which interference dominates. Open offices may lean toward co-channel contention on normal days, then swing toward adjacent leakage during all hands meetings when bodies shift isolation. Build profiles for these states and store radio templates that you can apply during change windows. The risk is configuration drift if teams make ad hoc edits, so centralized policies matter. Consider scheduling verification jobs that scan for primary alignment or power creep, because small deviations at 320 MHz have larger footprints. Keeping these guardrails codified ensures repeatability, especially when multiple administrators share responsibility across sites and time zones.

Finally, raise the bar on design documentation to include assumptions about isolation, client capabilities, and acceptable reuse counts. Record why a 320 MHz choice was made and what fallback path exists if interference grows, such as stepping selected radios to 160 MHz or engaging puncturing on impacted segments. This clarity accelerates incident response because the team knows which knob to turn first. As WiFi 8 clients adopt more MLO and improved filtering, revisit plans to reclaim width where safe. Treat 320 MHz as a tool to be validated regularly rather than a permanent setting, so throughput stays high without inviting fragile behavior as environments evolve.

Frequently asked questions

Does co-channel or adjacent interference hurt 320 MHz more?

Co-channel contention usually impacts airtime more, while adjacent leakage can raise errors near boundaries. The dominant issue depends on isolation, power, and client filtering in your specific layout.

When should I use preamble puncturing at 320 MHz?

Use puncturing when interference is narrow and localized so most of the 320 MHz block remains clean. If large segments are affected, step down width or replan reuse to avoid complex scheduling.

Is lowering transmit power helpful at 6 GHz?

Yes, trimming EIRP often shrinks contention domains and reduces adjacent stress while preserving close-in capacity. Validate with surveys and logs because too little power can create roaming or hidden nodes.

Are side-by-side 320 MHz channels safe in open spaces?

They are risky in open areas because receiver selectivity and aggregate power can raise errors. Use partitions or distance for isolation, or prefer bounded same-channel reuse with carefully reduced EIRP.

Do MLO clients eliminate interference problems?

MLO improves scheduling options but does not remove contention or leakage. Benefits depend on both ends supporting features and on clean primaries. Poor isolation or misaligned power still reduces gains.
