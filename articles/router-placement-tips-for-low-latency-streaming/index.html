Router Placement Tips for Low-Latency Streaming

By Jacob Reed • Published February 12, 2026

These router placement tips focus on practical wins for low latency streaming on a home LAN. Each tactic uses a measurable change like height, line of sight, or channel isolation and then validates impact with repeatable tests. Selections prioritize changes most likely to reduce jitter or stabilize frame delivery on Steam Deck remote play without requiring exotic hardware or firmware.

Key takeaways

  • Line-of-sight and mid-height placement reduce reflections and multipath delay.
  • Distance from metal and appliances cuts interference and improves stability.
  • Orient antennas toward the play area to strengthen beamforming focus.
  • Favor 5 GHz near the room and measure results with repeatable pings.
  • Test one variable at a time and log latency distributions, not averages.

1) Mount mid height with clear line of sight

Place the router near the center of the play area at mid height to minimize path loss and reflections that inflate latency. A bookshelf or wall shelf roughly chest level builds a clearer Fresnel zone than floor or ceiling corners, which introduce multipath echoes. For example, moving a unit from a TV cabinet to a shelf at 1.2 to 1.5 meters often stabilizes round trip times during Remote Play. The tradeoff is aesthetic and cabling constraints because central mounting might require longer Ethernet runs or visible wiring. The benefit is reduced retransmissions because fewer obstacles like furniture edges or appliance cases scatter the signal. Think of distance and obstruction counts as the two knobs you can adjust without changing gear.

Line of sight does not have to be perfect to be useful. Even partial visibility from the router to the Steam Deck reduces blockage by dense materials like brick or water-filled objects such as aquariums. A quick test is to stand where you play and visually confirm the router is not hidden behind a sofa, stacked books, or a TV chassis. In an apartment with limited outlets, move the router forward on a cabinet so the front edge clears the TV backplane. The compromise is dust and curious pets, but airflow is better and antennas radiate freely. You will often see fewer ping spikes because the direct path dominates rather than blurry reflected paths.

Section highlights

  • Mid-height placement clears obstacles and reduces multipath-driven latency spikes.
  • Accept longer cables to gain stability from a clearer signal path.

2) Keep distance from metal, mirrors, and appliances

Separating the router from large metal surfaces and appliances cuts reflections and conducted noise that drive jitter. Microwaves, refrigerators, and mirrored wardrobes all alter radio propagation and can detune antennas. As a rule of thumb, keep at least 1 meter from big metal objects and 2 meters from active microwaves during play. For example, relocating a router from atop a metal PC case to a wooden shelf often tightens latency percentiles, not just the average. The tradeoff is cable routing because moving away from a media center may shorten HDMI or power slack. Still, the payoff is consistent packet scheduling since the radio sees fewer sudden swings in signal-to-noise ratio when a door opens or a compressor clicks on.

Mirrors and glass act like frequency-dependent reflectors that produce delayed copies of packets. When these delayed copies overlap the main path, the receiver performs equalization that sometimes fails under movement, producing stalls. A practical placement is a few steps laterally offset from any full-length mirror and away from wall-mounted TVs with metal frames. In tight spaces, a small wooden riser that lifts the router above a metal shelf reduces coupling into the chassis. The limitation is decor and mounting hardware because some apartments restrict wall changes. Even with limits, small offsets and nonconductive spacing blocks reduce destructive interference without buying new radios.

Section highlights

  • Distance from metal and glass lowers reflections that inflate latency.
  • Use wooden shelves or risers to reduce coupling into conductive surfaces.

3) Orient antennas to match seating and beamforming

Antenna orientation and beamforming alignment concentrate energy where you play, improving minimum signal quality and lowering retransmits. For dual external antennas, set one vertical and one horizontal to improve polarization diversity for handheld devices that rotate during use. In a living room, aim the face of a flat router toward the couch so the internal array points at the seat. As an example, rotating the chassis by 30 degrees can lift the weakest percentile latency during head turns. The tradeoff is pattern bias because focusing energy forward can slightly degrade rooms behind the router. If you need coverage across rooms, test small angle changes while watching tail latency rather than only peak throughput, since matches in polarization matter more for real-time traffic.

Many routers expose options like explicit beamforming and MU-MIMO. Enable them and then validate on the couch where the Steam Deck rests during play. If the device rests on a lap, its orientation changes subtly with posture, so mixed antenna polarizations prevent deep nulls. A counterexample is mounting the router flat under a metal TV stand, which blocks beams and degrades side lobes. If you have only internal antennas, placement still matters. Rotate and tilt the chassis in small increments until latency spikes become rarer in a one minute ping test. The limitation is that some firmware presents limited controls, so you rely on physical orientation and furniture layout to guide energy.

Section highlights

  • Mixed antenna orientations protect against device rotation and polarization mismatch.
  • Rotate the chassis toward seating and confirm improvements with tail latency.

4) Prefer 5 GHz in-room, reserve 2.4 GHz for range

Use 5 GHz for in-room streaming because it offers wider channels and less congestion, which reduces latency variance when the path is short. For a Steam Deck within one room of the router, 80 MHz at moderate power is often ideal, trading raw speed for stable airtime. If walls are thick or distances long, let 2.4 GHz serve discovery and control while gameplay stays on 5 GHz. As an example, placing the router in the same room and steering the device to 5 GHz can cut spikes during scene changes. The tradeoff is sensitivity to obstructions, so 5 GHz suffers more when the router is hidden. Validate with a simple moving walk test while streaming, watching for bursty frame drops as you step behind furniture.

Band choice is only part of the story because channel planning determines interference exposure. In condos, pick a 5 GHz channel that neighbors use less rather than chasing the highest number. If your router supports DFS channels and your clients handle them, they can lower contention but may occasionally vacate due to radar events. That move causes a brief pause, which is a tradeoff you must accept for cleaner air the rest of the time. Section highlights

  • Use 5 GHz in the same room to reduce contention and jitter.
  • Let 2.4 GHz handle range where walls and distance dominate.

5) Center the router in the room, avoid corners

Corners create strong reflections and trap heat, both harmful for steady latency. Placing a router near the room center shortens average path length to likely seating positions and reduces the number of wall intersections a packet crosses. For example, moving from a corner entertainment center to a stand near the sofa often reduces variance during fast camera pans. The tradeoff is household traffic flow and aesthetics because central stands can be inconvenient. A small side table with cable clips offers a compromise that preserves mid-height and line of sight. Corners also accumulate dust that can raise device temperature, which in turn can cause radio throttling and buffer buildup under load.

Room center does not mean exact geometric center. Aim for functional center relative to where streaming occurs. If you play near a window or balcony door, position the router slightly behind your typical seat to keep the direct path unobstructed by your body. Humans are mostly water and attenuate 5 GHz, so placing the router where your torso does not block it during leaning or turning helps. The limitation is power outlet availability and Ethernet access to the modem. In those cases, run a longer Ethernet cable to the router and place a small surge protector under the side table to keep cabling tidy while keeping the device off the floor.

Section highlights

  • Corners increase reflections and heat, raising latency variance under load.
  • Use a side table near seating to balance access, cables, and signal paths.

6) Test each placement with repeatable latency checks

Validate placement changes with short, consistent tests so you do not rely on impressions. A simple method is to run a one to two minute ping from the Steam Deck or a nearby laptop to the router and to the game host, then note average and the worst five percent. For example, pinging both the gateway and the PC offers insight into radio airtime versus LAN queuing. The tradeoff is that ping is an approximation because ICMP handling differs across devices. Still, percentiles track spikes that translate into stutter. For a deeper end-to-end view, combine ping with an on-screen frame time overlay during Remote Play and move the router between two candidate spots. Keep everything else the same so you isolate the placement variable.

Use percentiles and packet loss to judge whether a move helped. If placement B shows fewer missed packets and tighter worst-case latency to the host, keep it even if the mean barely changed. That approach maps better to perceived smoothness during camera whips or fast UI transitions. If your router supports traffic prioritization, layer that after physical placement. A QoS tuning guide to prioritize Steam Deck streaming traffic can further reduce bufferbloat when uploads spike. Keep links distributed rather than clustered and retest after each change to separate causes.

Section highlights

  • Measure percentiles and loss to assess real streaming smoothness.
  • Change one variable at a time and repeat short tests consistently.

7) Move the seat closer rather than pushing power

Reducing client distance is often better than raising transmit power because shorter range lowers airtime per frame and decreases retries. In practice, sliding a chair one meter closer and keeping the router mid height can trim tail latency during heavy scenes more effectively than cranking power. Higher power raises interference footprints and can overwhelm nearby neighbors, which returns as contention. A scenario to test is moving the chair closer for boss fights or action-heavy sequences where steady frametimes matter. The tradeoff is room layout and household noise, but this change costs nothing and needs no firmware edits. It complements band steering because a closer client is more likely to hold higher modulation rates on 5 GHz without flapping down.

Power settings are not a cure-all. Excess transmit power can saturate receivers at short range or create hidden node effects if multiple access points coexist. If you use mesh nodes, consider seating relative to the closest node rather than the main router. The right seat placement prevents roaming events during streaming sessions that could trigger a pause. For a broader perspective on connection type tradeoffs, compare a head-to-head latency comparison of Wi‑Fi 6 versus Ethernet when selecting where to sit with the dock. The common theme is to shrink the path and maintain line of sight where possible so radio airtime stays predictable under load.

Section highlights

  • Closer seating cuts airtime and lowers retries without raising power.
  • Avoid roaming or hidden node problems by picking seats near one node.

  • Multi-User MIMO — Sending data to multiple clients at once using spatial streams.
  • Network Latency — The time it takes for data to travel between your device and the game server.
  • Quality of Service — Network rules that prioritize or shape traffic to manage latency.
  • Signal-to-Noise Ratio — The strength of the Wi‑Fi signal compared to background noise.

Frequently asked questions

Does wall material change the best placement?

Yes. Dense materials like brick or concrete block 5 GHz strongly, so prioritize line of sight and mid height. Drywall and wood are more forgiving, but avoid corners and metal shelves. Test placements with percentiles rather than just average ping.

Should I mount the router on the ceiling?

Ceiling mounts can work in open rooms, but many consumer antennas are tuned for horizontal coverage. Mid height usually gives better polarization alignment for handhelds and reduces reflections from floors and ceilings.

Is 80 MHz always better than 40 MHz on 5 GHz?

No. Use 80 MHz in cleaner environments and the same room. In busy apartments, 40 MHz can reduce overlap and contention, which may lower jitter. Choose based on scans and confirm with short repeatable latency tests.

How far from a microwave should I place the router?

Keep at least two meters away when possible. Microwaves can leak interference around 2.4 GHz during operation and create reflections. If space is tight, prefer 5 GHz for streaming and avoid aligning the path through the kitchen.

Looking ahead

Placement starts the optimization journey and testing closes the loop. After you stabilize line of sight, height, and interference, layer configuration changes like channel width and QoS only where measurements predict a gain. Keep a small notebook of percentiles across positions so trends emerge over time. As firmware updates and new devices join the network, recheck a couple of placements and retune channels to reflect the environment you actually have. Small location tweaks often beat costly upgrades because fewer reflections and shorter paths give radios easier work. With a measured approach, your streaming sessions will feel steadier and recover faster from bursts.
