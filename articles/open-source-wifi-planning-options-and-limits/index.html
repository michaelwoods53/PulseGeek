Open-Source WiFi Planning Options and Limits

By Michael Carter • Published January 11, 2026

Open source WiFi planning options can deliver credible first-pass insights when you frame the problem carefully and accept limits on precision. This list curates approaches that blend proven propagation models, freely available maps, and simple visualizations to guide planning decisions. Items were chosen for practicality, reproducibility, and the ability to validate results with small tests before scaling.

Key takeaways

  • Open source methods give directional planning guidance with clear caveats.
  • Indoor accuracy hinges on wall data, materials, and calibration.
  • Terrain models help outdoors but depend on reliable elevation sources.
  • Validation with surveys closes gaps between prediction and WiFi reality.
  • Combine simple models and visuals to cross-check planning assumptions.

1) SPLAT! for terrain-aware outdoor WiFi

SPLAT! is an open source RF propagation tool that models path loss with terrain profiles, which makes it useful for outdoor WiFi links where line of sight and Fresnel clearance dominate outcomes. For example, you can combine SRTM elevation tiles with SPLAT! site files to estimate received signal for a 5 GHz point-to-point hop across rolling hills, then adjust antenna heights to restore clearance. The upside is transparent inputs and physics-grounded results. The tradeoff is granularity and data currency. Elevation datasets may be coarse in urban canyons, and SPLAT! does not account for building diffraction or foliage seasonality by default. That means accuracy erodes in dense cities or leaf-on forests unless you add conservative fade margins or layer empirical corrections.

A practical pattern is to use SPLAT! for feasibility, then bracket likely throughput by mapping received signal to MCS levels with conservative interference assumptions. For a small park WiFi expansion, you might check whether a mesh hop clears the primary Fresnel zone at 2.4 and 5 GHz, then select channels based on least-obstructed azimuths. 2) QGIS raster math for quick coverage sketches

QGIS can approximate WiFi coverage by converting access point positions into rasters and applying distance-based attenuation formulas, which is helpful for sketching where different AP placements might land. A worked example is placing candidate AP points on a campus map layer, generating distance rasters for each, then applying a log-distance model with a path loss exponent of 2 to 3.5 to simulate received signal. The benefit is speed and transparency. You see every step and can tune exponents or add simple masks for obstacles. The limitation is that QGIS does not inherently model multipath, antenna patterns, or material-specific losses. Accuracy drops indoors or near reflective structures, so treat outputs as comparative deltas between placements rather than absolute signal guarantees.

To manage expectations, calibrate the exponent using a few on-site measurements, then re-run the raster workflow to align predictions with reality. On a multi-building quad, calibrating to 3.2 might match courtyard RSSI better than free-space 2.0. 3) ITM Longley-Rice for sub-6 GHz outdoor links

The ITM Longley-Rice model predicts median path loss over irregular terrain using climate, surface, and frequency inputs, making it suitable for sub-6 GHz outdoor WiFi backhaul planning where statistical reliability matters. As an example, feeding terrain profiles and a continental temperate climate into an open ITM implementation can estimate 50th percentile signal for a 3 km 5 GHz link across mixed terrain. The strength is that ITM reflects diffraction, surface refractivity, and variability better than simple free-space. The tradeoff is complexity and parameter sensitivity; assumptions about climate or terrain roughness shift results, and the model is not tailored for street-level clutter. ITM guides link budgets and antenna placement, but you will still need margins for foliage, vehicles, and urban density that ITM underrepresents.

A practical workflow pairs ITM with conservative fade margin targets for availability, then validates with a low-power test using directional antennas before committing to mounts. If the ITM estimate suggests 20 dB margin at median conditions, reserve an extra 10 to 15 dB for seasonal or event-driven load changes. 4) Indoor path loss notebooks for quick what-ifs

Simple indoor models like log-distance, ITU Indoor, or COST 231 let you approximate WiFi signal decay across rooms without full ray tracing, which is effective for scenario testing in notebooks. For instance, you can assume a reference path loss at one meter, apply an exponent around 2.2 to 3.0, then add wall attenuation factors of 3 to 8 dB per partition to estimate received power on a grid. The upside is agility. You can toggle wall counts or material loss to compare AP densities or placements in minutes. The limitation is granularity. Real interiors have mixed materials, doors ajar, and furniture that introduce variability. These models anchor decisions but cannot replace calibrated surveys when you need per-room predictability for voice or high-density classrooms.

A pragmatic approach sets thresholds as ranges rather than single values and validates outliers. If your model predicts -65 dBm to -70 dBm across a corridor, test endpoints and centerline, then tune wall losses. This yields a calibrated envelope you can trust for coverage planning. Meanwhile, when the question shifts from coverage to user throughput under load, escalating to tools that simulate contention may be necessary. That boundary condition signals when open source approximations should hand off to specialized platforms with richer RF behavior modeling.

Section highlights

  • Notebook models enable rapid indoor what-ifs with tunable parameters.
  • Use measured samples to calibrate wall losses and refine ranges.

5) OpenStreetMap footprints to approximate indoor attenuation

OpenStreetMap building footprints can seed indoor attenuation maps by inferring walls and zones from polygons, which helps when you lack CAD files. For example, you can overlay OSM footprints on a satellite basemap, generate interior grids, then apply heuristic losses at polygon edges to mimic perimeters and internal partitions. The advantage is free, global coverage and consistent formats. The tradeoff is accuracy. Footprints rarely include interior walls or materials, and polygon alignment may be off by a meter, shifting your estimated wall counts. This makes OSM-driven maps a starting point, not a design authority, especially for multi-tenant buildings where interior layouts differ from the shell.

One way to contain error is to use OSM only for outer shell losses and apply a uniform partition loss per meter indoors, then replace heuristics with measurements as you learn the space. When you reach the validation stage, pair these estimates with a repeatable survey route and active testing to compare modeled and observed throughput. 6) Kismet exports to inform DIY planning and checks

Kismet, an open source wireless analyzer, exports observations like RSSI and BSSID that can inform planning assumptions and find interfering networks. For a university building, you might capture passive surveys across hallways, export CSVs, and map mean RSSI by location to identify holes and dominant interferers on 2.4 and 5 GHz. The upside is independent evidence of the RF soundscape before you place new APs. The tradeoff is that Kismet captures are opportunistic and do not represent client performance under load. Passive RSSI snapshots can mislead if you treat them as throughput proxies, so pair them with active tests or treat them as preliminary constraints for your models rather than direct service indicators.

A crisp workflow is to align Kismet timestamps with your survey path and annotate outliers for re-checks. If a corridor shows unexpectedly low RSSI for the existing SSID, inspect hidden nooks, metal shelving, or sources of attenuation added after the last design. After planning updates, a structured active survey closes the loop and avoids overfitting to passive data alone. Over time, keeping a small library of pre and post-change datasets lets you build repeatable heuristics that improve your quick planning routines.

Section highlights

  • Kismet reveals RF conditions and interferers before design changes.
  • Confirm findings with active tests to understand user experience.

7) scikit-rf plus numpy to map signal to capacity ranges

While scikit-rf focuses on RF network analysis, combining it with numpy lets you apply straightforward mapping from received signal to modulation and coding scheme ranges to estimate throughput envelopes. For example, define RSSI bins that roughly correspond to MCS sets for 80 MHz channels, then apply conservative airtime efficiency factors to translate signal into capacity per client. The win is decision support. You can visualize expected user throughput in zones rather than just signal heat, which aligns with service objectives. The limitation is realism. Without contention and retries, capacity estimates can look optimistic. That is why these maps should include headroom for collisions and background utilization measured from production systems.

As a simple safeguard, cap per-station throughput by multiplying the ideal PHY rate by an efficiency factor between 0.3 and 0.6 depending on environment, then sum a small number of concurrent stations per cell to avoid double counting. This keeps the envelope honest for office or classroom patterns. 8) Matplotlib or Bokeh for reproducible heatmaps

Open source visualization libraries like Matplotlib and Bokeh turn DIY predictions into interpretable heatmaps that stakeholders can understand. On a floor grid, color-mapping predicted RSSI or throughput bins with consistent scales makes deltas between AP placements obvious. For instance, after computing a log-distance model over a grid, you can generate a discrete colormap with thresholds at -55, -65, and -75 dBm and annotate AP icons. The benefit is accessibility. Engineers, facilities teams, and decision makers discuss the same picture. The drawback is that visuals can imply false precision if readers forget the assumptions. Always include legends, thresholds, and a short note describing the model and calibration level to keep interpretations anchored.

To standardize reviews, fix color scales across iterations so changes reflect design choices, not palette shifts. Save figures with metadata describing model parameters and calibration date for traceability. Looking ahead

The most reliable pattern blends open tools for transparency with lightweight measurements to tune assumptions, then reserves complex RF behavior for specialized platforms when stakes are high. Start with the option that best matches your scenario, document parameters, and capture a few validation points so you can iterate confidently. Over time, you will build a local library of calibrated exponents, wall losses, and efficiency factors that travel well between similar sites.

If you later need automated change tracking, multi-floor path loss, or integrated contention models, transition selectively. Use your open source baseline as a benchmark so you can quantify how much fidelity and workflow speed you gain. That comparison makes budget discussions easier and keeps focus on measurable outcomes rather than features alone.

Section highlights

  • Begin with transparent models, then calibrate with small measurements.
  • Upgrade tools when validation shows limits hinder required outcomes.
