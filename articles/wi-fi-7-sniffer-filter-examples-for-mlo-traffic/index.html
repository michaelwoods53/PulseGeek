Wi‑Fi 7 Sniffer Filter Examples for MLO Traffic

By Allison Grant • Published February 10, 2026

These filter patterns focus on Wi‑Fi 7 multi link operation using practical fields most sniffers expose reliably. Selection prioritized repeatable behavior over vendor specific dissectors, favoring address scope, channel views, and QoS context. Each item explains the claim, shows an example filter or workflow, then weighs tradeoffs you must consider when MLO traffic shifts across links or when analyzers parse new elements inconsistently.

Key takeaways

  • Prefer address scope and channel filters when MLO dissectors are immature.
  • Correlate per link flows using frequency plus QoS TID consistency.
  • Validate setup with management frames, then confirm data path symmetry.
  • Detect fallbacks by contrasting EHT presence with legacy capability fields.
  • Use sequence and retry flags to expose reordering or hidden loss.

1) Scope by MLD addresses and QoS data frames

Start with the claim that clean address scoping reduces noise before diving into MLO specifics. In practice, filter to frames where either transmitter or receiver matches the known multi link device address, then narrow to data frames with QoS headers that usually carry application traffic. For example, combine wlan.addr == XX:XX:XX:XX:XX:XX with wlan.fc.type == 2 and include wlan.qos presence to sidestep probes and beacons. This approach surfaces the real payload path without hoping the analyzer labels every Multi Link element perfectly. The tradeoff is that address centric filters hide setup context and control frames that affect scheduling and link selection. To mitigate that, keep a second view parked on management traffic for the same devices and cross check timestamps when anomalies appear.

Use a contrasting rule of thumb that separates client to AP unicast from broadcast or multicast chatter. Add direction by pairing transmitter and receiver roles with wlan.ta and wlan.ra, which tightens attribution when multiple devices share the medium. For instance, target client uplink as wlan.ta == STA_MAC and wlan.fc.type == 2 and wlan.qos. The benefit is clearer causality for latency spikes because you can attribute them to a single station across its links. The limitation appears during fast link switches when hardware rotates MACs per link or masks the transmitter behind aggregation. When that happens, fall back to a broader wlan.addr == filter and rely on sequence numbers to keep continuity across frames that look like different subflows.

Section highlights

  • Anchor analysis with address and data frame filters before refinement.
  • Keep a parallel view for management traffic to preserve context.

2) Split per link traffic by channel frequency

The most reliable way to see each MLO link is to filter by RF frequency or channel since links often sit on different bands. In Wireshark, radiotap.channel.freq or a channel field lets you isolate frames per link even when dissectors do not expose Multi Link metadata. For example, create display filters for 5 GHz and 6 GHz and chart the same flow’s sequence across both views. This reveals steering or load balancing events as shifts in where TCP segments arrive. The tradeoff is that capture adapters must monitor both channels simultaneously or you will miss half the conversation. If you only have one radio, rotate captures in short windows and accept that you are sampling, not recording a continuous picture.

Because MLO can transmit simultaneously, align per link views by timestamps and sequence numbers to understand reordering. Record the same flow ID such as a five tuple on both channels and inspect wlan.seq gaps that correlate with loss or retries. A concrete workflow is to filter each channel, export time referenced CSVs, then merge on sequence or TCP sequence deltas to highlight where bursts moved between bands. The upside is precise link attribution without deep packet knowledge of EHT elements. The downside is overhead from synchronization and the risk of clock skew between adapters. Calibrate clocks where possible and prefer a multi channel sniffer to reduce drift and reconstruction effort.

Section highlights

  • Filter by frequency to view each MLO link independently and cleanly.
  • Align timestamps and sequences to reconstruct reordering across bands.

3) Verify setup using management and EHT capability context

Before trusting data path conclusions, confirm that the association negotiated features that enable multi link operation. Filter for beacons, probe responses, and association frames using wlan.fc.type == 0 and specific subtypes, then inspect information elements that advertise EHT capabilities and multi link support. For instance, you can park a display filter on the AP BSSID and wlan.fc.subtype == 8 to review beacons for the presence of EHT and any multi link identifiers in extended elements. This provides ground truth about what was possible at join time. The tradeoff is that element parsing may vary across analyzer versions, so rely on hex dumps if labels are missing. Document the exact OUI and element boundaries you observe for repeatable reviews.

Validate the client side by capturing reassociation or capability exchange frames and matching the client MAC. A practical filter is wlan.ta == STA_MAC and wlan.fc.type == 0 which surfaces probe requests and association requests that show what the station offered. Compare those to the AP’s advertised capabilities to explain why certain links were used or left idle. The benefit is a defensible chain from negotiation to behavior. The drawback is that scanning traffic often appears on different channels than data. If you cannot sniff the channels where probes occur, perform a targeted capture near association time or instruct the device to reassociate while your sniffer tracks both bands, accepting that some management frames may still be missed.

Section highlights

  • Use management subtypes to confirm EHT and multi link capabilities.
  • Compare client offers against AP beacons to explain link choices.

4) Trace TID and sequence numbers to analyze reordering

Reordering analysis hinges on tracking a single QoS traffic identifier across links. Focus filters on wlan.qos.tid for the flow’s TID and pair them with wlan.seq to catch gaps that indicate out of order delivery or retries. For example, wlan.qos.tid == 3 and wlan.addr == STA_OR_AP filters a voice or video stream depending on your mapping. Plot sequence progression by channel to show when frames leap between links and whether receive order diverges from transmit order. This clarifies if jitter comes from wireless delivery or the upper stack. The limitation is that aggregation obscures per frame timing. When A-MPDU appears, examine the subframe sequence fields and retry flags. If tooling hides them, pivot to TCP sequence behavior as a proxy for perceived reordering.

Apply a guardrail by excluding control frames and non QoS traffic that confuses sequence interpretation. Add wlan.fc.type == 2 and wlan.qos to ensure the presence of the QoS control field, then sort by frame.time_relative to view delivery cadence. As a working example, mirror the filter per frequency to separate link timelines, then compute inter arrival deltas to detect queueing bursts. This sharpens root cause when MLO steering triggers transient imbalances. The tradeoff is sensitivity to capture loss, which mimics true wireless loss. Mitigate by improving signal to noise with directional antennas or an over the air enclosure when possible, and note capture errors explicitly to avoid false attributions.

Section highlights

  • Filter by TID and sequence to reveal reordering and retries.
  • Split timelines by channel to isolate per link cadence changes.

5) Separate uplink and downlink to expose path asymmetry

Direction matters because MLO often chooses different links for send and receive. Use wlan.ta and wlan.ra with the station and AP MACs to isolate uplink versus downlink. For downlink, wlan.ta == AP_MAC and wlan.ra == STA_MAC and wlan.fc.type == 2 highlights frames the client receives. Repeat per frequency to see which band serves which direction and whether a link is stuck supporting only one side of the flow. This reveals schedule asymmetry that degrades throughput and latency under load. The tradeoff is MAC aliasing when radios expose per link addresses. If you see unexplained gaps, expand filters to include per radio BSSIDs you discover in beacons and map them back to the same logical device to restore continuity.

For infrastructure heavy traces, also reference the To DS and From DS bits to confirm the path role at the MAC layer. While display filters vary by dissector, fields like wlan.fc.to_ds and wlan.fc.from_ds can reveal whether the frame traverses distribution system paths that might introduce bottlenecks unrelated to radio selection. A concrete example is checking wlan.fc.from_ds == 1 on downlink to ensure the AP sourced the frame, then correlating with channel to see if the device balanced direction across bands. The upside is quick identification of asymmetric policy. The downside is that bridged topologies or mesh backhauls add copies that clutter views. Prevent confusion by restricting address scope to the edge AP and client pair first.

Section highlights

  • Use transmitter and receiver fields to isolate direction per link.
  • Check DS bits to rule out wired bottlenecks in path analysis.

6) Find MLO steering using transport layer markers

When EHT elements are ambiguous, lean on transport markers like TCP SYN, ACK, and burst boundaries to catch steering events. Filter a specific five tuple in Wireshark or Tshark and mirror that display filter per channel, then observe when packet counts or inter arrival times shift between bands. For example, isolate tcp.flags.syn == 1 for session start and pair with radiotap.channel.freq filters to see where the flow materializes. This method works even when the analyzer treats new fields inconsistently because TCP flags remain stable. The tradeoff is that encrypted payloads can hide L4 metadata in some deployments. If TLS offload or proxying complicates markers, pivot to UDP flows with known ports or use ICMP bursts during tests to create observable anchors.

To make this reproducible, run a short capture script that applies per channel display filters and writes summaries you can compare. A simple Tshark invocation can export timestamps and frame counts for the same stream across frequencies, revealing steering windows without guesswork. This supports regression tracking during firmware updates. The limitation is that multi radio capture hardware may still miss some packets due to channel hopping or adapter duty cycles. Address that by using dedicated adapters locked to target channels so each view records continuously rather than sampling.

Section highlights

  • Use TCP flags and per channel views to detect steering shifts.
  • Automate summaries to compare link usage across test runs.

7) Confirm legacy fallbacks and mixed mode operation

MLO capable devices may drop to legacy modes with certain peers or environments. Detect this by comparing capability elements in beacons and association frames to the data traffic you observe. Filter beacons from the AP and look for EHT capability presence, then filter the same AP’s data frames and check whether channel usage or rates align with Wi‑Fi 7 expectations. If you see only one band in use and no higher order modulation behavior under clean conditions, you likely hit a fallback. The advantage is a concrete explanation for lower throughput. The tradeoff is that some analyzers delay support for new element labels. Round out the check by exercising roaming or stress and watching whether the device resumes EHT operation. Create a controlled scenario and capture both management updates and data under load, then filter per channel to confirm if the second link ever participates. If the device never activates a secondary link despite acceptable SNR, consult certification scope materials that clarify what is mandatory versus optional. This anchors expectations and helps decide whether you are observing a bug, a configuration choice, or a standard compliant limitation. Frequently asked questions

Do I need Wireshark nightly builds to analyze MLO?

No. You can start with address, channel, and QoS based filters that work in stable releases. Newer builds may label EHT and multi link elements more clearly, which helps, but the core techniques here do not depend on bleeding edge fields.

How can I capture both MLO links at once?

Use two adapters locked to the target channels and a synchronized clock source when possible. Avoid channel hopping during analysis runs. If hardware is limited, capture short windows on each channel and treat results as samples rather than a full timeline.

What if radiotap frequency is missing in my trace?

Some drivers omit radiotap fields. Try a different capture adapter or driver that exports channel information. As a fallback, derive bands from known BSSIDs and AP beacons, though that is less precise for per link separation.

Are these filters valid for encrypted networks?

Yes for MAC layer analysis and steering detection. You can still filter by addresses, QoS presence, sequence numbers, and channels. Transport layer markers like TCP flags remain visible, though payload inspection is not possible without keys.

How do I avoid misreading capture loss as wireless loss?

Improve SNR at the sniffer, reduce distance to devices, and use directional antennas or RF enclosures when feasible. Track adapter drops and align multi adapter clocks so you can discount artifacts that come from the capture system.
