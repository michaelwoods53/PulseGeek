No Sound in Onion OS? Quick Troubleshooting Checklist

By Jordan Kelly • Published March 5, 2026

Silence on a handheld often points to simple misconfiguration before real faults. This checklist prioritizes quick wins in Onion OS audio troubleshooting, then advances into drivers and configs only when needed. Items were chosen for high impact, low risk, and repeatability, and each step includes a concrete cue so you can confirm progress. Expect to verify mute states, confirm the correct audio device, and reset minimal configuration safely. Links to deep dives appear where recovery involves broader resets or file integrity.

Key takeaways

  • Start with mute and volume checks before touching drivers or configs.
  • Confirm the correct audio device and driver in RetroArch settings.
  • Use minimal config resets to restore sound without losing saves.
  • Update carefully and roll back if audio regresses after firmware changes.
  • SD integrity issues can mute audio by breaking config writes or paths.

1) Check volume and mute states

Begin with the most common root cause by confirming that no sound is simply a mute or volume conflict. Check the hardware rocker or side slider, the Onion OS quick menu volume, and the emulator layer such as RetroArch where a per-core or global mute can override system levels. For example, RetroArch’s Quick Menu offers a fast “Audio Mute” toggle that can silently persist across sessions. A good rule is to test one known-good ROM and core while watching a visible volume overlay move. The tradeoff is that visual indicators can be misleading if the underlying driver is misselected, so treat volume success as a hint, not proof. If nothing changes, reboot once to clear transient mixer states before moving on to device and driver checks.

Also verify in-game options, because some systems default to low volume or disabled audio, especially homebrew ports where the mixer initializes late. Load a title with a steady attract mode soundtrack to eliminate false positives from quiet scenes. As a quick control, plug in wired headphones to check if the device routes audio to a different path than the speaker. If you hear sound in headphones but not the speaker, suspect a route or mixer profile issue rather than content. The limitation is that handheld hardware idiosyncrasies vary, so a correct test on one model may not perfectly map to another. If this step changes nothing, proceed to driver selection where the audio path is defined.

2) Select the correct audio driver and device

Incorrect audio driver or device selection can silence Onion OS even when volumes look fine. RetroArch typically runs well with the ALSA driver on Linux-based handhelds, and device selection should target the default or built-in output rather than a dormant HDMI or null sink. For instance, switching RetroArch’s Audio driver to ALSA and choosing the default device restores sound for many users after a firmware change. A practical test is to set Audio latency to a moderate value like 64 to 128 ms, which avoids underruns without adding obvious lag. The tradeoff is that forcing a driver can mask a deeper regression introduced by updates, so document your baseline before tweaking. If the driver flips back or devices disappear across reboots, suspect a config write issue or SD card file integrity problem rather than a mixer bug.

If you rely on standalone cores or ports, check their own output settings because they may not inherit RetroArch’s driver or device. Use one consistent title to eliminate content variance while you toggle drivers. When a driver swap works only until the next boot, copy the working configuration file to a safe location, then validate whether Onion OS is saving settings reliably. 3) Reset minimal audio config safely

When settings drift or corrupt, a minimal audio reset often brings sound back without wiping saves. The goal is to restore a sane baseline in RetroArch by toggling only a few lines that control enablement, driver, and volume. First back up your current retroarch.cfg, then apply small changes and reboot to confirm persistence. For example, ensure audio is enabled, the driver matches your device, and volume sits at a known-good level around 1.0. This approach avoids broad factory resets that can disrupt input mappings and overlays. The tradeoff is that editing configuration files can introduce typos that make matters worse, so keep a clean backup you can immediately restore. If a minimal reset succeeds, reintroduce prior tweaks one at a time so you can spot the setting that breaks audio.

Start by reading the change notes and testing with a clean config backup to confirm whether the issue stems from new defaults. As a safety net, copy saves and states before any system-level changes. 5) Verify config writes and SD integrity

Silent audio sometimes traces to configuration writes that never persist because the SD card is write-protected, near failure, or formatted inconsistently. A tell is when volume or driver changes look correct but revert after reboot. Test by toggling a harmless setting, rebooting, and confirming it sticks. Run a read and write check on another device if available, and ensure the card uses a recommended filesystem and allocation size. If RetroArch creates duplicate config copies or timestamps look wrong, suspect filesystem errors or unsafe shutdowns. The tradeoff is that diagnostics take time, but fixing storage reliability prevents recurring audio issues. If a fresh card stabilizes writes and audio persists, clone the working state and retire the failing media. If not, the bottleneck is higher in the stack, possibly a driver regression or conflicting theme overlay. When large config overhauls are needed, ensure you also back up input mappings so a reset does not trade audio for broken controls. 6) Isolate core or port specific audio settings

Even when global audio works, a specific core or port can mute or misroute sound via its own options. For example, some cores expose sample rate, low latency modes, or speed hacks that desynchronize audio and produce silence under stress. Create a controlled test by running the same game across two different cores, keeping the frontend driver unchanged. If one core works and the other does not, reset that core’s options to defaults. The benefit is quick isolation without touching global configs. The tradeoff is that core defaults vary across versions, so document working versions before updates. If a core remains silent after defaults, switch content to a verified working ROM and retest, which rules out bad dumps or mismatched region audio behavior.

Ports and standalones may bundle their own mixers that ignore RetroArch settings entirely. Open their settings and reset audio to standard values, then confirm output through speakers and headphones. Some ports auto select high output rates that certain drivers cannot sustain, so try 44100 or 48000 Hz. If sound only fails after longer sessions, watch for thermal throttling that increases audio buffers until underruns appear. That is rare but worth confirming with a short and then extended test. When your findings imply broader system drift or broken overlays, widen the scope using a diagnostic framework rather than toggling options indefinitely. Frequently asked questions

Do I need to factory reset to fix audio?

Usually not. Most audio issues resolve by unmuting, choosing the correct driver, and resetting minimal config lines. Save a factory reset for persistent regressions or when config writes fail and simple changes do not stick.

Which audio driver should I use in RetroArch on Onion OS?

ALSA is the typical choice on Linux-based handhelds. Pair it with the default output device and a moderate latency such as 64 to 128 ms. Test speakers and headphones to confirm the route before trying other drivers.

Why do my audio settings revert after reboot?

This often indicates configuration write failures. The SD card may be write-protected, failing, or improperly formatted. Verify that a simple setting persists across reboots. If not, test with a known-good card.

What sample rate should I select?

Use 44100 or 48000 Hz for broad compatibility. Higher rates can increase CPU and bandwidth demands and sometimes create silence when the driver cannot keep up. Start conservative, verify stability, then adjust if needed.

How can I avoid losing saves during troubleshooting?

Back up saves and states before driver changes, config edits, or updates. Keep a copy of your working retroarch.cfg. If rolling back versions, clone the SD card first so you can recover quickly if results worsen.
