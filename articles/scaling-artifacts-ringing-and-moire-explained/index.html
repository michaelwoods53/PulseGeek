Scaling Artifacts: Ringing and Moiré Explained

By Logan Pierce • Published January 30, 2026

Scaling artifacts are predictable outcomes of sampling and reconstruction choices, and two of the most common are ringing and moiré. Understanding these artifacts unlocks better decisions when choosing filters or shader passes, especially when balancing clarity against performance. This guide maps the mechanisms that create halos and interference patterns, then offers decision lenses that prevent chasing symptoms with blunt blur. We focus on practical diagnostics you can repeat across emulators and displays, letting you tune once and reuse the approach. The goal is not a single perfect preset, but a consistent way to reduce ringing without erasing detail and to avoid moiré without hiding texture intent.

Key takeaways

  • Ringing comes from sharp filters overshooting at edges during reconstruction.
  • Moiré arises when fine patterns beat against pixel grids or masks.
  • Choose sampling per scale factor, not by a one size preset.
  • Mitigate artifacts early in the pipeline to avoid compounding.
  • Test edges and textures separately to isolate root causes quickly.

Concepts and definitions

Ringing is a haloing artifact created when reconstruction filters overshoot near a sharp edge, often visible as bright or dark ripples around sprites or UI text. A classic example appears when using high sharpness Lanczos at fractional scales like 1.5x, where side lobes accentuate contrast transitions. The tradeoff is straightforward: stronger edge emphasis can recover perceived detail, but increases overshoot amplitude and makes halos obvious on neutral backgrounds. The mechanism is rooted in how band-limited signals are approximated by kernels with finite tap counts, causing Gibbs-like effects at discontinuities. To reduce ringing, prefer kernels with weaker lobes or tuned windowing, and consider pre-smoothing only when the content’s line art is extremely jagged, since wholesale blur sacrifices pixel intent.

Moiré is a different failure mode, arising when fine detail interacts with a sampling grid to produce beat patterns or false contours. You will notice it on subpixel dithering, checkerboard textures, or thin line hatching as scale factors drift away from clean integers. Unlike ringing, moiré does not require overshoot; it emerges from aliasing where high spatial frequencies fold into visible bands. The tradeoff is that aggressive anti aliasing reduces these beats but also mutes high frequency detail that gives pixel art its character. Mechanistically, moiré reflects energy folding back into the passband due to insufficient low pass filtering before resampling. A better approach is to target the problematic frequencies with measured prefiltering instead of broad blur.

It helps to separate aliasing from ringing when diagnosing artifacts, because the fixes target different steps in the pipeline. Ringing is about kernel shape and edge response, so you adjust reconstruction weights or reduce overshoot terms. Moiré is about sampling mismatch, so you manage frequency content before or during resampling, not only after. For example, integer scaling suppresses many moiré cases by aligning source pixels to target pixels, while carefully windowed kernels limit ringing without heavy blur. If you need shader-based CRT masks, consider that subpixel masks can reintroduce interference, which you can tame with mask strength or scanline balance. A complete approach treats edges and textures separately, testing each with tailored targets to confirm improvements.

Frameworks and decision lenses

A reliable first lens is scale alignment, because integer factors often suppress both moiré and visible stair stepping without excessive filtering. If your display or window cannot hit an integer scale, target near integer values that keep error under a few percent and let small black borders absorb the mismatch. The tradeoff is cosmetic space loss versus fidelity, but it preserves pixel intent and reduces the need for heavy kernels. When you must fill the screen, select reconstruction filters by content: nearest or sharp integer scaling for pure pixel art, and smoother kernels like Catmull-Rom for textured 3D. This decision avoids overfitting a single preset while acknowledging that different source material needs different passbands.

The second lens is kernel selection by edge behavior, because ringing scales with lobe energy and sample taps. For UI text and high contrast sprites, reduce side lobe weight using a well windowed Lanczos or Mitchell-Netravali with B and C tuned for moderate sharpness. A rule of thumb is to start neutral and increase sharpness only until the first halo appears in a test pattern with black and white steps. The tradeoff is subtle loss of microcontrast against clean halos, but you limit overshoot before it compounds with later passes like masks or scanlines. If CRT aesthetics matter, keep mask strength moderate to prevent interaction between ringing halos and subpixel layouts that can shimmer on motion.

A third lens is pipeline ordering, because earlier decisions amplify or attenuate later artifacts. Place resampling before heavy stylistic passes so that any mild low pass filtering happens while frequencies are still separable, reducing moiré risk when you add a mask. This is where a complete guide to CRT shaders, scaling, and pipeline order helps you reason about pass sequencing. The tradeoff is that adding prefiltering too early can reduce crispness before reconstruction, so keep it minimal and targeted. As a diagnostic, alternate a high contrast edge test with a fine checkerboard, verifying that tuning one does not worsen the other, which suggests a balanced pipeline rather than a fix that moves the problem.

Examples and short scenarios

Consider a 320 by 240 title scaled to 1080p, where 1080 divided by 240 is 4.5x, a fractional factor prone to moiré and mild ringing. A safe route is 4x integer scale to 960 pixels tall and small borders, preserving sprite edges without kernel stress. If full screen is non negotiable, try Mitchell with moderate parameters, then slightly prefilter using a narrow Gaussian radius so dithered textures do not alias into beats. The tradeoff is a small blur on hairline details, but you avoid strong halos that would echo around text. Verify using a checkerboard overlay and a single pixel line slope test, confirming minimal shimmer on motion while text remains legible from a normal seating distance.

Now take a PlayStation era 3D scene with subpixel dithering used to simulate gradients, which often triggers moiré when scaled with sharp reconstruction. A better choice is to apply a light prefilter to the dither frequencies, then resample with a neutral kernel so gradients remain smooth without banding. If you then add a CRT mask, reduce mask strength and scanline contrast to stop interference from creating crawl in fine areas. The tradeoff is muted microtexture on walls, but lighting gradients and character silhouettes stay stable during motion. For handheld sources like 160 by 144 scaled to modern displays, integer scaling often leaves large borders, tempting full screen fractional scaling that invites artifacts. A hybrid approach is to use integer scaling for the longer axis, then center with minimal fractional stretch on the short axis, combined with a soft kernel that avoids ringing. The tradeoff is slight aspect compromise or small pillarbox bars, but stability improves and moiré on LCD subpixels diminishes. If you prioritize pixel crispness, keep nearest on the integer axis and gentle smoothing on the fractional axis only. Looking ahead

The next step is to institutionalize quick diagnostics so you catch ringing and moiré before they spread through your pipeline. Build a tiny test set with edge steps, slanted lines, and fine textures, and run it before adopting a new preset or backend. The tradeoff is a few minutes of setup against hours of chasing regressions later, and it enables faster iteration when you change kernels or add CRT styling. Document the settings that pass both the edge and texture tests, noting your display’s native resolution and any fractional scale thresholds that cause issues. This documentation becomes a playbook you can reuse across systems and rebuilds, cutting variability while preserving your chosen look.

As your library grows, consider backend choices and shader formats only insofar as they affect ordering and performance of your mitigation steps. You do not need to adopt every new filter; you need predictable scaling that avoids haloing and beat patterns across content. When experimenting, change one variable at a time and record impact on both readability and motion stability, not only static screenshots. The tradeoff is slower exploration, but it protects you from complex interactions that reduce quality. If you later introduce new effects, retest with the same diagnostics to confirm you have not reintroduced ringing or moiré under motion, which often reveals weak points that static testing hides.

Finally, keep a short list of preferred kernels and scale strategies tied to content types so decisions stay fast. For example, integer scaling with nearest for pure pixel art, Mitchell for general use when filling the screen, and a mild prefilter for dither heavy 3D. The tradeoff is accepting that no single preset wins on every source or display. When in doubt, revisit your test suite and make adjustments that preserve intended edges first, then address texture stability. Frequently asked questions

How can I spot ringing versus moiré quickly?

Ringing appears as light or dark halos near sharp edges or text. Moiré shows as wavy interference or false bands on fine patterns like checkerboards or dither. Test with high contrast steps and a fine texture grid to tell them apart.

Does integer scaling always fix moiré?

Integer scaling helps by aligning source and target pixels, which reduces many interference cases. It does not solve issues from added effects like masks or from camera transformations. Use it first, then adjust other passes if needed.

Which filter is safest for UI text?

Filters with restrained overshoot are safer, such as Mitchell-Netravali with moderate parameters or a well windowed Lanczos. Start neutral, increase sharpness until the first halo appears on a step pattern, then back off one notch.

Will CRT masks increase artifacts?

CRT masks can interact with fine details and amplify shimmer or moiré, especially at fractional scales. Reduce mask strength and scanline contrast and test motion. Keep resampling earlier in the pipeline to minimize compounding effects.

Is prefiltering always necessary?

No. Prefiltering helps with dither heavy textures or when fractional scaling is unavoidable. Apply it narrowly to the problematic frequencies. Broad blur reduces true detail and can hide intended pixel structure, so use it sparingly.
