LPI Limits in 6 GHz: EIRP, PSD, and Indoor Boundaries

By Olivia Adams • Published March 7, 2026

LPI defines a 6 GHz operating mode that pairs EIRP ceilings with PSD constraints to reduce interference beyond building boundaries. It applies to indoor-only deployments where the access point and clients remain within an enclosure, using integral antennas or low-gain fixtures. The point is predictable containment. By constraining transmit power per megahertz, PSD rules keep wide channels from radiating disproportionate energy, while total EIRP caps bound peak emissions. Together, these limits let planners choose channel width and spatial reuse without risking harmful interactions with incumbents. The tradeoff is straightforward. You gain large contiguous spectrum for WiFi 8 efficiency, yet you must ensure walls attenuate energy and equipment stays inside. Understanding how PSD scales with bandwidth and how EIRP interacts with antenna gain is the starting line for compliant design.

Key takeaways

  • LPI relies on PSD and EIRP limits to contain indoor emissions.
  • Channel width changes PSD per MHz, affecting range and interference risk.
  • Antenna gain counts toward EIRP, so patterns and placement matter.
  • Indoor-only boundaries require full enclosure and no outdoor leakage.
  • Use walls and materials as part of the compliance and reuse plan.

Concepts and definitions

LPI refers to low power indoor operation where devices use bounded EIRP and PSD inside buildings to limit external exposure. EIRP measures effective radiated power after antenna gain, so both transmitter output and antenna pattern matter. PSD describes power per unit bandwidth, typically per megahertz, which scales with channel width. The key mechanism is proportionality. If PSD is fixed per megahertz, doubling channel width increases total allowed power but not per-megahertz energy. That reduces spectral density spikes that could trouble incumbents. A practical example is a 160 MHz channel that can run higher total EIRP than an 80 MHz channel under the same PSD ceiling, yet the per-megahertz footprint stays constrained. The tradeoff is that wider channels still occupy more spectrum, so co-channel overlap and contention can rise indoors.

Indoor boundary means the entire access point system, including antennas and cabling, remains within a building enclosure that offers meaningful attenuation to outside. This requirement is not about signage or administrative rules. It is about physics and verification. Walls, floors, and glazing add path loss that supports the intent of low power indoor limits. A glass curtain wall is an edge case because its attenuation varies with coatings and thickness, and high-floor line-of-sight can project energy farther than expected. Designers must validate that siting and materials help contain emissions. A quick screening approach is to model worst-case line-of-sight paths through openings and ensure expected field strengths fall below conservative thresholds beyond property boundaries.

Antenna gain interacts with LPI limits because EIRP aggregates transmitter power and antenna directivity into a single number. High-gain antennas narrow energy into a beam and can violate EIRP ceilings even if conducted power seems modest. Conversely, low-gain integral antennas spread energy broadly, increasing near-field overlap that may raise contention indoors. The design goal is to keep beams modest and patterns predictable to avoid hotspots that push limits near exterior walls. Mounting height is a second lever. Ceiling placement often reduces human obstruction loss while maintaining uniform coverage, but near skylights or atriums it can increase leakage risk. Treat antenna choice and placement as part of compliance, not just performance tuning, and document calculations that tie gains to EIRP.

Frameworks and decision lenses

Start with a PSD-first lens that treats channel width as a budget allocation, not a default. If user traffic is bursty and latency sensitive, 80 MHz often balances throughput and reuse because its PSD keeps range manageable while limiting co-channel impact. When clients are sparse and need high peak rates, 160 MHz can be justified, but only with stronger isolation from walls and fewer overlapping cells. A rule of thumb is to widen channels only after validating that spectral occupancy remains below a conservative threshold during busy hour. A second lens is adjacency planning. Pair non-overlapping 6 GHz lanes and rotate them floor by floor to keep same-channel neighbors at least one attenuation barrier away.

Use an EIRP-backsolve method that starts from the weakest client target and works toward access point settings. Identify the minimum modulation and coding scheme you want at the room edge, estimate required signal-to-noise, and compute necessary receive power. With measured or modeled path loss through walls and air, you can backsolve the maximum transmit power that meets the edge target while honoring the LPI EIRP ceiling. This approach avoids setting power by habit and makes antenna gain a transparent variable in the math. The tradeoff is precision depends on correct loss estimates. When uncertain, apply a safety margin and verify with surveys after installation to refine assumptions.

Compliance confidence improves when documentation links settings to regulations and environment. Keep a single page that records EIRP calculations, antenna gains, mounting heights, and channel plans tied to PSD reasoning. Include notes about building materials and any nearby openings, such as exterior doors or atriums, that could change leakage risk. Looking ahead

Plan for verification because LPI decisions benefit from feedback loops more than fixed templates. After deployment, measure received power at interior edges and just outside building boundaries to confirm PSD and EIRP choices perform as modeled. A portable analyzer or validated survey client can capture practical snapshots across channels and widths during busy periods. If readings approach conservative thresholds outdoors, reduce channel width or adjust antenna orientation to reclaim margin. Future-proofing is about keeping an envelope of headroom that tolerates furniture moves or tenant changes. Establish a recheck cadence after renovations and seasonal occupancy shifts to ensure containment still aligns with the indoor-only intent.

Expect evolving client behavior as WiFi 8 features mature, which will change how LPI power interacts with scheduling and aggregation. Multi-link operation can spread traffic across channels, so your PSD budget may be effectively distributed across links. That favors cleaner reuse patterns over indiscriminate widening. As devices support higher modulation consistently, edge requirements may relax slightly, allowing lower EIRP within limits without sacrificing user experience. The risk is misinterpreting short-term lab gains as field-ready norms. Keep test plans that compare pre and post firmware performance with the same survey routes to separate feature gains from environmental variance.

Stay informed about regulatory clarifications that may refine interpretations of indoor boundaries or measurement methods. Guidance sometimes updates accepted practices for verifying enclosure, interpreting antenna categories, or handling unusual spaces like glass-heavy atriums. Where uncertainty persists, choose conservative settings that still meet application targets. When planning adjacent topics like automated frequency coordination or cross-domain differences, reference an explainer on automated coordination rules only if you need standard power or outdoor paths later. Keeping LPI compliant today simplifies future expansions because your documentation, power math, and siting discipline translate cleanly to more complex modes when requirements demand them.

Key terms

  • Automated Frequency Coordination (AFC) — A system that manages 6 GHz outdoor use to avoid interference with incumbents.
  • Channel Reuse — Using the same channels in different cells to increase capacity.
  • Client Station — A Wi‑Fi device that connects to an AP.
  • Equivalent Isotropically Radiated Power — The total radiated power in a given direction from an antenna system.
  • Low Power Indoor — A 6 GHz device class limited to indoor use with capped EIRP.
  • Power Spectral Density — Power distributed across frequency over channel bandwidth.
