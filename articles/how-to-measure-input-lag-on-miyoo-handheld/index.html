How to Measure Input Lag on Miyoo Handheld

By Austin Price • Published January 9, 2026

Yes, you can measure input lag on a Miyoo handheld with enough precision to compare settings. The most reliable approach is high-speed video of a button press and the first visible on-screen response, paired with consistent baselines. This article shows how to measure and interpret the delay, when results are trustworthy, and how to connect findings to pacing, vsync, and audio options for meaningful improvements.

Key takeaways

  • High-speed capture provides the most defensible input lag measurement on Miyoo.
  • Lock frame pacing and audio sync before comparing settings across test runs.
  • Use identical scenes and controllers to limit variance between recordings.
  • Interpret distributions, not single trials, to avoid misleading conclusions.
  • Changes to vsync or buffering should shift both median and worst frames.

Short answer and nuance

The shortest path is to record a button press and the first screen change using a 120 to 240 fps camera, then count frames between contact and response. This captures the full stack, including controller polling, emulation, and display scanout, which is why it aligns with perceived feel. The tradeoff is complexity, since partial-frame events and rolling-shutter exposure can blur the earliest change. To reduce error, align the camera to minimize reflections, increase screen brightness, and use a high-contrast test scene. A repeatable example is a menu cursor movement or a character jump from idle. Consistency across trials matters more than absolute precision, because you will compare settings such as vsync modes or audio buffering rather than publish a single number.

A second option is to use emulation overlays that estimate latency from frame timing, which is faster but less comprehensive. RetroArch features like frame time graphs expose pacing stability that correlates with lag, yet they do not include controller physiology or display modes. These estimates are useful during quick tuning, especially when testing frame delay or runahead interactions, but they can understate worst-case behavior. Evaluate them as a leading indicator that tells you which changes to validate with video. If an overlay shows tight frame times and a lower median, you should still confirm that stutter tails do not grow, because thick tails usually drive perceived sluggishness when action spikes.

For Miyoo devices, small screens with sample-and-hold behavior add exposure timing quirks you need to consider. A change might register in the top rows before the camera exposes the bottom, which affects how you count frames. A workable rule is to count the first unambiguous change anywhere on screen, then repeat at least ten trials to average out exposure offsets. You can also bias the view to the region you expect to change first, such as the top-left HUD. Because these handhelds run power-limited cores, minor jitter from background tasks or filesystem access can creep in, so perform tests after a short warmup and avoid writing saves during measurement.

When it applies vs when it does not

High-speed capture applies when you can create a deterministic on-screen change tied to a single button. Menu navigation or a training stage with consistent backgrounds works well because the signal is clean. It is less appropriate in scenes with heavy particle effects, motion blur, or variable lighting where the first meaningful change is ambiguous. If your phone can only do 60 fps, you can still compare settings, but your granularity is coarse and you risk aliasing single-frame shifts. To mitigate, collect more trials and rely on distributions. If the median difference spans multiple frames at 60 fps, the change is substantial enough to act on, but treat marginal one-frame shifts with caution.

Overlay-derived estimates apply when you want to check pacing effects from options like vsync, FPS limiters, or audio sync before running camera tests. These tools help detect micro-stutter that a quick video might miss if the scene change is subtle. They do not measure controller scan latency or USB polling, so they cannot validate end-to-end gains. Use them to narrow choices, then confirm with capture if the stakes are high. Edge cases include wireless controllers, overlays, or shaders that alter scanout timing and confuse early-frame detection. Wireless adds variable radio delay that inflates variance, so prefer wired or native controls for baseline measurement. Overlays and heavy shaders may shift GPU workload and frame delivery, which can widen tails. How to implement or validate

Build a simple workflow that isolates variables and records enough trials to see patterns. First, choose one scene and one button, then film the physical press and the screen at 120 to 240 fps with stable lighting. Second, lock settings like vsync, FPS limiters, and audio sync so every run is comparable. Third, take ten or more trials per setting and log counts per run. Finally, summarize with median, 10th, and 90th percentiles so you capture both typical response and worst spikes. If you are troubleshooting pacing along with lag, add a separate pass that documents frame time stability. After recording, a lightweight way to count frames is to extract a sequence around the button press and look for the first changed frame. If your motion is visible in the video, you can mark the contact frame by hand and advance frame by frame. If you prefer automation, you can compare brightness or pixel differences in a region of interest and tag the first change that exceeds a threshold. Automation reduces human bias but risks false positives from noise. To balance, constrain the region to a HUD element that flips state and test your threshold on a few known segments before batch processing the rest.

Alternatives and related questions

Some readers ask about using runahead or frame delay as proxy measurements, since both affect feel. These are tuning features, not measurement tools, so interpret changes through validation. A modest runahead can mask latency by predicting frames, yet it may introduce visual artifacts or break cores that do not support deterministic rewind. Frame delay trims idle time before render, which can improve responsiveness but risks stutter if you set it too aggressively. Treat both as levers you adjust after establishing a stable baseline with consistent pacing and audio behavior. If predictions or trimmed time improve the median but worsen the worst-case, note the tradeoff and decide based on the game’s demands.

Another path is to measure only pacing stability instead of end-to-end lag when you lack a high-speed camera. Frame time histograms or stats help you decide if your device is delivering evenly spaced frames, which correlates with controllability. This does not quantify input to photon, but it can rule out major contributors like missed sync or unstable FPS limits. Pair this with targeted tuning for vsync and audio buffering so both video and sound stack remain coherent. When you need guidance on balancing tearing and delay, see resources that explain how to pick sync options for smooth play with manageable latency, like the practical advice in choose the right vsync option.

Finally, consider environmental factors that add noise to results, such as overlays, shaders, and storage contention. Overlays and heavy shaders shift GPU time and can add jitter that obscures gains. Large save writes can delay frames when you test near autosave triggers. To avoid misreads, disable cosmetic overlays, choose lightweight shaders, and position tests away from autosave events. Frequently asked questions

Is 60 fps video enough to measure input lag?

It can show big differences, but granularity is coarse. Use more trials and treat one-frame shifts as inconclusive. For precise comparisons, 120 to 240 fps is better.

How many trials do I need per setting?

Ten to twenty runs per setting is a practical range. Summarize median and tails so you see both typical behavior and spikes that influence feel.

Do overlays or shaders affect measurement accuracy?

Yes. Overlays and heavy shaders can change GPU timing and widen frame time tails. Disable them during tests or use lightweight presets to reduce noise.

Should I test with wired or wireless controllers?

Prefer wired or native controls for baseline. Wireless adds variable radio delay that increases variance and can hide small improvements between settings.

What if my results vary across runs?

Variance is normal. Check for background tasks, autosave events, or inconsistent scenes. Then increase sample size and report distributions instead of single numbers.

Looking ahead

Your measurements should guide targeted changes that improve both median response and worst-case spikes. Start with the baseline that stabilizes pacing and audio sync, then retest after each adjustment so gains stack rather than conflict. If a tweak helps typical frames but grows tails, reconsider whether the tradeoff fits the game’s demands. Keep your process light by saving camera setups and logging templates for future tests, and revisit them when firmware or cores update. By maintaining repeatable methods, you turn quick checks into dependable decisions and keep your Miyoo responsive without risking regressions.
