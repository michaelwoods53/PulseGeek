Texture Filtering and Dithering in DuckStation

By Brian Rogers • Published March 5, 2026

Texture filtering and dithering are two levers in DuckStation that shape how PlayStation graphics look on modern displays. Filtering decides how textures scale and blend across pixels, while dithering simulates extra color depth by patterning noise. Tuning them together changes sharpness, color banding, and shimmering in motion. If you want original console character, you will lean one way. If you prefer cleaner edges and smoother gradients, you will lean another. This guide explains what each option does, where it helps or hurts, and how to balance accuracy, clarity, and performance on different hardware. Along the way, you will see decision rules, short scenarios, and references to renderer choices that influence outcomes.

Key takeaways

  • Texture filtering smooths scaled art while risking blur on low detail.
  • Dithering hides banding but can add grain or shimmer in motion.
  • Original look favors nearest textures with native or simulated dithering.
  • Sharper visuals use linear or anisotropic filtering with dither disabled.
  • Mix settings per game and renderer to avoid edge artifacts.

Concepts and definitions

Texture filtering determines how texels map to screen pixels when images scale or rotate. Nearest neighbor keeps hard edges and pixel clarity, which suits low resolution UI but can shimmer during motion. Bilinear blends samples for smoother transitions, often reducing crawl, though it may soften fine details like text. Anisotropic filtering improves angle-dependent clarity on slanted surfaces, which helps ground textures that otherwise smear. Dithering simulates higher color depth by distributing quantization error as patterns, masking banding in gradients and fog. On classic PlayStation art, dithering unifies tones that would otherwise step visibly. The tradeoff is grain that can flicker with movement. Understanding these fundamentals sets expectations for when sharpness, smoothness, or grain will dominate.

DuckStation exposes filtering at multiple points, including the renderer path and texture sampling, which interact with internal resolution. Raising internal resolution magnifies detail, so bilinear softening becomes less noticeable while reducing shimmering. Conversely, at native scale, nearest filtering preserves the authentic look of sprites and menus but amplifies crawl on subpixel movement. Dithering can be emulated for authenticity or disabled to let true display bit depth show gradients more cleanly. Games that leaned on dithering for fog or night scenes often look flatter with it off. The key is recognizing that filtering changes spatial detail while dithering changes tonal continuity. Balancing both is how you keep character without unwanted artifacts.

Because DuckStation targets original behavior and enhanced output, filtering and dithering are not one-size settings. Some titles feature low contrast textures where bilinear smoothing hides seams effectively. Others rely on crisp pixel art where nearest looks intentional and clean. Similarly, dithering patterns may be critical to mood in horror scenes, yet distracting during bright, flat UI overlays. A practical rule is to evaluate three frames: static, camera pan, and high-contrast UI. If the pan shows crawl, consider bilinear or anisotropic. If static gradients band, enable dithering. If UI blurs, restore nearest for menus using per-game overrides. This frame-by-frame lens keeps changes grounded in what players actually notice.

Frameworks and decision lenses

Start with a goal-oriented matrix that weighs authenticity, clarity, and performance. If authenticity is primary, prefer nearest texture filtering with dithering enabled, then raise internal resolution modestly. If clarity is your priority, pick bilinear filtering with dithering off, then consider anisotropic at 2x to 4x for slanted planes. Performance-first builds often stick to bilinear without anisotropic and toggle dithering only when gradients band. Each choice has costs. Nearest may shimmer at camera pans. Bilinear may blur HUD elements. Dithering adds grain that some players dislike. The why is simple. Textures and gradients respond differently to sampling and quantization. Tuning per goal avoids chasing a single setting that cannot satisfy every scene.

Renderer choice changes outcomes, so treat it as a lens in your framework. Vulkan and OpenGL handle sampling and driver-level precision differently, which can alter edge cases around subpixel movement or gamma. If you are exploring renderer tradeoffs, compare a demanding scene across both paths. For a structured deep dive on renderers, see the comparison of compatibility and speed to judge which path best fits stability and accuracy. Once the renderer is fixed, revisit filtering and dithering to avoid misattributing improvements to the wrong layer. This sequence prevents circular tuning and gives you replicable results when you change hardware or drivers later.

Finally, adopt thresholds to prevent over-tuning. If bilinear resolves shimmer without blurring text at your chosen internal resolution, avoid stacking anisotropic unless you notice angle-specific smear. If dithering eliminates visible banding in two representative scenes, do not push further unless grain appears intrusive. When an edge case remains, consider a limited override rather than global changes. DuckStation allows per-game graphics overrides, which keeps your baseline sane while handling exceptions. This keeps knobs scoped to the problem and reduces regression risk. Structured limits stop you from chasing perfection that most players will not see during normal play and preserve consistent feel across your library.

Examples and short scenarios

Consider a racing game with a long track texture receding into the distance. Nearest filtering keeps paint lines crisp but makes the asphalt crawl during camera motion. Switching to bilinear smooths the motion and reduces aliasing on distant stripes, though the HUD may soften slightly at native scale. Adding 2x anisotropic improves the angle clarity of road markings without a big frame cost on midrange GPUs. If you disable dithering here, sky gradients may band during sunsets. If banding is visible, re-enable dithering only for that scene using a per-game profile. This trade keeps the road stable while preserving color continuity in the sky and avoids unintended blur on interface text.

Now look at a menu-heavy role-playing title with small fonts and iconography. Nearest filtering preserves the pixel edges in text, which reads cleanly even at modest internal resolution. Bilinear can soften the font strokes, especially when UI scales from non-integer factors. If you see blur, revert UI contexts to nearest and leave 3D scenes on bilinear using appropriate overrides. Dithering often matters less on flat UI colors, but it can help in gradient backdrops that would otherwise step. The risk is grain that distracts during long reading sessions. In that case, disable dithering for menus and keep it on for in-game fog or night scenes where tonal smoothness contributes to mood.

For a dark action game with heavy fog, dithering becomes central. With dithering off, fog layers may show visible steps across gradients, breaking immersion during slow pans. Enabling dithering reduces banding by spreading error into a fine pattern, which reads as texture rather than steps. Pairing this with bilinear creates smoother edges where fog intersects geometry, though it may soften thin textures like wires. If you rely on Vulkan for performance, test OpenGL briefly to confirm similar tonal output before attributing differences to filtering. Also check input latency after changes, since triple buffering or VSync choices can influence perceived smoothness more than small filtering differences.

Pitfalls, limitations, and edge cases

The most common pitfall is chasing shimmer solely with higher internal resolution. While scaling helps, subpixel movement can still produce crawl if nearest sampling remains, especially on thin geometry and textures with high contrast. Bilinear reduces that motion artifact, but at a cost to crisp UI. Another trap is enabling strong anisotropic without need, which marginally sharpens angles while increasing bandwidth use. The better path is to add anisotropic only when you see angle-specific smear in ground or wall textures. If dithering grain becomes noticeable during bright scenes, consider disabling it globally and reintroducing it only for games that relied on the effect to hide gradient steps.

A second limitation arises when combining filtering with shader-based CRT effects. Many CRT shaders add their own mask and bloom that interacts with dithering patterns. With dithering on, the combined pattern may moiré at certain scales, creating distracting interference. In that setup, disabling dithering while retaining the shader’s mask often yields a cleaner composite. Also test at integer scale factors for UI to prevent bilinear from softening text before the shader applies. If you plan to run widescreen hacks, verify texture sampling with forced aspect changes, since off-label geometry can reveal seams that were not visible at the original resolution or field of view.

Renderer differences can surface rare visual mismatches. On some drivers, Vulkan may present slightly different gamma handling than OpenGL, which changes how banding appears even with identical dithering settings. When investigating, lock frame pacing and VSync to remove timing as a variable, then compare captured frames in identical scenes. If OpenGL avoids a specific artifact in your setup, prefer it for that title and keep notes in a per-game profile. For deeper renderer guidance, review a comparison of compatibility and speed to ensure your choice reflects the game’s needs. Finally, remember that audio issues or frame pacing can amplify perceived visual problems, so validate stability before crediting filtering changes.

Frequently asked questions

Should I enable dithering in DuckStation?

Enable it if you see color banding in gradients, fog, or night scenes. Disable it if grain distracts or patterns shimmer. Many players keep it on for authenticity and selectively turn it off for bright UI or clean menus.

Which texture filtering looks best for menus?

Nearest filtering usually preserves crisp UI text and icons. If bilinear is required for 3D scenes, consider per-game overrides to keep menus on nearest while leaving gameplay on smoother filtering to reduce shimmer.

Does anisotropic filtering always help in DuckStation?

It helps most on angled surfaces like floors and roads. Start at 2x or 4x and increase only if you still see smear. If your scene looks stable already, higher levels add little while consuming extra resources.

Will Vulkan or OpenGL change my filtering results?

They can differ in precision or gamma handling on some drivers. Lock frame pacing and compare the same scene under each renderer. Choose the path that avoids artifacts on your hardware, then finalize filtering and dithering.

Do higher internal resolutions reduce the need for filtering?

Higher resolution often lessens shimmer and makes bilinear softening less visible. It does not remove every artifact from subpixel motion, so you may still prefer bilinear or limited anisotropic for stability in motion.
