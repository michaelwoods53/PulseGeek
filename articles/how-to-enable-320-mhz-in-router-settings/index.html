How to Enable 320 MHz in Router Settings

By Rachel Morgan • Published January 22, 2026

Enabling 320 MHz on a router requires more than flipping a width toggle. Successful results depend on compatible radios, firmware that exposes 6 GHz options, and clients that can use wider lanes without collapsing modulation. This guide focuses on WiFi 8 devices and access points where 6 GHz and 320 MHz are supported, and it assumes a simple single AP environment to start. You will plan channel placement, prepare prerequisites, apply settings safely, and verify with objective checks. Where uncertainty exists, you will use decision rules that favor repeatable performance over theoretical peak rates, especially when mixed client types share the spectrum.

Key takeaways

  • Confirm 6 GHz, 320 MHz, and regulatory support before changing router settings.
  • Reserve a clean primary 6 GHz channel and disable legacy coexistence features.
  • Start with moderate power, then validate MCS and retries under light load.
  • Use firmware that exposes 320 MHz width controls and country enforcement.
  • Measure client effective throughput, not only link rate, for real gains.

Plan the work

Start by defining the objective and constraints for enabling 320 MHz on your router. A clear target might be improving single stream throughput in a short range office where walls are thin and neighboring 6 GHz activity is low. In that scenario, a wider channel can lift top end rates if the client and access point both support 802.11be widths and the regulatory domain allows the chosen center channel. The tradeoff is simple. Wider spectrum reduces spectral reuse and can increase susceptibility to localized interference. That means you should plan for a controlled test area first, then expand. A rule of thumb is to validate in a one room environment within ten meters line of sight before rolling out across a larger floor plan.

Map the spectrum conditions and regulatory restrictions that govern 6 GHz in your country. Some regions allow multiple 320 MHz allocations while others restrict power or disable certain center channels. You do not need precise measurements to start, but collecting a quick scan from a modern analyzer app helps reveal neighboring service sets. If the noise floor is elevated or several nearby APs occupy overlapping punctured patterns, a 160 MHz baseline may produce steadier results. Planning for a fallback prevents downtime during busy hours. Capture your intended center frequency, a candidate backup width, and a note on transmit power so you can revert quickly if stability drops after the change.

Account for client mix and application sensitivity before you change settings. High bitrate local transfers to a single workstation benefit from 320 MHz when signal is strong and modulation coding schemes stay high. Conversely, latency sensitive voice traffic or many low capability devices may suffer if the airtime becomes fragmented or if multi link operation is not enabled. A practical path is to stage changes during a maintenance window and isolate one SSID on 6 GHz for testing, leaving existing 5 GHz service untouched. That separation lets you compare real outcomes while keeping production users stable. Document success criteria such as minimum sustained throughput and acceptable retry rates to avoid guessing later.

Prepare environment

Verify the router and clients truly support 6 GHz and 320 MHz channel width. Vendor datasheets for WiFi 8 radios usually list 320 MHz capability, but firmware maturity determines whether the UI exposes these options. Check the release notes for your build and confirm the correct regulatory domain is set, since country code enforcement hides invalid widths and channels. If your clients are a mix of one and two stream devices, expect different results. A 1x1 handset may gain less from wide lanes than a 2x2 laptop at short range. When uncertainty persists, temporarily test with a known capable client so the router configuration is not blamed for client limitations.

Ensure your physical environment supports stable 6 GHz operation. Wide channels favor short range and clean line of sight. Place the AP centrally in the test room, elevate it on a shelf, and route it on wired backhaul rather than mesh until stability is proven. Disable band steering that might push test clients back to 5 GHz while you validate. Also check that legacy coexistence features like target wake time settings or airtime fairness do not accidentally throttle a single test device. Finally, snapshot the current configuration so you can rollback. Most routers offer a backup export, which is useful if a firmware upgrade is needed to expose the 320 MHz option later.

Clarify UI labels and defaults across firmware to avoid misconfiguration. Some interfaces present 320 MHz as channel width, others as bandwidth, and a few embed it under advanced 6 GHz radio settings. Center channel selection may be fixed to a small set that aligns to regional rules. If the UI mentions puncturing, it lets you mask interfered sub blocks while keeping the wide channel logically intact. That can help when a single subchannel is noisy, but it reduces theoretical peak rate. Use the smallest necessary puncture mask during early tests to simplify comparisons. Execute steps

Approach the configuration with a minimal set of changes so you isolate the effect of enabling 320 MHz. Start by duplicating your primary SSID into a 6 GHz only variant to keep comparisons clean. Then adjust the 6 GHz radio to set channel width to 320 MHz and choose a valid center channel. If the UI provides transmit power control, pick a moderate level rather than maximum to reduce distortion at close range. Leave advanced features like multi link operation disabled initially if they are experimental in your firmware. This sequencing helps you detect whether a single toggle breaks connectivity, and it creates an obvious rollback if the client fails to associate or the link rate looks inconsistent.

After saving, force a clean reconnect on the test client by forgetting the network and joining again. This step clears stale capabilities exchanged during earlier sessions and ensures the association includes the new extended channel width. Watch for the negotiated MCS and NSS in your client diagnostics. A 2x2 device near the AP should advertise and use high MCS indexes when the signal is strong. If you see low MCS at short range, suspect power settings, antenna placement, or interference. Some interfaces also show HE or EHT details for 6 GHz. Focus on whether the effective throughput improves on a single transfer, not just the reported link speed, because retries can quietly erode headline rates.

If your firmware supports channel puncturing, use it sparingly to salvage stability without abandoning the 320 MHz width. Mask a single interfered subchannel first, then retest. Overuse of masks complicates airtime scheduling and can confuse older clients. Likewise, be cautious with automatic channel selection during this phase. Automatic selection may pick a legal center channel that conflicts with neighbor deployments. Manual selection keeps your test controlled. Record each change and elapsed time to stable performance so you can later decide whether the complexity of wide channels is justified in your environment. The ordered steps below summarize the safest order of operations for typical routers.

  1. Create a 6 GHz test SSID: isolate changes from production service.
  2. Set country code correctly: expose valid 6 GHz centers and widths.
  3. Select 320 MHz bandwidth: choose the widest channel in radio settings.
  4. Pick a legal center channel: align selection with your regulatory domain.
  5. Apply moderate transmit power: reduce distortion and improve EVM near AP.
  6. Save and reboot the radio: force a clean reload of configuration state.
  7. Reconnect the client: re-associate to refresh negotiated capabilities.
  8. Verify MCS and retries: check client diagnostics for healthy modulation.

Validate results

Confirm the router is actually broadcasting at 320 MHz and that clients are using it effectively. Start with a simple throughput test like a single large file copy from a wired server to the test client in the same room. Compare results to a 160 MHz baseline you captured earlier. If the gain is marginal, check retry rates or application logs for signs of instability. Next, review radio statistics from the router UI. Look for consistent MCS near the top of your client capability and note signal to noise values. Big swings indicate environmental noise. In that case, a slightly different center channel may be better than holding a theoretically optimal but noisy allocation.

On devices that expose a Linux shell, you can verify channel width and center frequency directly. Use it only if your firmware provides shell access and the iw tool. The expected outcome is a line indicating channel width 320 and a center frequency consistent with your selection. If the output shows a smaller width, the regulatory domain or firmware may have limited the configuration silently and you should revisit earlier steps before proceeding with further tuning.

Validate client capabilities separately to avoid blaming the router for device limits. On Windows, inspect adapter properties for EHT support and stream count. On Android, recent builds display 6 GHz connection details that include channel and negotiated rate. Aim for repeatable test files and consistent distances, and capture three runs for each scenario. Stability across runs is more valuable than a single peak number. If throughput improves but latency or jitter worsens, adjust transmit power or position slightly and repeat. Keep notes on exact settings that produced acceptable balance. This discipline lets you decide whether 320 MHz remains enabled only for short range use or across the whole coverage area.

Troubleshoot and optimize

When associations fail or rates collapse, start with regulatory domain, center channel validity, and firmware version. An incorrect country code often hides wide width options or silently narrows the channel after a reboot. If clients connect but performance is erratic, inspect retry counters and error logs. High retries at short distance hint at distortion or poor antenna placement rather than congestion. Try stepping transmit power down one notch and retest. If the environment is busy, consider a narrowly punctured mask to sidestep a single interfered block. The tradeoff is a slight reduction in peak throughput for a larger improvement in stability. Keep each change small and retest so you learn which factor matters most.

Optimize for mixed clients by separating SSIDs or enabling advanced scheduling features carefully. Older devices with limited capabilities may waste airtime on a very wide lane, dragging down aggregate performance. Providing a dedicated 6 GHz SSID for modern hardware and leaving 5 GHz for everything else creates a simple way to manage expectations. If your firmware supports multi link operation and your clients implement it well, you might regain efficiency by letting the device steer sub flows intelligently. Be conservative until you see steady behavior under your workload. Test interactive applications and background transfers, not only bulk throughput tools, to judge end user perception accurately.

Finally, document the stable settings and consider whether the wide lane is the right default for your space. In dense environments with neighbors, the narrower 160 MHz plan may offer higher consistency and better spectral etiquette. Use routine spot checks to verify that no silent firmware updates change behavior. If you are deploying multiple APs, revisit your channel plan so wide allocations do not overlap. Frequently asked questions

Why do I not see 320 MHz in my router UI?

Your hardware or firmware may not support 6 GHz wide channels in your region. Check country code, update firmware, and verify the model lists 320 MHz capability. Some vendors hide controls until regulatory data is confirmed.

My client shows a high link rate but throughput is low

High retries or poor signal quality can erode effective throughput. Test at short range, lower transmit power slightly, and check MCS stability. If conditions are noisy, a narrower width or minimal puncturing can improve consistency.

Should I enable multi link operation at the same time?

Start without it to isolate the impact of 320 MHz. Enable MLO only after you confirm steady behavior and verify your clients implement it reliably. Mixed client environments sometimes behave better with a staged rollout.

Is 320 MHz worth it for long range coverage?

Wide channels favor short range and strong signals. At longer distances, modulation drops and retries increase, which can negate gains. In many spaces, 160 MHz offers a better balance of throughput and reliability.

Can I use mesh backhaul with 320 MHz enabled?

Use wired backhaul while testing to reduce variables. If you must use mesh, ensure backhaul stability first. Wide channels can reduce resilience to interference, so verify latency and retries before enabling for client traffic.
