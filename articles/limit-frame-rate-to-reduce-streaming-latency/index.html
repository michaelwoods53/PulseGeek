Limit Frame Rate to Reduce Streaming Latency

By Jacob Reed • Published January 26, 2026

Limiting frame rate can reduce streaming latency when the encoder, network, or decode path becomes the bottleneck. By lowering the frame rate target, each frame receives more encode time, smaller bitrate spikes, and steadier pacing for transport. On Steam Deck, this can transform choppy sessions into responsive play, especially with HEVC or AV1 under tight bandwidth. The tradeoff is reduced motion resolution, which may be noticeable in fast shooters. The trick is picking a cap that aligns with display refresh and encoder capability so you cut delay without obvious judder. This article explains the mechanisms, offers a decision framework, and provides examples that show when a 40, 45, or 50 FPS cap pays off and when higher rates remain preferable.

Key takeaways

  • Frame limits cut latency by lowering encode load and bitrate spikes.
  • Align caps with refresh to minimize judder and frame pacing errors.
  • Choose codec and bitrate per cap to balance clarity and delay.
  • Test 40 to 50 FPS when bandwidth or GPU budget is constrained.
  • Watch latency graphs to confirm reductions at the chosen frame rate.

Concepts and definitions

An FPS cap reduces streaming latency by shortening the queue between render, encode, and network send. When the game targets fewer frames, each frame’s render time rises slightly, yet the encoder gains headroom which lowers waiting time. For example, at 60 FPS with spikes to 16.7 ms render and 8 to 12 ms encode, the pipeline may queue. Capping to 45 FPS shifts render toward 22 ms, but encode can drop to 5 to 8 ms with fewer backlogs. The network also benefits because bitrate peaks flatten, reducing retransmit and jitter buffers. The downside is motion detail is sampled less often, which can blur fast panning. The mechanism works best when the bottleneck was encode or network rather than pure CPU simulation or GPU rendering.

Display refresh and synchronization determine whether a frame limit feels smooth or stuttery after the cap. Steam Deck commonly runs at 40, 50, or 60 Hz, and aligning the cap to a divisor or exact refresh avoids cadence conflicts. For instance, 40 FPS on a 40 Hz panel delivers one frame per refresh, minimizing frame pacing variance. At 45 FPS on 60 Hz, cadence alternates and can introduce rhythmic judder that masks latency gains. Picking 50 FPS on a 100 Hz display repeats each frame twice, which is predictable and responsive. The tradeoff is that lower refresh reduces perceived motion fluidity, so the ideal cap balances cadence simplicity with enough samples to keep inputs feeling crisp during quick camera turns or strafes.

Codec behavior makes caps more or less effective because compression latency varies by algorithm and hardware implementation. H.264 generally encodes faster at the same complexity than HEVC or AV1 on older GPUs, making high caps more viable. HEVC and AV1 can match or beat quality per bit but often need more cycles per frame, so a modest cap can materially reduce delay. On Steam Deck, decode time for HEVC is comfortable, while AV1 depends on decode support and bitrate. If the encoder saturates, capping stabilizes motion search and rate control, trimming waiting time before packetization. The cost is potential detail loss in quick motion scenes. When decode lacks hardware support, capping prevents the client from becoming the new bottleneck that erodes any latency savings.

Frameworks and decision lenses

A practical decision rule is cap until the bottleneck shifts off the encoder or network, then stop. Start at 60 FPS and monitor encode latency and network jitter in your streaming overlay. If encode exceeds roughly half the frame interval or jitter spikes near scene cuts, reduce to 50 or 45. The goal is encoder latency under about one third of the frame budget, which keeps queues small. If network send time remains spiky across caps, reduce bitrate or enable a more efficient codec instead of capping further. The tradeoff is diminishing returns past the point where encode and network are stable, because lower sampling reduces motion resolution without additional delay benefits that players can perceive.

Align caps with display refresh using simple divisors to maintain even pacing. On Steam Deck, try 40 FPS at 40 Hz when quality per bit is strained, because the tight cadence often feels more immediate than a jittery 50 or 60 with buffering. When bandwidth is stable and the encoder is near headroom, 50 FPS at 50 Hz preserves responsiveness while reducing queue pressure relative to 60. If your monitor supports VRR, a 45 to 50 FPS window can feel smooth if the client respects variable refresh. The caveat is some streaming clients quantize modes to fixed refresh steps, so verify the target is actually applied rather than silently rounded to a less favorable cadence.

Choose codec and bitrate in tandem with the cap, not after the fact, because rate control assumes a temporal cadence. With H.264, a moderate cap like 50 may allow a lower preset latency without artifacts, holding texture detail at the same bitrate. With HEVC, 40 to 50 FPS often stabilizes motion estimation for complex scenes, avoiding the need for aggressive B frame usage that adds delay. AV1 can produce excellent clarity per bit at 45 to 50 when hardware support exists. If the network is the constraint, prefer constant bitrate and a cap that trims spikes. If the encoder is the constraint, pick faster presets and a cap that keeps encode time well below the frame interval to prevent queuing.

Examples and short scenarios

When streaming an open world game with dense foliage using HEVC, a 60 FPS target may push the encoder to 10 to 12 ms with frequent spikes, leading to input lag. Capping at 45 drops encode closer to 6 to 8 ms and smooths bitrate bursts, which trims end to end delay noticeably. On Steam Deck’s 40 Hz mode, shifting to 40 FPS further regularizes cadence and may feel more immediate despite fewer frames. The cost is slightly softer motion in rapid camera sweeps, which is acceptable for exploration. If you observe blurrier foliage, consider a small bitrate increase rather than raising FPS. Where the LAN is robust but encoder cycles are thin, a faster H.264 preset at 50 can beat HEVC at 60 for total delay. If artifacts appear during strafing, trade a small bitrate increase for a cap that keeps encode time under one third of the frame window. Looking ahead

The durable approach is iterative: cap, measure, then refine bitrate or codec before changing the cap again. Start sessions at the highest stable rate, then step down by five FPS when encode latency or jitter exceeds a third of the frame window. After each change, watch client graphs for two minutes across heavy scenes to confirm the queue shrank. Adjust one variable at a time so you attribute improvements correctly. If multiple metrics improve simultaneously, lock the setting and test different game genres. The limitation is that overlays can misattribute delay if capture is enabled, so avoid extra recording during tests. This method steadily narrows toward a cap that feels quick while keeping artifacts controlled under typical home network variability.

Document your working presets per codec, because small differences matter once you cap. With H.264, a faster preset at a moderate cap can outperform a slower preset at higher FPS for delay. With HEVC, consider lowering B frames or using all intra on title screens to reduce spikes during transitions. For AV1, ensure hardware decode support on the client before adopting tighter caps that depend on lower bitrate for similar clarity. Keep bitrate ceilings realistic for your LAN, and revisit during peak usage hours where bufferbloat may rise. The value of notes is transferring what worked in one title to another without repeating a full test cycle, saving time and giving consistent input response across your library.

Finally, align expectations with hardware encoder capabilities and client updates that affect decode paths. A GPU driver that improves HEVC motion estimation can shift the optimal cap upward because encode time shrinks at the same quality. A client update that adds better VRR handling can make a 45 or 50 FPS target feel smoother than before. Re test quarterly or after major updates so your chosen cap keeps delivering low latency. If your results suggest bandwidth remains the dominant limit, consider reviewing how to tune overall bitrate alongside resolution to keep encode and transport efficient. Incremental improvements compound, and small, measured steps keep your streaming responsive even as your software stack evolves.

Frequently asked questions

Does lowering FPS always reduce latency for streaming?

No. It helps when the encoder or network is the bottleneck. If rendering is saturated or decode is slow, a cap may not improve latency and can reduce motion clarity without noticeable delay gains.

Is 40 FPS better than 60 FPS on Steam Deck?

It depends on cadence and bottlenecks. At 40 Hz, 40 FPS yields even pacing and can feel responsive, but if encode and network are stable at 60, the higher rate usually feels smoother for fast motion.

Should I change bitrate or codec before capping frame rate?

Probe the metrics first. If network jitter or bitrate spikes dominate, adjust bitrate or use a more efficient codec. If encode time is high, a moderate cap often reduces queueing more effectively than bitrate tweaks alone.

What frame rate cap works best for shooters?

Try 50 FPS at 50 Hz if 60 is unstable. It often preserves aim feel while reducing queue pressure. If encode or network still spikes, experiment with 45 FPS and a faster preset to cut delay further.

How do I verify that the cap reduced latency?

Watch the streaming overlay for encode time, network jitter, and total pipeline delay across challenging scenes. Look for lower peaks and fewer spikes after the change. Test for several minutes to confirm stability.
