Fix Audio Latency in RetroArch on Miyoo Mini

By Jeremy Crawford • Published January 4, 2026

Audio latency on a Miyoo Mini usually traces back to RetroArch timing choices, buffer sizes, and sync interactions. This guide targets a practical fix path with safe defaults and measurable checks so changes feel deliberate, not lucky. We assume a stock RetroArch build on the handheld and a standard SD layout. You will tune audio latency, verify sync, and keep stability by avoiding aggressive options. No third party tools are required, and every tweak remains reversible. Where tradeoffs exist, you will learn why a setting helps smooth playback, what risk it adds, and when to stop turning knobs. The goal is clean sound without crackle, with button presses lining up closely to effects in-game.

Key takeaways

  • Lower audio latency gradually and confirm stability after each change.
  • Keep audio sync on while avoiding aggressive frame hard sync settings.
  • Use small rate control to hide drift without audible artifacts.
  • Validate with timed inputs and sustained tones for repeatable results.
  • Log each setting so rollback is instant if crackle returns.

Plan the work

Start with a clear goal that balances responsiveness with stability, because audio latency interacts with frame pacing on handheld hardware. Aim to reduce delay until button sounds align naturally with input while keeping stutter and crackle absent. A common safe target is lowering buffers one step at a time, testing for at least five minutes per game scene with mixed effects. Use one fast-action title and one slower title to spot different stressors. Record your starting configuration and any changes in a simple text note so you can revert instantly. The tradeoff to remember is that too small a buffer often introduces pops, while too large a buffer feels sluggish. Success means perceptibly tighter response without artifacts, not chasing absolute minimum numbers.

Define your constraints upfront to avoid chasing fixes that your system cannot sustain. RetroArch’s audio pipeline can sync to video, apply rate control, and buffer audio frames to hide drift, but each knob moves pressure elsewhere. For instance, enabling tight video hard sync may improve timing at the expense of GPU headroom, which can cause frame drops on compact devices. In contrast, audio rate control applies minute resampling to maintain lock without heavy CPU spikes. Plan to touch only a few settings per round, and test under fixed conditions. A controlled approach avoids confounding variables and helps you identify which change solved which symptom, such as delayed menu beeps or inconsistent battle effects.

Decide on validation methods before changing anything so you can detect improvements reliably. Use repeatable triggers like menu cursor ticks or a known in-game jump sound that follows a button press. Count off beats or use a metronome app nearby to keep timing consistent, but do not record numbers unless you trust your method to be repeatable. Instead, compare relative feel between baseline and candidate settings. The limitation of subjective testing is bias, so add stress tests like busy scenes or long map transitions to expose crackle and underruns. Choosing these tests now reduces the temptation to keep tweaking indefinitely and keeps the focus on a stable, responsive end state.

Document the current audio driver and whether audio sync is enabled, since these shape how latency behaves. If you rely on per-game overrides, note them as well to avoid accidental conflicts during tests. A limitation here is that different cores may have their own timing quirks, so you will validate on at least two systems when possible. A clean baseline prevents chasing problems introduced by unrelated tweaks.

Check storage health and free space, because SD hiccups can masquerade as audio problems. Fragmented or nearly full cards may slow asset streaming or save writes, leading to intermittent stutter. Free a few hundred megabytes to give the system breathing room, and ensure write protection is off. While this step rarely fixes pure latency by itself, it removes a nasty variable that can make timing feel inconsistent. Consider scanning for file system errors if you have experienced random crashes. The reason is simple: consistent I/O keeps RetroArch’s real-time tasks predictable. If you skip this, you may mistake storage stalls for buffer underruns and over-tighten audio settings beyond what the device can handle.

Execute steps

Begin with core RetroArch audio controls that influence latency predictably, then verify stability before moving deeper. Reduce audio_latency by small increments, keep audio_sync enabled, and allow mild audio_rate_control to smooth drift. This triad reduces delay while avoiding constant resyncs that cause pops. Avoid aggressive video hard sync on limited hardware, since it can starve the pipeline and create stutter that sounds like audio trouble. If your current setup already crackles, first raise latency slightly to restore headroom, then step back down. The why is straightforward: a small buffer absorbs timing jitter from cores, while sync and rate control keep video and audio aligned without heavy CPU spikes that handhelds dislike.

If you prefer file edits for reproducibility, update a few lines in retroarch.cfg instead of clicking through menus. This keeps changes auditable and easy to revert. Validate results

Validate with repeatable audio cues so you do not rely on memory. Use a menu cursor sound or a consistent in-game beep tied directly to a button press. Tap ten times at a steady pace and judge whether the sound feels anchored to your input. Then hold a sustained tone if your game offers one and listen for wavering that suggests resampling is too aggressive. Repeat the same test at your baseline to compare. This approach exposes both short delay and long drift. The limitation is that human perception varies, so you should also seek objective flags like visible frame hitches or logged underruns in the on-screen display if available.

Cross-check more than one core because timing budgets differ by system and emulator implementation. A platform with heavier CPU usage may exhibit underruns sooner at the same latency setting. Choose a lightweight system like NES and a mid-weight system such as SNES or GBA to probe both ends. If one remains clean while another crackles, your global settings might be acceptable but a per-core override is warranted. This nuance helps you avoid overcorrecting globally when only a demanding core needs extra buffer. The why is practical: matching the buffer to load preserves responsiveness where possible without sacrificing stability everywhere else.

Troubleshoot and optimize

When pops or crackle appear, treat them as underrun signals and restore headroom. Increase audio_latency modestly or disable any newly added hard sync setting. Verify that your frame rate is stable in demanding scenes, because video drops often sound like audio faults. If the handheld warms significantly during long sessions, consider a tiny buffer increase to absorb occasional CPU dips. Another remedy is reducing expensive visual filters so the audio pipeline remains prioritized. The idea is to fix the source of starvation rather than masking it with extreme rate control. Pushing too hard invites artifacts that feel worse than slight delay and can discourage continued tuning.

Use per-core overrides to localize compromises instead of sacrificing global responsiveness. If a single core needs extra latency, create an override that retains your snappy defaults elsewhere. Keep rate control gentle, since large deltas can cause pitch shimmer on sustained notes. Monitor whether frameskip options are active in a problematic core, because aggressive skipping can desync perception even if audio is technically in lock. Where visual crispness matters more than latency, you might adopt scaling strategies that do not cost frame time. This keeps improvements resilient across updates. Frequently asked questions

Why do I hear pops after lowering audio latency?

Pops usually indicate underruns where the buffer empties during busy scenes. Increase audio_latency slightly, keep audio_sync on, and retest. If stutter persists, reduce costly visuals or disable hard sync to restore headroom.

Should I disable audio sync to reduce delay?

Disabling audio sync can shrink delay but risks drift and resync clicks. A better approach is small audio buffers with audio_sync enabled and modest rate control to keep lock without artifacts.

What if only one emulator core crackles?

Use a per-core override so the heavier core gets a larger buffer while global settings remain responsive. This targets the problem without penalizing lighter systems.

How can I tell if rate control hurts sound quality?

Hold a sustained tone and listen for pitch shimmer. If you notice wobble, reduce audio_rate_control_delta in small steps until the artifact disappears while sync remains stable.

Do shaders affect audio latency?

Shaders increase GPU work and can reduce frame headroom, which indirectly causes underruns that sound like audio problems. Test with simple scaling first, then reintroduce visual effects gradually.
