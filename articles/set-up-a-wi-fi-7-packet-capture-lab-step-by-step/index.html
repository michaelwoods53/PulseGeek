Set Up a Wi‑Fi 7 Packet Capture Lab: Step by Step

By Allison Grant • Published February 4, 2026

This guide shows how to set up a Wi‑Fi 7 packet capture lab that yields reproducible traces. You will plan RF paths carefully, configure monitor mode adapters, and validate timing fidelity before relying on results. We assume a Linux capture host, access to channels across 2.4, 5, and 6 GHz, and at least one adapter capable of Enhanced High Throughput features. If you are new to amendment terminology, skim a standard overview first, then return to the practical steps. The workflow balances clarity and rigor so that later analysis benefits from clean metadata, stable timestamps, and known interference boundaries. By following a stepwise approach, your lab reduces guesswork and produces captures suitable for debugging MLO behavior and interoperability scenarios.

Key takeaways

  • Define RF scope first to control interference and capture fidelity.
  • Select adapters that truly support EHT channelization and MLO fields.
  • Pin timestamps to a single clock source for stable trace timing.
  • Record capture parameters alongside pcap files for repeatability.
  • Validate radiotap fields and bandwidth to verify Wi‑Fi 7 signaling.

Plan the work

Start by declaring your capture objective, because scope dictates lab boundaries and tool choices. For example, if you intend to analyze Multi Link Operation control exchanges, you need visibility into both links and accurate radiotap headers. That implies dual radios or a sniffer supporting concurrent channels. A narrower goal like EHT channel puncturing on a single band demands less hardware but stricter channel selection. Define success metrics such as decoding EHT-SIG fields, capturing beacons across 20 to 320 MHz, or extracting stable RSSI distributions. The tradeoff is granularity versus complexity. Wider bandwidth capability increases insight but raises noise susceptibility and storage needs. Anchoring goals early prevents rework and sets realistic expectations for equipment and time.

Next, model your RF environment, since interference control determines capture quality. Decide whether to use open air with careful isolation, near-field shielding, or a small anechoic chamber. Open air is convenient but invites variable co-channel traffic. Chambers reduce that uncertainty but require calibration and can hide corner cases like reflections you might see in homes. Sketch a path diagram that includes antennas, attenuators, splitters, and optional bandpass filters. Assign target received signal levels at the sniffer within a practical range, for instance between roughly −35 dBm and −65 dBm to avoid clipping or lost frames. This explicit map helps you predict failure modes and quickly adjust when captures do not show expected EHT preambles.

Formalize channels and bandwidths you will monitor, because Wi‑Fi 7 introduces wider options and new puncturing patterns. Pick center frequencies that match the device under test and note any regional constraints for 6 GHz operation. Plan primary and secondary channels in 5 GHz when mimicking legacy behavior, and define 320 MHz scenarios only if the sniffer hardware can process them without downclocking. Document MCS ranges and modulation you expect to see so you can spot anomalies later. The limitation here is hardware capability. Some adapters claim EHT support but actually truncate radiotap fields or misreport bandwidth. By fixing a discrete matrix of channels and bandwidths, you can iterate methodically and reduce noise in your analysis.

Prepare environment

Select capture hardware that truly supports monitor mode and EHT fields, because feature mismatches silently corrupt results. Check chipset support matrices for 6 GHz, 320 MHz bandwidth, and radiotap parsing in your preferred tools. A second adapter for control or legacy-channel visibility is helpful when tracking coexistence events. Antennas with appropriate bandwidth and a reasonable gain profile improve consistency, while attenuators prevent receiver saturation in near-field setups. As a reference for requirements across devices, review a practical certification guide that outlines lab setup and over the air methods, then map your bench choices to it. Budget constraints may force compromises, but prioritize reliable monitor mode and stable timestamps over luxury analyzers.

Harden the host operating system, because kernel and driver versions control capture stability. Use a long term support Linux distribution with recent wireless drivers and disable power saving on capture interfaces. Keep time synchronized with a local NTP server so multiple sniffers share a stable clock base. For storage, ensure sustained write throughput for large pcaps by using an SSD and preallocating capture directories. Create a non root workflow with capabilities like CAP_NET_ADMIN and CAP_NET_RAW to avoid risky privilege escalations. This reduces operational friction and preserves logs if a process crashes. The downside is extra setup time, but the result is fewer dropped packets and clearer timing relationships across links.

Lay out your bench and RF path, because physical organization simplifies repeatability. Place the device under test and sniffer antennas at fixed distances, marking positions on the table or chamber floor. Use low loss coax for conducted paths and document each attenuator value. Label every cable and capture interface so scripts can target them deterministically. If you plan to test interoperability across amendments, reserve channels for 802.11ax clients to reduce cross traffic confusion. For a deep technical baseline on amendment behavior and timelines, study a clause aware standard overview that explains how the newer features integrate with base operations. This background informs which frames must appear and which belong to fallback modes.

Execute steps

Before starting, confirm the device under test is active on planned channels, because alignment avoids empty pcaps and guesswork. Use a spectrum snapshot or brief passive scan to verify center frequency and bandwidth. Then place the sniffer in monitor mode with the correct channelization and disable offloading or filtering that could hide control frames. Prepare a run sheet that records date, channel, bandwidth, MCS hints, and link role. Include storage rotation criteria such as file size or time to prevent single huge files. Choosing ring buffers trades convenience for potential boundary loss, while single files simplify indexing but can grow unwieldy. Start with conservative rotations and adjust once you know typical frame rates.

Run a baseline capture that intentionally includes beacons, association, and a small data transfer, because you need anchor points to judge decoding. After verifying radiotap presence and expected fields like bandwidth and RU allocation, proceed to specific scenarios such as MLO setup or triggered multi RU. Keep the sniffer at signal levels that avoid clipping. If RSSI is consistently stronger than roughly −30 dBm, add attenuation. If frames sporadically vanish at −70 dBm, reduce path loss. Record any anomalies in a text log colocated with the pcap so later analysis tools can load context. This practice helps correlate environmental changes with missing headers or odd EHT SIG interpretations.

When automation is required, script capture commands and parameter logs, because reproducibility depends on precise invocation. Use explicit interface names, numeric channels, and bandwidth flags to prevent driver heuristics from shifting behavior. Include timestamps in filenames and write a JSON sidecar with the parameters used for each run. The tradeoff is initial scripting overhead, but in return you gain consistent results across operators and days. If your toolchain supports it, enable per packet timestamp precision and preserve original radiotap fields. These details will later help debug time correlation across two links during MLO testing. Keep scripts under version control so improvements do not obscure past configurations.

Validate results

Begin validation by opening your baseline pcap and inspecting radiotap headers, because these reflect how well your sniffer reports physical details. Confirm decoded bandwidth matches the configured channel, and look for EHT specific fields where supported by the tool version. If fields are missing, update dissectors or check driver capabilities. Validate timestamps by measuring intervals between beacons and comparing against nominal target values. Large jitter hints at clock or load problems. The tradeoff is that dissector updates may change field names, so lock tool versions for repeatability. This disciplined check ensures that subsequent MLO flows and control exchanges sit on top of trustworthy metadata.

Cross verify with a second tool or adapter when possible, because independent perspectives expose silent decoder or driver quirks. Capture the same scenario with tshark after using tcpdump and compare link layer counters, bandwidth detection, and PHY header parsing. If both agree, your confidence grows. If they diverge, search release notes for known issues and constrain analysis to fields both handle consistently. When examining performance traces, pair capture sessions with throughput or latency measurements so you can correlate PHY annotations with observed application behavior. For method guidance on measurement consistency, review a practical writeup on getting repeatable throughput and latency results.

Finally, replay your planned matrix of channels and bandwidths, because edge configurations often surface misconfiguration or hardware limits. Include at least one case near your widest bandwidth and one at a narrow channel to verify stable decoding across extremes. Check that RU allocation and puncturing make sense for traffic mix, and ensure your sniffer did not drift to a neighbor channel under load. Save validation notes with explicit tool versions and kernel identifiers so future operators can reproduce your findings. The limitation is time, but systematic coverage prevents costly misinterpretations during later debugging cycles and makes your capture lab dependable for ongoing work.

Troubleshoot and optimize

When frames are missing or decoding fails, inspect RF levels first, because overload or deep fades cause silent loss. Adjust attenuators to keep RSSI in a middle range where the ADC performs well. If delivered power is too high, expect CRC errors and truncated payloads. If too low, control frames disappear and timing analysis collapses. Next, check channel center and bandwidth, since misalignment by one notch yields plausible but incorrect decodes. Interference from adjacent networks can masquerade as puncturing patterns. A practical remedy is a short conducted test to isolate your path, then returning to over the air for realism. This isolates whether contention or configuration drives the loss.

For unstable timestamps or intermittent gaps, profile host resource usage, because capture tools contend with CPU and disk I/O. Pin processes to dedicated cores, raise capture priority, and route writes to an SSD that sustains your expected data rate. Consider reducing snap length if you only need headers for protocol analysis. Enable a local NTP server and verify offset stays within a tight bound across the session. If offsets drift, avoid correlating inter device timing metrics. The tradeoff is reduced payload visibility when trimming snaplen, but for control centric studies the benefit outweighs the loss. These resource safeguards lower jitter and keep trace timing dependable.

To improve depth when analyzing Wi‑Fi 7 features, extend your lab with a second synchronized sniffer, because multi link events are inherently distributed. Start both captures against the same time source and annotate which interface targets which channel. Use sidecar logs to mark expected events such as association and link setup. Add a small batch of interoperability exercises with legacy clients to ensure your lab decodes fallback behavior reliably. If toolchain capability diverges across vendors, note differences in EHT field naming. Maintaining a compatibility ledger helps analysts avoid misreads and saves rework. This iterative tuning turns a functioning bench into a robust capability for ongoing engineering.

Quick start checklist

  1. Pin your goal: decide which frames and features you must capture.
  2. Stabilize the host: update drivers and lock time to a local NTP source.
  3. Verify channels: scan or sweep to confirm center frequency and bandwidth.
  4. Enable monitor mode: set interface type and explicit EHT channelization.
  5. Record parameters: log channel, bandwidth, tool versions, and RSSI range.
  6. Run a baseline: capture beacons and association before scenario specifics.
  7. Check radiotap: validate bandwidth fields and timestamp stability first.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need 320 MHz support to study Wi‑Fi 7 behavior?

No. Many analyses are valid at 80 or 160 MHz. If your goal includes 320 MHz puncturing or highest MCS validation, ensure hardware and drivers decode those widths reliably first.

Why are radiotap EHT fields missing in my capture?

Your tool or driver may not export those fields. Update dissectors, verify adapter capabilities, and check that the channel and bandwidth match the device under test.

How do I stabilize timestamps across two sniffers?

Use a shared NTP or PTP source, keep both hosts lightly loaded, and write to fast storage. Avoid correlating sub millisecond events if offsets exceed your tolerance.

Should I capture full payloads or headers only?

Capture headers when protocol analysis is the goal, as shorter snap length reduces I O pressure. Capture payloads only if content is essential for the study.

What if my sniffer shows the wrong channel under load?

Lock channel settings explicitly in commands and disable roaming or auto selection. Verify with a quick passive scan before each scenario run.
