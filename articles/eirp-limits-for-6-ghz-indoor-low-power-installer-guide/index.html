EIRP Limits for 6 GHz Indoor Low Power: Installer Guide

By Courtney Lawson • Published February 20, 2026

Installers ask how EIRP, 6 GHz spectrum, and low power rules interact, because wrong assumptions can erase link stability or risk noncompliance. This guide frames indoor limits through power spectral density, antennas, and bandwidth so you can predict headroom before turning knobs. You will see how regulatory intent translates into practical constraints that still allow robust service levels when planned methodically.

Key takeaways

  • EIRP is governed by PSD across bandwidth, not a single knob.
  • Antenna gain boosts EIRP but also tightens PSD headroom.
  • Bandwidth changes shift PSD limits and affect usable MCS rates.
  • Region rules differ, so templates must be country aware.
  • Verify with calibrated tools, not only controller dashboards.

Concepts and definitions

EIRP expresses radiated power including antenna gain, and for indoor low power at 6 GHz it is constrained by power spectral density across the occupied channel. The practical meaning is that widening bandwidth spreads power, so per megahertz levels must remain within the permitted envelope. For example, a 160 MHz channel concentrates less energy per megahertz than 80 MHz at the same total output, which can help meet PSD while maintaining throughput headroom. The tradeoff is that noise aggregation and adjacent channel activity can reduce effective rates if the environment is congested. Understanding EIRP as antenna gain plus conducted power, bounded by PSD, is essential because tuning one variable affects the others. This interdependence explains why a simple target number rarely guarantees compliance or performance in real deployments.

Indoor low power rules rely on PSD to reduce interference risk to incumbents and neighboring systems while still enabling practical service levels. In planning terms, that means you should think in density of energy over frequency rather than chasing a single total power figure. For instance, holding PSD steady while doubling bandwidth will raise total EIRP but keep spectral intensity constant, which aligns with regulatory intent. The limitation is that some client radios may not support wide channels or may prefer narrower allocations for stability. As a result, wide settings can leave underutilized capacity or complicate multiuser scheduling. Considering PSD first forces a security minded design where coexistence is built in and unintentional leakage is minimized by construction.

Antenna gain modifies EIRP directly, which is helpful for shaping cells but reduces remaining conducted power you can apply under the same PSD limit. A ceiling mounted omnidirectional element may provide modest gain that keeps patterns uniform, while a directional antenna can extend coverage for a corridor with tighter beams. The tradeoff is that higher gain reduces your permissible transmitter level to avoid exceeding the EIRP cap, and beam sharpness can create dead zones behind partitions. Installers should choose antennas to match the intended service area rather than to chase range, because 6 GHz attenuation through walls is already high. By mapping the pattern to the room geometry, you respect EIRP limits while improving signal to interference and reducing unnecessary spill.

Frameworks and decision lenses

A useful lens is bandwidth budgeting, which starts with target applications and computes feasible channel width under PSD constraints and client capabilities. If latency sensitive uses dominate, moderate widths can stabilize scheduling and reduce collisions while still honoring EIRP. When throughput is the priority in low interference environments, wider channels provide headroom without violating PSD, but only if clients and backhaul can use them. The tradeoff is that wider channels consume more spectrum and may complicate reuse plans on dense floors. By mapping application needs to bandwidth choices, you keep compliance predictable and avoid chasing peak numbers that collapse under realistic client mixes. This method also pairs cleanly with channel reuse diagrams to preserve isolation between adjacent rooms.

A second lens is antenna gain accounting, where you set a design gain budget before selecting hardware. Begin by defining the maximum pattern gain that still lets conducted power meet PSD limits across your intended bandwidth, then shortlist antennas that stay within that envelope. This avoids later surprises where a tempting high gain element forces lower transmitter levels and starves edge devices. The downside is reduced flexibility if teams later change furniture layouts or add partitions. Still, documenting a gain budget formalizes tradeoffs and helps operations staff adjust safely when replacing units. It also strengthens change control, because any antenna swap becomes a measurable risk against EIRP and coexistence rather than an informal tweak.

A third lens is region awareness, acknowledging that 6 GHz allocations and indoor low power definitions vary by country and may evolve. Treat regulatory domains as inputs to templates, not post install checks, and ensure your controller profiles keep country codes consistent with physical locations. The benefit is predictable behavior in roaming and power control, avoiding hidden failures from mismatched client assumptions. The limitation is increased configuration discipline and the need to track updates from regulators. A practical mitigation is to maintain a versioned matrix of allowed channel sets and power policies by region, reviewed during change windows. Pitfalls, limitations, and edge cases

A common pitfall is assuming total power settings map directly to allowed EIRP without considering antenna gain and occupied bandwidth. This shortcut can push a deployment past limits when channel width or antennas change during site refreshes. A concrete scenario is an AP upgraded to a model with higher default gain, which silently narrows headroom and forces lower conducted power to remain within PSD. The tradeoff is that while received signal improves in some directions, clients at angles off boresight may see reduced rates. The fix is to inventory antenna patterns during procurement and tie them to power templates, enforcing checks whenever hardware part numbers change. This discipline prevents unintentional overruns while keeping performance predictable across rooms.

Another edge case involves mixed bandwidth clients where legacy devices prefer 40 or 80 MHz while newer radios can use 160 MHz or more. If the network advertises wide channels but real traffic concentrates on narrower allocations, the PSD assumptions may not match practical use. The risk is perceived underperformance if scheduling does not align with the effective occupied spectrum. A mitigation is to set channel widths based on observed client distributions and reevaluate during peak seasons. Installers also trip over country code mismatches, especially during pilots where lab gear is repurposed for office floors. Controllers may allow operation, but client devices can interpret beacons differently, leading to unstable rates or connection failures that resemble interference. The limitation is that dashboards rarely surface the nuance, so teams chase ghosts in spectrum analyzers. The better approach is to gate changes on a simple checklist that verifies regulatory domain, antenna types, and configured bandwidth against the intended location. Looking ahead

The next step is to formalize verification so PSD and EIRP remain traceable across changes. Use calibrated test gear or trusted measurement modes to audit representative APs after configuration updates, recording occupied bandwidth, antenna part numbers, and observed spectral levels. The limitation is time, so target high risk zones first like conference areas with variable furniture and high client churn. Document readings alongside floor plans to build a baseline that operations can validate after firmware or hardware swaps. This practice moves compliance from a one time setup to a continuous control, which is necessary as environments shift and tenants rotate.

Teams should also evolve channel plans with coexistence in mind, revisiting reuse patterns when density increases or new services appear. A small change in neighboring occupancy can upend the balance between throughput and stability, especially at 6 GHz where attenuation is high yet reflections still matter. Consider stepping bandwidth down one notch in contested areas to preserve scheduling efficiency and maintain PSD clarity, then recover capacity via spatial reuse elsewhere. Frequently asked questions

Does wider bandwidth always increase EIRP for indoor low power?

No. PSD limits govern energy per megahertz. Widening bandwidth can raise total EIRP while keeping spectral density compliant, but only if transmitter settings and antenna gain respect the permitted envelope.

How does antenna gain affect allowed transmitter power?

Antenna gain adds to EIRP, so higher gain reduces the conducted power you can apply under the same limit. Choose patterns that fit rooms so you preserve headroom and avoid unintentional overshoot.

Why do some clients underperform on very wide channels?

Client radios vary in channel support and scheduler behavior. If traffic concentrates on narrower widths, wide settings can increase contention or reduce effective rates. Match configured bandwidth to observed client use.

Are indoor low power limits identical across countries?

No. Definitions, allocations, and power policies differ by region and may change. Treat regulatory domain as a design input and keep country aware templates to maintain compliance during rollout.
