Continuous vs Discrete Collision: When Precision Matters

By Ethan Palmer • Published December 22, 2025

Fast movers challenge physics because collision checks happen between frames. When objects are small, fast, or both, discrete sampling can miss contact, while continuous methods predict sweeps to preserve precision. The decision often hinges on whether tunneling changes gameplay or only visual polish. A grenade clipping through a door ruins fairness, but a pebble slipping through a distant fence may pass unnoticed. We will compare continuous collision detection with discrete approaches using clear evaluation criteria and realistic constraints. Along the way, we will reference core physics concepts and point to deeper reading like a full guide to collisions, rigid bodies, and solvers for those who want broader context.

Key takeaways

  • Use continuous detection on fast, small, or critical gameplay objects.
  • Prefer discrete checks for slow, large, or noncritical interactables.
  • Mix modes and isolate layers to bound performance cost safely.
  • Adjust timesteps, restitution, and solver iterations before enabling CCD globally.
  • Test edge cases like grazing hits and stacked contacts across devices.
  • Profile per scene and cap velocities to reduce continuous workload.

Evaluation criteria and how to weigh them

Start with accuracy: if missing a hit changes outcomes, favor continuous detection for that object. A classic case is a bullet crossing several meters per frame, where a discrete sample might skip the wall. If the item determines win or loss, or blocks progression, treat the collision as critical. The tradeoff is time spent sweeping shapes and resolving earlier contacts, which can raise CPU cost and queue lengths. Mitigate by enabling continuous for a minimal set like projectiles, dashes, or falling hazards. Consider a rule of thumb: if an object’s linear travel exceeds its smallest dimension during a frame, elevate its accuracy tier and evaluate continuous options.

Next weigh performance, expressed as total physics step time under normal and stress conditions. Discrete checks scale well for crowds and props, while continuous sweeps are heavier, especially with convex hulls or complex meshes. Use representative scenarios like dense firefights and debris showers to profile worst cases. When spikes appear, check alternatives before broad CCD rollout: cap velocities, increase collider size slightly, or restructure interactions. For example, a shotgun can raycast pellets on fire rather than simulating dozens of fast rigid bodies. The goal is to keep budget predictable by reserving continuous passes for the few items where tunneling creates unfair or confusing results.

Finally consider stability and secondary effects that arise from earlier contact resolution. Continuous methods may detect collisions sooner along a path, which changes stacking order, restitution, and friction work. That can improve believable bounces yet may also introduce jitter on thin surfaces or at grazing angles. Test corner cases like skimming a slope, threading narrow doorways, and landing on moving platforms. If you see chattering or micro penetrations, try modest solver iteration increases or slightly thicker colliders before abandoning discrete or enabling CCD globally. Stability hinges on how your engine resolves constraints, so small changes to timestep or contact bias often tame artifacts without enlarging the continuous set.

Deep dives per attribute with tradeoffs and concrete examples

High speed accuracy drives the headline decision, because tunneling is the defect players notice first. If a projectile traverses more than its smallest collider dimension during a single frame, a discrete sample risks missing a wall. Continuous methods sweep along the trajectory and detect impact earlier, preserving hits and bounces. Yet CCD can still fail when surfaces are ultra thin or when steps are so large that even the sweep misses subframe motion. Counter this with velocity caps and slightly thicker proxy colliders on fragile geometry. For melee dashes, consider hybrid logic that enables continuous only during the dash window to reduce constant overhead.

CPU cost often determines how wide you can deploy continuous detection. Swept volumes and time of impact calculations are more expensive than point in time checks, particularly with concave meshes or many overlapping candidates. Before turning CCD on for everything, prune interactions with collision layers and broadphase masks. For projectiles, a limited raycast at fire time can substitute for full rigid body simulation while still resolving the first hit deterministically. When continuous is necessary, prefer simpler collider shapes like capsules or boxes that sweep efficiently. The practical goal is predictable frame time across busy scenes rather than spotless accuracy everywhere.

Stability and rebound quality depend on when contacts are formed and how the solver iterates. Because continuous methods register impact earlier, they generally produce cleaner restitution and fewer deep penetrations that require aggressive bias. However, grazing contacts can jitter if solver iterations are too low or if friction coefficients are unrealistic. A modest increase to position or velocity iterations often helps more than a blanket CCD rollout. If stacks chatter with thin objects, consider broadening contact offsets or thickening key colliders by small margins. Fit by scenario with short, explicit recommendations

Projectiles and hitscan stand at opposite ends of cost and determinism. For bullets and rockets, prefer discrete only if speed times timestep stays below collider thickness. Otherwise enable continuous on the projectile or switch to raycast on fire with a short lived trail effect for visuals. If explosives spray fragments, simulate a few primary shards with continuous and handle the rest with ray queries to limit sweeps. For melee dashes that cross thin doors, enable continuous just for the dash window, then revert to discrete during normal movement. This pattern controls overhead while protecting moments where a miss would feel unfair.

Characters, crowds, and props usually thrive on discrete checks combined with sensible timesteps and collider sizes. Walking speed and animation blending keep interframe travel small, making a discrete default reliable and fast. When lifts or moving platforms create quick relative motion, continuous can help on the platform rather than the rider, reducing total sweeps. If you ship across devices, test extreme frame pacing to ensure discrete stays stable under spikes. If jitter appears, consider fixed timestep adjustments and friction tuning before enabling continuous globally. For platforming, a kinematic mover with careful body configuration often prevents micro penetrations without requiring continuous on every actor.

Fragile or thin level geometry like vents, railings, and glass deserves special handling. Slightly thickened proxy colliders reduce misses for discrete and improve contact manifolds for any mode. When accuracy must be pristine, enable continuous on the few fast bodies that interact with these surfaces and filter collisions so sweeps ignore irrelevant layers. For more nuance on broadphase versus narrowphase choices in such situations, you can compare sweeping strategies with precision checks and decide where to tune each. Navigating these edges is about picking the least intrusive safeguard that preserves intent while keeping the physics step budget predictable across complex scenes.

Frequently asked questions

Does continuous collision detection always prevent tunneling?

No. It reduces tunneling but can still fail with very thin geometry, extreme timesteps, or unrealistic velocities. Thicker proxies, velocity caps, and tested timesteps are still important safeguards.

Should I enable continuous collision detection for every rigid body?

No. It is more expensive and unnecessary for slow or large objects. Reserve it for fast, small, or gameplay critical actors to protect accuracy without harming frame time.

Is raycasting a valid replacement for simulated projectiles?

Often yes. For hitscan weapons, a raycast per shot gives deterministic first hits with low cost. Use visual trails for feedback and keep physics bodies for rare explosives or ricochets.

What should I try before turning on CCD globally?

Reduce timestep size within budget, cap velocities, thicken critical colliders, tune friction and restitution, and use collision layers to prune contacts. Test stress scenes before expanding CCD coverage.
