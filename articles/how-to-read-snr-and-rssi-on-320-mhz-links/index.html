How to Read SNR and RSSI on 320 MHz Links

By Rachel Morgan • Published February 15, 2026

This how-to explains how to read SNR and RSSI for 320 MHz WiFi links, set practical thresholds, and confirm numbers across tools. You will need access to a controller, a capable 6 GHz client, and a quiet test area where you can control distance. We assume standard WiFi 8 features like wide channels and puncturing may be present and that you can temporarily reduce background load. By the end, you will know how to translate reported signal strength into throughput expectations, when a noisy floor skews results, and which counters to trust. You will also see a small example for parsing telemetry so you can cross-check values when dashboards disagree.

Key takeaways

  • Use SNR above 25 dB for stable 320 MHz throughput under load.
  • Treat RSSI alone as incomplete and validate with noise floor context.
  • Cross-check controller and client metrics using consistent averaging intervals.
  • Re-test after channel changes or puncturing because SNR can shift.
  • Map SNR bands to MCS targets to predict realistic link behavior.

Plan the work

Start by defining what SNR and RSSI mean for your outcome, not just the numbers. Signal to noise ratio reflects how much stronger the desired signal is than the noise floor and typically correlates with achievable modulation and coding scheme levels. RSSI is a received power indicator in dBm and varies by vendor scaling. For a 320 MHz channel, wider bandwidth raises the integrated noise, so an SNR that looked fine at 80 MHz might be marginal now. Establish goals like sustained throughput targets and retry bounds, then tie those to SNR bands. A practical rule is aim for SNR above 25 dB for consistent high MCS on wide lanes, but accept that client radio quality and spatial streams change the threshold.

Translate goals into measurement checkpoints so you do not chase the wrong variable. Choose fixed distances for near, mid, and far points, such as 3 meters, 10 meters, and 20 meters, to see how SNR and RSSI decay. At each point, collect idle measurements and loaded measurements while running a steady traffic generator. This reveals how interference and contention alter effective SNR. Set threshold bands like green at 30 dB and above, yellow between 20 and 29, and red below 20, understanding that walls and human bodies can lower SNR sporadically. The tradeoff is stricter thresholds reduce coverage but increase modulation stability and reduce retries.

Decide which tools will be authoritative and how you will reconcile them. Controllers commonly compute SNR as RSSI minus noise, while client drivers may average over different windows. Pick an interval, such as one second or five seconds, that matches your validation test duration. Document whether a dashboard displays per-chain or combined readings and whether it uses linear or logarithmic averaging. This avoids confusion when a client reports 27 dB while the AP shows 29 dB. If uncertainty remains about vendor math, compare the raw RSSI and noise floor values directly. Establishing this comparison up front prevents misinterpretation and reduces needless adjustments later.

Prepare environment

Control the environment so SNR and RSSI readings reflect reality instead of transients. Reserve a test window when neighboring 6 GHz networks are quiet or use a site where you manage all APs. Disable unnecessary services that generate chatter and consider temporarily reducing the number of active SSIDs. If your platform supports dynamic channel selection, hold the 320 MHz channel constant during tests. The benefit is repeatability, but the tradeoff is you might test on a suboptimal channel. Note if puncturing is active, since excluded resource units reduce effective bandwidth and alter noise integration, which changes the SNR you observe at the same RSSI.

Align device capabilities before measuring to avoid apples to oranges comparisons. Ensure the client supports WiFi 8 features and 320 MHz operation, and verify the AP’s transmit power is within regulatory limits for the environment. Calibrate the client’s driver reporting by checking its RSSI scale against a known attenuator step or a short coax baseline if available. This can expose consistent offsets like a client underreporting by roughly two dB. While such offsets are acceptable for trend analysis, they can mislead when setting absolute thresholds. Knowing the device’s behavior lets you interpret a 24 dB SNR as effectively closer to 26 on another client.

Choose telemetry sources and configure logging so you can replay measurements. Enable per-station RSSI, noise floor, retry counts, and MCS indices where available. If the controller supports exporting JSON or syslog, set a bounded retention window and consistent time synchronization through NTP. This supports correlating a dip in SNR with a rise in retries or a channel utilization spike. Capture both control plane and data plane views to distinguish interference from congestion. The limitation is storage and privacy, so filter to the test SSID and MAC addresses. Proper logging eliminates guesswork when dashboards briefly diverge during roaming or rate adaptation events.

Execute steps

Collect SNR and RSSI in a consistent workflow so comparisons hold. Begin with idle channel observations at near distance to baseline noise floor, then add midpoint and far point checks. Next, introduce a controlled load such as a sustained throughput test long enough for rate adaptation, typically at least thirty seconds. Record RSSI and noise concurrently so you can compute SNR if a tool only shows components. Capture MCS and retries to understand whether a modest SNR reduction actually harms throughput. The tradeoff is test time, but a structured sequence prevents chasing artifacts. Use averaging windows that match your validation plan to minimize disagreement among tools.

After capturing values, compute SNR bands and flag outliers automatically so you do not rely on eyeballing the chart. A small script can ingest controller or client JSON and emit a per-interval SNR along with simple statistics. Aim to surface minimum, median, and a short moving average to catch dips that matter for voice or VR traffic. Keep the calculation transparent as SNR equals RSSI minus noise in dB units. If a source provides linear power values, convert to dB before subtraction. The benefit is repeatable scoring, though be aware that differing sampling cadences between AP and client can still cause minor disagreement.

The expected outcome is consistent min, median, and average values that match what you see in dashboards within a small tolerance. If your export includes timestamps and MCS values, extend the script to join metrics by time and annotate intervals where SNR dips align with retries. Avoid adding smoothing that hides short outages because those moments often matter most for latency sensitive applications.

Validate results

Validate that SNR and RSSI readings support your throughput and latency goals before making design choices. Compare controller metrics with client-side utilities and look for consistent patterns rather than exact matches. If both show SNR above 30 dB during peak traffic and retries remain low, your threshold likely holds. When discrepancies appear, align sampling windows and confirm whether one tool reports per-chain values. Also verify that the client was actually using 320 MHz and not a punctured subset during the test. This check prevents overcrediting performance to wide channels when a narrower effective bandwidth carried the load.

Treat the table as guidance rather than a guarantee because radio quality and multipath vary by site. If you need tighter bounds, run site-specific baselines with a representative client. High channel utilization with acceptable SNR often indicates congestion or duty cycle saturation rather than noise. Troubleshoot and optimize

When SNR is unexpectedly low, isolate whether noise or distance is the culprit. Check the noise floor directly and scan for intermittent emitters that align with SNR dips. If the noise floor rises while RSSI stays steady, interference is likely. Try moving to an adjacent 6 GHz channel or using puncturing to avoid hot subchannels. If RSSI drops with stable noise, adjust placement or consider narrower bandwidth to reduce integrated noise and boost effective SNR. The tradeoff is lower peak throughput, but voice and collaboration often benefit from higher stability and fewer retries.

Examine controller and client settings that influence rate adaptation and retries. Aggressive minimum data rates can backfire when SNR swings at the cell edge, causing recurrent retransmissions. Tune QoS and WMM policies so latency sensitive traffic gets priority without starving bulk flows. If you manage multiple vendors, compare analytics features carefully to ensure that you can surface true 320 MHz visibility. Frequently asked questions

Why do my client and controller show different SNR values?

They may use different averaging windows or per-chain versus combined reporting. Align time intervals and confirm the client was using the same bandwidth. Compare raw RSSI and noise numbers and compute SNR yourself to reconcile.

Is RSSI enough to judge link quality on 320 MHz?

No. RSSI lacks noise context. Two links with the same RSSI can behave differently if the noise floor differs. Always consider SNR and utilization to understand throughput and latency stability.

What SNR should I target for high throughput?

As a starting point, aim for at least 25 dB on wide 320 MHz channels for stable high MCS. Adjust up or down based on client radio quality, spatial streams, and site materials.

Do punctured channels change SNR readings?

Yes. Excluding subchannels reduces effective bandwidth and lowers integrated noise, which can increase SNR at the same RSSI. Note puncturing state when comparing tests.

Should I narrow the channel to improve SNR?

Sometimes. Narrower bandwidth integrates less noise and can raise SNR, improving stability under variance. The tradeoff is lower peak throughput, which may be acceptable for latency sensitive apps.
