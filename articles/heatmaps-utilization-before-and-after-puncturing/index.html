Heatmaps: Utilization Before and After Puncturing

By Michael Carter • Published January 11, 2026

Heatmaps that visualize utilization offer a fast way to diagnose how puncturing affects channel capacity and fairness. By contrasting utilization before and after preamble puncturing, you can separate genuine throughput improvements from cosmetic noise. The core idea is simple yet easy to misread under pressure. Color intensity reflects airtime use, but interpretation shifts when a portion of the channel is intentionally excluded. Look for how busy time concentrates around unaffected resource units while the punctured part quiets. If overall utilization drops slightly while throughput per client climbs, that often signals healthier contention. If colors remain uniformly hot, interference may still dominate, and puncturing alone might not help. Set expectations early, then validate with consistent timing and matching client mixes to avoid confounding variables.

Key takeaways

  • Use matched time windows so heatmap deltas reflect puncturing effects.
  • Expect cooler bands where subchannels are excluded by the mask.
  • Pair utilization with throughput per client to confirm real gains.
  • Watch for fairness shifts that move contention to remaining resource units.
  • Reassess width choices if residual hotspots persist after puncturing.

Concepts and definitions

Utilization heatmaps try to answer which parts of the channel consume airtime, and when. The map aggregates busy time, often in one to five second bins, and paints higher occupancy as warmer colors. Before puncturing, a wide channel may appear uniformly hot even if only a narrow slice is actually constrained, because contention ripples across the entire width. After enabling puncturing, the excluded subchannels should show cooler tones and reduced busy time. A reliable before and after comparison demands matched intervals, consistent client load, and the same measurement granularity. Otherwise, differences reflect schedule changes rather than radio conditions. When tools expose per subchannel or per resource unit views, prefer those over whole channel averages, because averages can hide localized interference that drives poor user experience.

Preamble puncturing lets an access point advertise a primary channel and selectively mark parts of a wider channel unusable during a transmission. This behavior preserves some wide channel benefits while avoiding known noise segments. In heatmaps, punctured segments typically appear as consistent cooler bands, while active segments retain warmer hues. The tradeoff is potential concentration of traffic on fewer subchannels, which can raise contention and increase collisions if client density is high. That is why a neutral or slightly cooler overall heatmap can still correspond to better throughput per client. The mechanism works because the transmitter and compatible receivers coordinate on narrower effective bandwidth when interference is detected or policy requires masking. Legacy devices may not fully participate, which can blur the visual signature.

Utilization differs from throughput, and conflating them leads to poor decisions. Utilization measures airtime occupancy, not payload efficiency, so a hot map can indicate retries, protocol overhead, or slow clients dominating contention. After puncturing, successful transmissions often need fewer retries if the noisy subchannel is removed, which can reduce heat without sacrificing goodput. Conversely, if the remaining usable width is too narrow for the application mix, you may see cooler maps but lower aggregate throughput due to capacity limits. A practical rule is to pair utilization heatmaps with at least two corroborating metrics, such as median PHY rate and retry percentage. Heatmap changes without supporting improvements are suspect, since color shifts alone cannot prove a healthier network.

Frameworks and decision lenses

Airtime quality improves when removal of a noisy slice lowers retries without shifting congestion elsewhere. Use a three lens framework: spatial, temporal, and fairness. Spatial checks whether cooler bands align with known interference sources and whether remaining bands warm proportionally. Temporal examines whether improvements repeat across the day rather than appearing during brief lulls. Fairness assesses contention across clients and access points, watching for one node monopolizing a surviving subchannel. A balanced outcome shows modestly cooler overall utilization, higher median PHY rates, and retry reductions in the five to fifteen percent range. Outliers occur when client radios differ widely in capabilities. In those cases, segment by capability group to avoid masking problems behind smooth global averages.

Examples and short scenarios

Consider a venue where a narrow interference source sits inside a wide channel. Before puncturing, the heatmap shows a warm band across time with occasional deep red spikes. After enabling a modest mask, the noisy slice cools noticeably while neighboring segments warm evenly. Median retries drop, and the 50th percentile PHY rate rises. That is the signature of healthy exclusion. If the warmed neighbors show extreme hotspots, the fix is incomplete and you should reconsider width or power. Use it when multiple rooms or sectors share congested spectrum.

In a second scenario, the before heatmap looks uniformly hot during busy hours without a distinct band. After applying puncturing, colors cool slightly across the board but throughput gains are modest. This usually points to contention from many slow clients rather than one noisy slice. Puncturing reduces opportunities to schedule wider transmissions, so the net effect is neutral. The better lever is client steering or width reduction to fit capability distribution. If you doubt the diagnosis, compare utilization during off hours. If the pattern stays hot with few clients present, environmental noise or neighboring networks are likely culprits. Only then should you revisit masks, since further exclusions may sacrifice capacity without fixing the root cause.

A third case involves transient radar or industrial equipment that flares unpredictably. Before puncturing, heatmaps show episodic red streaks aligned with external events. After masking, those streaks disappear, yet adjacent segments gain only marginal warmth. That outcome suggests the interference was infrequent but disruptive when it appeared. The policy still helps by avoiding retries during bursts, which improves latency tails. Decision tradeoffs shift if clients are extremely bandwidth hungry. In those cases, consider time based policies or planned channel reuse rather than heavy static masks. Looking ahead

Validation matters more than a single attractive heatmap. Build a repeatable schedule that captures before and after windows across several peak periods with matching client mixes. Tag snapshots so analysts can pair them confidently. Then, tie utilization changes to service goals like latency percentiles and application goodput. If improvements fade during special events, revisit masks and power. Over time, expect environmental noise to drift as neighbors reconfigure networks. Plan quarterly reviews rather than one time tuning. If your environment includes legacy devices that ignore masks, segment them or constrain widths where they congregate. That reduces their influence on contention and keeps heatmaps interpretable for the modern fleet that respects puncturing.

Strategy thrives on context, so embed heatmap reading within a broader interference playbook. Start with the cleanest feasible channels, then apply measured exclusions only when evidence points to persistent trouble. Where partial interference dominates across many rooms, use channel reuse plans that minimize co channel overlap while retaining practical widths. When unfamiliar patterns emerge, consult resources that explain adjacent channel effects under puncturing and how the dynamics change. Pair visuals with telemetry that captures retries, rate shifting, and client distribution to avoid making calls on color alone. Patterns that do not fit known scenarios deserve small experiments and time boxed observation, not sweeping configuration changes.

Finally, keep documentation current. Record the exact mask configuration, date, and rationale next to the heatmaps. That history speeds future audits and prevents accidental rollbacks when staff changes. Create templates that include slots for spatial notes, temporal windows, and fairness observations, so reviewers think in lenses rather than eyeballing colors. If you support distributed sites, standardize time zones and snapshot durations to make cross site comparisons meaningful. When a new tool arrives, validate that its utilization definitions match previous baselines. Even subtle differences in busy time accounting can change the story a heatmap tells. Precision protects decisions, and it ensures puncturing remains a controlled tactic rather than a guess.

Frequently asked questions

How long should the before and after windows be?

Use matching windows of at least 30 to 60 minutes during similar load. Longer windows help smooth short term noise, but keep client mix consistent so comparisons remain fair.

What if heatmaps cool but throughput does not improve?

Check retries, median PHY rates, and client distribution. Cooling without throughput gains often means contention moved or width is mismatched for the client mix.

Do legacy clients affect the heatmap after puncturing?

Yes. Some legacy clients may not fully honor masks. Their behavior can blur cooler bands or keep utilization high. Segment or adjust widths where such devices dominate.

How do I spot over masking in a heatmap?

Look for intense hotspots on remaining segments and rising contention for a subset of clients. If fairness degrades and retries climb, the mask is likely too aggressive.

Are whole channel averages useful for decisions?

They are a starting point but often hide localized issues. Prefer per subchannel or per resource unit views when deciding whether puncturing helped.
