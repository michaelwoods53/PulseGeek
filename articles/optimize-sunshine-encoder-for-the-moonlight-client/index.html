Optimize Sunshine Encoder for the Moonlight Client

By Jacob Reed • Published January 27, 2026

You will optimize the Sunshine encoder for a Moonlight client on a Steam Deck by aligning codec, bitrate, and pacing to the limits of your hardware and network. Bring a host PC with a recent NVIDIA, AMD, or Intel GPU that supports hardware encoding, plus a stable LAN. We assume Sunshine on Windows or Linux and Moonlight on SteamOS with a wired dock or strong Wi Fi 6. The process moves from planning constraints to configuration, then validation and remediation, so every change has a measurable effect and a rollback point if quality regresses or latency rises unexpectedly.

Key takeaways

  • Pick a codec your GPU encodes well and the Deck decodes efficiently.
  • Start bitrate from the slowest link capacity, then nudge quality upward.
  • Prefer CBR for stability when diagnosing stutter and frame pacing issues.
  • Cap frame rate to reduce render and encode latency under load.
  • Validate with repeatable scenes and watch both loss and frame times.

Plan the work

Start by fixing an explicit goal such as stable 60 fps at 800p with minimal stutter, because target clarity directs codec and bitrate choices later. Identify constraints like host GPU encoder quality, Steam Deck decoder capability, and LAN throughput. For example, Moonlight on the Deck decodes H.264 and HEVC broadly, while AV1 depends on GPU and client build. Establish a budget: if your Wi Fi peaks around 80 Mbps, plan 25 to 35 Mbps for video to reserve bandwidth for audio and overhead. The tradeoff is headroom versus image quality, so leave space for scene complexity spikes. Clarifying goals and limits prevents oscillating between settings that fight each other.

Next define test content that stresses motion, detail, and darkness, because encoder weaknesses appear differently across scenes. A fast racing title exposes motion estimation limits while a dense RPG city reveals fine texture retention. Use repeatable segments like a 20 second camera pan in the same area so comparisons remain valid. The limitation is that single scenes can mislead, so rotate through two or three varied segments. Logging frame time graphs and packet loss alongside perceived quality helps attribute problems to encode, decode, or network. A consistent test set keeps later tweaks objective rather than anecdotal.

Finally map dependencies between controls, since bitrate, resolution, and frame rate interact. Doubling frame rate roughly doubles motion data which raises bitrate demand, so capping at 60 can reduce artifacts without noticeable input penalty on the Deck. Resolution also multiplies pixel cost, so 800p at 60 fps is usually friendlier than 1080p at the same rate on constrained links. When choosing between H.264 and HEVC, remember HEVC’s better compression can trade a little decode latency for cleaner edges at the same bitrate. Planning the order of changes avoids confusing results when several variables move at once.

Prepare environment

Validate hardware encode and decode support before tuning, because unsupported paths silently fall back to software and add delay. On the host, confirm NVENC, AMD VCE, or Intel Quick Sync availability using vendor tools or OS reports. On the Steam Deck, ensure Moonlight is up to date since decoder improvements can reduce latency. If your hardware supports AV1, consider it for higher quality per bit, but be aware that some combinations add encode delay. This prep step ensures your chosen codec actually runs on silicon rather than CPU threads, which would otherwise distort test results and limit achievable frame rate.

Stabilize the network to isolate encoder behavior from transport problems. Prefer wired Ethernet for the host and docked Deck when possible, or use Wi Fi 6 with strong signal and minimal interference. Set your router to a fixed channel and width that avoids congestion. If you must use wireless, prioritize the Steam Deck and host MAC addresses and disable power saving features that cause periodic stalls. The tradeoff is flexibility versus consistency, but a stable link improves troubleshooting clarity. By constraining variability in the path, you can attribute visible artifacts or stutter to encoder settings rather than packet loss.

Confirm Sunshine and Moonlight settings are not overridden by previous experiments. Reset any unusual toggles and document the baseline. In Sunshine, note current encoder, bitrate cap, keyframe interval, and rate control mode. In Moonlight, verify resolution, frame rate, and any decoder or video buffer adjustments. Keep a change log so you can revert if a tweak backfires. If you need broader foundations for LAN setup, review an end to end process like this guide on LAN remote play setup for low latency streaming. A known clean starting point avoids chasing ghosts from earlier misconfigurations.

Execute steps

Begin configuration with codec selection because it sets the quality and latency envelope for all further choices. If your GPU’s HEVC quality is strong and the Deck decodes it smoothly, prefer HEVC over H.264 at the same bitrate for fewer block edges. If both ends support AV1 and encode delay is acceptable, it can deliver crisper foliage and text at moderate bitrates. Keep a fallback path if a game spikes complexity or your network dips. For a decision framework that expands this choice with hardware specifics, see a focused comparison like the codec deep dive for Steam Deck streaming. The initial pick limits or unlocks gains in subsequent steps.

Next set rate control and bitrate to stabilize delivery. Use CBR at a conservative starting value that fits your slowest link, then raise in small increments while watching loss and queue growth. VBR can improve detail during calm scenes but may burst beyond buffer capacity during high motion. If you notice stutter at scene transitions, switch back to CBR or tighten VBR caps. Configure a keyframe interval around two seconds to aid recovery from errors without bloating overhead. This balances predictability against peak complexity, keeping decode buffers steady without starving images of bits when motion surges.

Finally align frame rate, resolution, and pacing. Cap at 60 fps to reduce render and encode pressure if the host GPU struggles at higher rates. Match output resolution to the Deck’s display like 1280 by 800 to avoid needless scaling on the client. Enable vsync or game frame pacing that complements your streaming rate target to avoid uneven delivery. If motion still looks uneven, consider capping the game engine to the stream rate to limit frame time variance. The tradeoff is slightly higher input latency versus smoother delivery, which improves perceived fluidity when bandwidth or encoder headroom fluctuates.

  3. Choose a bitrate: begin conservative, increase gradually while monitoring loss and queues.
  4. Adjust keyframe interval: target about two seconds for faster recovery.
  5. Cap frame rate: align game and stream at 60 fps to stabilize pacing.
  6. Match resolution: set 1280x800 to reduce scaling overhead on the client.

Validate results

Validate by replaying your predefined scenes and capturing both telemetry and impressions, because numbers and perception can diverge. In Moonlight, watch loss counters and frame pacing while scanning for blur, banding, or blocks in motion. A clean graph with visible artifacts suggests bitrate too low or a mismatched codec, while choppy delivery with acceptable detail points to network congestion or buffer pressure. Repeat at least twice to confirm consistency. This approach exposes whether changes improved resilience or only shifted artifacts. It also prevents overfitting to a single pass where Wi Fi interference briefly eased or spiked.

Cross check with the Steam Deck performance overlay to see GPU load, clocks, and frame time distribution under streaming. If the host GPU spikes during complex scenes, consider lowering frame rate or switching to a more efficient codec for your silicon. If the Deck shows decode pressure, reduce resolution or bitrate slightly. The limitation is that overlay sampling can lag, so read trends rather than single points. By pairing encoder metrics with device load, you trace whether bottlenecks originate at encode, decode, or transport, which guides the next iteration without guesswork.

Record before and after clips for side by side review on a larger display. Pause on foliage, signage, and shader-heavy effects where compression struggles. If fine edges smear despite stable graphs, raise bitrate modestly or tighten rate control peaks. If motion blur vanishes but stutter appears, your pacing constraints are too tight and need slack. Video evidence makes subjective impressions more comparable across versions.

Troubleshoot and optimize

When stutter appears despite low loss, suspect frame pacing mismatches. Cap the game at the stream rate and check vsync interactions that can introduce uneven frame delivery. If micro stutter persists, lower the stream frame rate or reduce maximum bitrate to keep buffers steady during motion spikes. As a separate lever, test CBR if VBR bursts cause queue growth. For a deeper look at how capping rates helps, review reasoning that explains how limiting frame rate reduces render and encode delay. The fix often trades peak smoothness for consistent motion which users typically prefer during remote play.

If macroblocking or banding dominates, assume insufficient bits for high complexity areas. Increase bitrate in small steps or switch to a more efficient codec your GPU handles well. If you already use HEVC and artifacts remain, try a slightly lower resolution at the same bitrate to increase bits per pixel on difficult scenes. Verify the host encoder preset is not overly fast which can starve motion estimation. If available, enable content adaptive options conservatively rather than aggressive modes that destabilize rate control. This combination tightens detail retention without amplifying variability.

For latency spikes that do not correlate with packet loss, examine encoder queue depth and buffering. Reduce prefetch or look ahead features that add delay and prefer smaller video buffers on the client when stable. If AV1 shows great quality but adds noticeable button to photon delay, revert to HEVC on twitchy titles and reserve AV1 for slower genres. Keep a fallback profile per game so switching is quick. When in doubt, retrace to your baseline and re apply one change at a time while logging. Controlled iteration almost always beats big leaps when symptoms overlap.

Quick start checklist

  1. Confirm hardware encoding: verify NVENC, VCE, or Quick Sync is active.
  2. Stabilize your link: use Ethernet or strong Wi Fi 6 with QoS.
  3. Pick a codec: start HEVC if available, otherwise use H.264.
  4. Set CBR bitrate: begin near 25 to 35 Mbps for 800p60.
  5. Cap to 60 fps: align game and stream targets to reduce pacing variance.
  6. Test repeatable scenes: log loss, frame times, and visible artifacts.

Frequently asked questions

Should I use CBR or VBR with Sunshine for Moonlight?

Use CBR when diagnosing or on unstable links to avoid bursts. Try VBR with tight caps after stability is proven to recover detail during calm scenes. If stutter returns at scene changes, revert to CBR or lower the VBR ceiling.

What bitrate is good for 800p at 60 fps on Steam Deck?

A conservative starting point is around the mid 20s in Mbps for HEVC and slightly higher for H.264. Increase gradually while watching packet loss and frame pacing, stopping when artifacts fade without triggering bursts or queue growth.

Is AV1 worth it for LAN streaming to the Steam Deck?

AV1 can deliver cleaner detail at lower bitrates, but some GPUs add encode delay. If latency feels sluggish in fast games, prefer HEVC or H.264 and reserve AV1 for slower genres or when extra quality at modest bitrate matters more.

How do I reduce micro stutter without lowering visual quality?

Align frame rate caps between game and stream, then stabilize with CBR. If pacing improves, nudge bitrate upward in small steps. Avoid aggressive look ahead or large buffers that add latency and can mask timing mismatches.

Why do I see blocks in dark scenes even with high bitrate?

Dark gradients compress poorly and reveal banding. Switch to a more efficient codec if supported, reduce resolution slightly to raise bits per pixel, or increase bitrate modestly. Longer keyframes or excessive VBR peaks can also destabilize delivery.

Looking ahead

Now that a stable baseline exists, create a few profiles aligned to genre and network conditions to streamline future sessions. Keep a fast action preset that favors HEVC or H.264 with conservative bitrate and tight pacing, plus a slow paced profile that tolerates AV1’s extra delay to gain texture fidelity. Revisit your test scenes quarterly because driver updates and client builds can shift the best settings. Lastly, document what symptom prompts which change so troubleshooting remains mechanical rather than experimental. Continued iteration guided by measurements preserves quality as your hardware and network evolve.
