320 MHz QoS Profiles for Voice and Video

By Rachel Morgan • Published January 30, 2026

Voice and video require predictable QoS even when 320 MHz channels amplify contention risks. This guide focuses on building practical profiles that map DSCP markings to WMM access categories and prioritize latency-sensitive traffic without starving other flows. You will plan markings, apply consistent queue behavior on access points and upstream devices, then validate with packet captures and simple quality metrics. We assume a managed WiFi 8 environment with controller access, 6 GHz radios enabled, and a test SSID that mirrors production settings. If your uplink trusts DSCP incorrectly, or your clients ignore WMM, you will learn containment steps that keep calls intelligible and video smooth while preserving fair airtime for the rest of the network.

Key takeaways

  • Prioritize voice with EF and video with AF41 while preserving fairness.
  • Map DSCP to WMM ACs consistently on APs and upstream switches.
  • Validate QoS using captures, jitter targets, and MOS thresholds.
  • Contain mismarked traffic with edge remarking and queue rate limits.
  • Use test SSIDs and rollback timers before production changes.

Plan the work

Start by choosing DSCP markings that match application behavior and latency needs. A common pattern sets voice to EF for tight jitter and video to AF41 to balance throughput and loss sensitivity. This choice aligns with WMM access categories, letting APs place frames into Voice or Video queues with shorter contention windows. The tradeoff is that overusing EF invites queue starvation during bursts. To avoid that, reserve EF only for real-time media and keep signaling or best effort flows on CS0 or AF classes. Document your mapping in a simple table so operations teams can audit packets at any hop and confirm intent is preserved end to end.

Next, define per-SSID policy boundaries so remarking is predictable where clients enter the network. Edge control is crucial because different phones or soft clients may set DSCP incorrectly or not at all. Enforce a rule that trusts DSCP from known subnets or device groups, and rewrites all others to defaults before classification. This protects voice queues from untrusted bulk data that might claim premium service. A practical safeguard is a maximum percentage cap for expedited forwarding per BSS during busy hours. If EF exceeds the cap, demote the remainder to video or best effort to sustain fairness across diverse clients without sacrificing intelligibility.

Finally, align airtime goals with 320 MHz behavior, where wide channels can attract greedy flows and increase collision domains. Set expectations that QoS can prioritize latency but cannot conjure capacity beyond what RF conditions allow. Use admission control for voice, limiting calls when RSSI drops below a threshold or retries exceed a safe range. For video, apply rate adaptation and resolution scaling policies so congestion results in graceful quality reduction rather than freeze frames. Capture baseline metrics like call MOS and frame loss before changes, then track the same after enabling profiles to confirm improvements without unintended side effects.

Prepare environment

Create a controlled test SSID that mirrors production security and band settings, including 6 GHz with 320 MHz channels if permitted indoors. Mirror client VLANs and apply the planned QoS policy only on this SSID initially. This isolation lets you compare behavior before deployment and limits risk if a mapping error suppresses throughput. Install a softphone and a video test tool on two laptops, then stage a few IoT or best effort devices to simulate typical airtime pressure. Aim for at least two access points on non-overlapping channels so roaming effects appear during validation. Document AP firmware versions so any anomalies can be reproduced or rolled back.

Prepare packet capture options at the AP or a wired mirror near the controller to observe DSCP and WMM tags. If your platform supports over-the-air capture at 6 GHz, enable it minimally to avoid performance impact. Otherwise, capture on the wired side and correlate with radio stats for retries and RSSI. A practical method is to filter EF and AF41 while noting average jitter and loss across short windows. Keep a small timeseries log with timestamps, SSID, and BSSID to attribute changes to specific radios. This reduces guesswork when a profile change affects one floor differently than another.

Define acceptance thresholds that translate user experience into measurable targets. For voice, aim for one-way jitter under 30 ms and packet loss below a low single digit percentage in sustained tests. For live video, define a fallback bitrate ladder so congestion triggers adaptive downgrades before stalls. These thresholds guide go or no-go decisions and help prioritize tuning areas. As a safety net, configure controller rollback timers for QoS changes so any bad profile automatically reverts after a set window. Keep a maintenance window and change record so operations can trace outcomes and so future audits understand why specific decisions were made.

Execute steps

Apply DSCP to WMM mappings on the controller so APs classify frames consistently, then ensure upstream switches and routers either trust or remark per your boundary rules. For bridged deployments, shaping at the AP uplink helps contain bursts before they traverse aggregation links. When clients do not set DSCP, use application signatures or policy groups to assign EF or AF41 based on known ports or media types, with careful exceptions for encrypted traffic where ports are ambiguous. The objective is predictability from client to gateway. Validate results

Validate with controlled traffic before involving real users. Place a sustained voice call between two testers and generate a video stream with adaptive bitrate. While running both, capture packets at the AP uplink and verify that EF and AF41 markings remain intact across hops. Check radio counters for retries, then correlate with jitter and loss on the media apps. Stable behavior looks like low jitter for voice and adaptive rate changes for video under load without stalls. If remarking is inconsistent, identify the hop that resets DSCP and fix trust or rewrite rules so the end-to-end path reflects your intended class of service.

Troubleshoot and optimize

When voice remains choppy despite EF, check for airtime starvation from aggressive video or large downloads. On 320 MHz, wide channels can invite long aggregates that delay latency-sensitive frames. Mitigate by capping queue depth for best effort and applying airtime fairness so small voice frames are not trapped behind lengthy transmissions. If airtime fairness is too aggressive, it may throttle high-throughput clients unnecessarily, so start with conservative thresholds. For a deeper understanding of when wide lanes help or hurt, see this overview of efficiency and puncturing tradeoffs that explains contention impacts during real deployments.

If DSCP markings keep disappearing, the issue often lies at L3 boundaries or firewall rules that reset bits. Audit each hop and adjust trust on switch ports that face APs. When a platform cannot trust, rewrite explicitly at ingress and document exceptions for tunnels or encrypted traffic that obscures application identities. Persistent jitter may point to interference or rate control instability rather than QoS. Compare retries and MCS distribution before and after enabling the profile. If retries spike, reduce maximum MCS for voice frames or enable admission control to limit calls on marginal RSSI. Where IoT must coexist, keep wake schedules predictable so bursts do not collide with voice. Frequently asked questions

What DSCP should I use for voice and video?

Use EF for real-time voice and AF41 for live video. Keep signaling and best effort traffic on CS0 or appropriate AF classes to avoid starving other queues. Reserve EF for latency sensitive media only.

How do I handle clients that ignore WMM?

Rewrite DSCP at SSID ingress and enforce priority at the AP uplink. Admission control for voice and conservative airtime fairness help protect latency when clients do not advertise proper access categories.

Why does EF traffic still experience jitter on 320 MHz?

Wide channels increase the chance of long aggregates blocking small frames. Cap best effort queue depth, enable airtime fairness, and reduce retries by lowering MCS where links are marginal.

Should I trust DSCP from all devices?

No. Trust known device groups or subnets and rewrite unknown markings. This prevents bulk data from claiming premium service and protects queues reserved for latency sensitive flows.

Do I need to change markings for encrypted media?

If application ports are ambiguous due to encryption, use device identity or policy groups to assign classes. Avoid broad trust that could misclassify unrelated flows as premium traffic.
