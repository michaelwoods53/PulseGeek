MLO Policy: Prefer Low-Latency Link for Gaming

By Danielle Mitchell • Published January 10, 2026

Gaming benefits when a multi link setup can prefer the lowest latency path while maintaining reliable throughput. This list prioritizes MLO policy choices that bias a fast link, then locks game traffic to it with guardrails against flapping. Each item was chosen for measurable game feel improvements like steadier RTT, fewer jitter spikes, and faster input response. We balance link selection, traffic steering, and interference avoidance without relying on vendor specific tricks. Expect practical steps you can stage in minutes, plus deeper tuning that pays off in longer sessions where consistency matters. Our selection favors mechanisms that survive neighbor congestion and transient noise rather than peak throughput numbers that rarely show up in a tight match.

Key takeaways

  • Favor the fastest MLO link, then pin game flows to it.
  • Add hysteresis so brief jitter does not trigger a switch.
  • Avoid DFS as primary to prevent mid match quiet periods.
  • Separate throughput and control paths to stabilize latency under load.
  • Measure RTT and jitter, not only signal strength, before locking choices.

1) Prefer a low latency primary link

Set the MLO policy to prioritize the link with the shortest round trip time instead of the highest signal or raw rate. The claim is simple: ping wins games more than peak Mbps. For example, if your 5 GHz path shows 12 to 16 ms RTT with low jitter and 6 GHz hovers at 18 to 22 ms due to distance, prefer 5 GHz as primary even if 6 GHz advertises higher modulation. This keeps aim micro-corrections crisp when bursty cross traffic appears. The tradeoff is marginally lower ceiling throughput during downloads, but gaming rarely needs that headroom. The why is that airtime and contention shape latency more than RSSI alone. Prioritizing measured RTT hedges against deceptive link rates that wobble under interference.

Hold the preference with a minimal threshold so small noise does not trigger churn. As a rule of thumb, require the alternate link to beat the current primary by at least 4 to 6 ms in median RTT over a rolling 5 to 10 sample window before switching. A short window reacts to meaningful changes without amplifying variance from a single bad sample. The tradeoff is slower adaptation if the environment shifts suddenly, yet you avoid maddening mid fight toggles that spike jitter. 2) Pin game traffic to the primary link

Once the fastest link is chosen, bind latency sensitive flows to that path so they do not wander during spikes. The claim is that flow pinning keeps aim and hit registration steady even when background devices start streaming. Practically, mark game traffic with DSCP CS6 or AF41 on the device or router and map those marks to the primary MLO link using your router’s QoS or policy routing feature. For example, tag your game process on PC, verify marks with packet capture, then prioritize that class on the preferred link. The tradeoff is administrative overhead and occasional misclassification, which you mitigate by scoping rules to console IPs or known ports. Why this works comes down to queue isolation and deterministic scheduling that keeps critical packets ahead of bulk transfers.

Preserve stability by isolating throughput tasks to the secondary link so big downloads do not disturb the preferred path. For instance, assign BE or CS0 traffic to the alternate link during game sessions and reserve the primary for marked classes only. This separation prevents buffer growth and keeps airtime available where it matters. It can reduce peak aggregate throughput, but your in match experience improves. 3) Avoid DFS as your primary path

Do not use a DFS channel as your primary gaming link when consistent latency matters more than peak spectrum access. The claim is that radar detection events and channel availability checks can interrupt service at the worst moments. For example, a DFS hit triggers a move or quiet period that drops packets, producing visible stutter in aim tracking. Instead, prefer a clean non DFS 5 GHz or 6 GHz channel that meets your RTT targets. The tradeoff is fewer channel choices in congested areas, but you gain stability that survives sudden environmental changes. Use puncturing or narrower widths to dodge local interference while keeping latency flat. The claim is that a narrower or punctured channel often yields steadier ping than a wide but noisy lane. For instance, if a 160 MHz span shows intermittent noise in one segment, puncture that portion or drop to a stable 80 MHz while maintaining low airtime occupancy. The tradeoff is reduced theoretical throughput, which typically does not affect shooters or fighters that send small, frequent packets. The why is straightforward: fewer retries and cleaner contention lower jitter. Looking ahead

Make your settings durable by validating with repeatable measurements and recorded sessions. The claim is that a stable MLO policy shows up as tighter RTT distributions, steadier frame pacing, and fewer micro stutters across multiple evenings. For example, log ping to your gateway and a nearby game server every 5 seconds while streaming or downloading on another device. Keep your primary link pinned to the lower median RTT and observe whether jitter stays within the range you set as a switch threshold. The tradeoff is the time spent collecting traces, but even one or two focused tests can reveal whether a tweak holds under pressure. The why is that controlled tests surface edge cases like hidden interference or short lived congestion that classroom settings often miss.

As environments change, revisit thresholds, channels, and flow mapping quarterly. The claim is that neighbor deployments, firmware updates, and device churn alter interference patterns and retransmit behavior, which shifts the best link for low latency. For instance, a new microwave link next door might nudge your 6 GHz path noisier while opening a cleaner 5 GHz channel. The tradeoff is occasional rework, yet the benefit is a policy that continues to feel responsive months later. Key terms

  • Differentiated Services Code Point (DSCP) — A packet header field used to mark priority.
  • Dynamic Frequency Selection — A system that detects radar and moves Wi‑Fi channels to avoid it.
  • Multi‑Link Operation (MLO) — Using multiple bands or channels at once between a client and AP.
  • Quality of Service — Network rules that prioritize or shape traffic to manage latency.

Frequently asked questions

Does preferring a low latency link reduce overall throughput?

Usually yes by a small amount, because you may skip wider or noisier channels that advertise higher rates. For gaming, the trade favors latency. You can still send bulk traffic over the secondary link to recover throughput.

How often should MLO switch the primary link?

Switch only when the alternate link shows a clear and sustained improvement in median RTT, such as several milliseconds over multiple samples. Add hysteresis to avoid flapping on brief jitter spikes.

Is 6 GHz always better for gaming latency?

No. 6 GHz can be excellent at short range with clean spectrum, but attenuation is higher through walls. If 5 GHz consistently delivers lower RTT and fewer retries in your space, prefer it as primary.

Should I avoid DFS channels entirely?

Do not use DFS as your primary gaming path. DFS is fine for bulk transfers or a secondary link, but radar events and channel checks risk interruptions that disrupt live play.

Do I need QoS markings to pin game traffic?

They are not mandatory, but DSCP or similar markings make steering precise. Marking helps routers keep game packets on the preferred link and prevents bulk traffic from starving critical flows.
