320 MHz Transmit Power and TPC Settings

By Rachel Morgan • Published January 5, 2026

Our goal is to set 320 MHz transmit power and choose TPC settings that respect EIRP limits while sustaining throughput. We assume a managed 6 GHz deployment with enterprise APs, controller or cloud management, and basic survey data. The guide emphasizes practical math for EIRP, how antenna gain changes targets, and how dynamic TPC interacts with wide channels. If you are planning dense floors, we will show how to keep the AP signal usable without inflating contention domains. By the end, you will understand safe ranges, when to cap the radio, and how to verify results with spectrum and client metrics.

Key takeaways

  • Compute EIRP from conducted power, antenna gain, and cable loss.
  • Use lower power in dense areas to reduce 320 MHz contention.
  • Pick TPC modes that converge slowly and avoid oscillation.
  • Validate with client RSSI, MCS, and channel utilization trends.
  • Respect regional 6 GHz limits and AFC constraints outdoors.

Plan the work

Start by defining the performance objective and regulatory boundary for 6 GHz, because 320 MHz channels change interference geometry. A reasonable target is maintaining client RSSI around the mid to high minus 60s at cell edge for typical enterprise devices, knowing ultra wide lanes increase probability of overlap. Sketch desired cell sizes for office or venue areas and note walls or absorptive materials that help contain energy. The tradeoff is clear: more conducted power increases coverage but enlarges the contention domain, which in a wide channel magnifies co-channel exposure. Establish an EIRP cap per area that meets regional rules, and record antenna gain for each AP model, since that directly shifts allowed conducted power. This plan anchors later TPC choices and prevents trial-and-error from drifting into noncompliant settings.

Translate objectives into measurable thresholds before touching TPC settings, because thresholds make tuning repeatable. Pick a baseline noise floor expectation for 6 GHz indoors, then allocate headroom for bursts from neighboring BSS or incumbents. For example, if your survey shows a typical noise floor near minus 95 dBm, design toward received midstream data around 25 dB SNR at edge for reliability. That implies a rough edge RSSI target and points to an EIRP that is neither aggressive nor fragile. The limitation is that client radios vary widely in sensitivity and antenna efficiency, so one edge RSSI does not fit all tiers. Document which client classes drive the requirement, such as scanners or laptops, and accept that handhelds might see lower throughput at the same boundary.

Map building zones to power tiers, since TPC operates best within sane limits. Assign lower max EIRP in dense open offices and higher caps in isolated conference areas, ensuring APs cannot escalate beyond your containment plan. Include policy notes for MLO, because multi-link operation may couple behavior on 6 GHz with 5 GHz, and that can mask issues if the 6 GHz link is overpowered. Create a change window and a rollback baseline with current power, EIRP, and channel settings for every AP group. The tradeoff with rigid tiers is slower adaptation to real-time churn, but the benefit is protection against runaway increases that bloat contention. Document all assumptions so later validation can challenge them rather than guess intent.

Prepare environment

Gather inputs that drive conducted power math before changing TPC modes. You need antenna gain per AP, any cable or pigtail losses, and the regional EIRP limits for 6 GHz low power indoor or standard power with AFC. With those, compute EIRP as conducted power plus antenna gain minus cable loss, and set a target range for each zone. Expect differences across hardware families, where a 6 dBi integrated panel requires lower conducted watts than a 3 dBi omni to meet the same cap. The tradeoff with integrated gain is tighter vertical beam which may create coverage holes on upper floors, so verify a floor stack. If outdoor standard power is in scope, confirm AFC availability and database registration timing to avoid unintended outages.

Verify client mix and capabilities because TPC choices interact with client transmit power and MCS headroom. Many handheld devices transmit lower power than APs, creating asymmetry that harms uplink reliability when AP EIRP is pushed. A rule of thumb is to align AP EIRP so downlink RSSI at the edge roughly mirrors what clients can return on uplink, preserving bidirectional throughput. Test with at least one constrained client type to ensure the link budget closes both ways. A limitation appears when roaming policies force stickiness at high RSSI, which can mask an uplink deficit. Consider enabling features that prioritize uplink MCS feedback in your monitoring so these imbalances are visible during pilots.

Choose initial TPC behavior with a clear algorithmic expectation, since some controllers converge rapidly while others adjust slowly to avoid oscillation. If your platform offers fixed, static cap, or adaptive modes, start with a static cap plus narrow adaptive range to control variance during the pilot. The rationale is simple: on a 320 MHz channel, power changes ripple farther, so cautious step sizes reduce network-wide oscillations. Set a minimum floor to prevent power collapse when background energy spikes briefly. Note that rapid TPC can chase transient utilization, causing ping-pong power changes that destabilize roaming decisions. Capture the interval, step size, and floor-ceiling values in change notes so you can correlate them with metrics later.

Execute steps

Begin configuration by translating EIRP caps into conducted power limits that your AP can enforce, then apply TPC settings with constrained dynamics. For example, if your indoor cap per region is 30 dBm EIRP and the antenna adds 5 dBi with 1 dB loss, the conducted budget should not exceed 26 dBm. Lock that as a ceiling, then allow TPC to move within a small band, such as minus 6 to 0 dB relative to ceiling, to accommodate local density. The reason for a narrow band is to prevent the 320 MHz domain from swelling on busy days. An edge case occurs in long corridors where reflections inflate perceived coverage; limit max power further there and rely on placement.

Apply a stable TPC algorithm and verify the step and interval choices match your environment’s rate of change. In busy office floors, five to fifteen minute intervals with 1 to 2 dB steps often reduce oscillation risk without freezing the system. Wider 320 MHz channels experience more concurrent energy, so the controller should average utilization and noise across enough windows before acting. If your platform supports preamble puncturing, remember that power choices still affect the unpunctured segments, so do not overcompensate. When mixed-width neighbors exist, cap 320 MHz radios modestly lower to minimize dominance. As you stage changes, communicate windows to stakeholders and record per-AP adjustments to facilitate rollback if needed.

Validate results

Validate with layered checks that tie power settings to client experience, not just AP counters. Start with cell-edge measurements of RSSI and SNR on representative clients and verify that downlink and uplink both close the link budget. Then examine MCS distributions during normal load; wide channels should show healthy mid to high MCS for stationary clients if SNR is adequate. The caution is that 320 MHz inflates noise and contention, so lower-than-expected MCS can be normal during bursts. Compare against a control area to understand the baseline. If client retries climb while AP retries stay low, suspect uplink asymmetry from an AP EIRP that is too high, and lower the ceiling by a few dB.

Inspect channel utilization and duty cycle across the 6 GHz band to ensure the wide lane is not monopolizing airtime beyond its coverage intent. Look for sustained utilization plateaus that track TPC increases, which suggests the power change expanded the contention domain. Pair utilization with BSS color or OBSS metrics if available to detect co-channel exposure. A good rule is that average busy time should remain comfortably below half of the busiest neighbor segment under typical load for predictable latency. The limitation is that short-lived spikes can be harmless and should not cause reactive power cuts. Trend across hours and days rather than reacting to a single snapshot.

Verify roaming quality and MLO behavior with the tuned power profile, because misaligned power can trap devices or force premature switches. Measure time-to-roam and packet loss during transitions at walking pace, and observe if devices cling to a strong 6 GHz AP despite a weak uplink. If your platform shows per-link MLO stats, ensure 6 GHz is not overshadowing 5 GHz due to overpowered settings, which could degrade aggregate behavior. When roaming shows sticky tendencies, reduce the ceiling or adjust minimums to encourage handoff. As a sanity check, compare before and after client satisfaction metrics such as throughput percentiles, not just averages, to capture tail performance.

Troubleshoot and optimize

If throughput dips after increasing power, suspect contention expansion rather than raw signal issues. On 320 MHz lanes, a few dB can link faraway cells and pull their talkers into your airtime, lowering spectral efficiency. Roll back power in 1 to 2 dB steps and watch utilization, retries, and MCS. If metrics improve while edge RSSI remains acceptable, you confirmed an oversized domain. Another angle is preamble puncturing to dodge localized energy, but treat it as a surgical tool, not an excuse for excessive power. In particularly reflective spaces, address geometry with placement or attenuation rather than further power hikes, as reflections can mislead TPC heuristics.

When TPC oscillates, reduce step size or lengthen the interval so decisions integrate across real usage patterns. Add a minimum floor to prevent collapse from transient spikes, and cap the ceiling to stop spirals during peak noise. Check whether neighboring 160 MHz or 80 MHz cells are set significantly lower; mismatched widths and power can bias client steering unfavorably. A practical step is to align 320 MHz ceilings a few dB below neighboring narrower cells in dense regions to avoid dominance. Also review any controller feature that adapts power based on channel utilization alone; consider multi-metric triggers that include retries and SNR to stabilize actions.

Frequently asked questions

Should AP power match client power exactly?

No. Aim for similar edge RSSI both ways, not identical transmit power. Clients vary widely, so match the link budget rather than trying to mirror conducted values.

Why does 320 MHz sometimes show lower MCS than 160 MHz?

Ultra wide channels see more concurrent energy and interference, which can reduce effective SNR. That lowers usable MCS despite adequate RSSI during busy periods.

How fast should TPC react to utilization spikes?

Slowly. Use modest steps and multi-minute intervals so decisions reflect sustained patterns. Fast reactions can cause oscillation and unstable roaming.

Can preamble puncturing replace careful power tuning?

No. Puncturing avoids narrow interference slices, but transmit power still controls contention area. Use both judiciously for best results.

Do I need different power for MLO-enabled clients?

Not different power, but balanced links. Ensure 6 GHz does not dominate 5 GHz, or MLO may favor one leg and hurt aggregate performance.
