Capacity Planning Assumptions to Include in SLAs

By Michael Carter • Published February 2, 2026

Strong SLAs depend on explicit capacity planning assumptions, not only target numbers. This list prioritizes assumptions that keep WiFi 8 throughput, latency, and reliability predictable as load changes. Items were chosen for controllability, measurability, and direct impact on user experience. Each entry explains the claim, offers a concrete example, and calls out tradeoffs you should consider when writing the agreement.

Key takeaways

  • Declare concurrency and device mix to anchor realistic throughput promises.
  • Tie application classes to QoS mappings and enforcement points end-to-end.
  • Set latency, jitter, and loss budgets under defined loaded conditions.
  • Reserve headroom with utilization thresholds to prevent brownouts.
  • Document measurement locations and tools so SLA tests match reality.
  • Align upgrade cadence to projected growth to protect SLA integrity.

1. Concurrency and device mix

State concurrent users and device mix because SLA viability depends on offered load. For example, an office that assumes 120 active clients per floor with 70 percent laptops, 20 percent phones, and 10 percent IoT can model airtime demand and duty cycles realistically. Declare typical PHY rates by device tier and expected MCS distribution to make throughput targets testable. The tradeoff is rigidity. If concurrency or mix drifts, the same targets may require more spectrum or AP density. Mitigate by adding a variance band that triggers review when active clients exceed a defined threshold or when low-rate devices surpass a small percentage. Linking assumptions to a threshold-based review keeps capacity planning aligned with SLAs without promising impossible performance during atypical surges or device onboarding spikes.

Section highlights

  • Declare active clients, device tiers, and expected rate distribution.
  • Set variance thresholds that trigger capacity and SLA review.

2. Per-user throughput targets

Per-user throughput targets must include context like test duration, content type, and concurrent load. A practical example is 8 Mbps sustained downlink for 60 seconds with 100 simultaneous users per AP group, validated using standard TCP tools. Tie targets to traffic classes where needed, such as higher floors for video collaboration hours. The tradeoff is that higher guarantees raise AP count or require additional spectrum. To set realistic targets, start from modeled airtime and verify under synthetic load. For deeper guidance on modeling and upgrade levers, reference this complete capacity planning guide. Document both sustained and short-burst expectations so users understand variability, and specify retry handling that could reduce application goodput versus PHY rate.

Section highlights

  • Add load, duration, and traffic class to throughput statements.
  • Model airtime, then validate sustained and burst behavior.

3. Application classes and QoS mapping

SLAs should bind application classes to specific QoS behavior so intent survives across domains. For example, map real-time voice to WMM AC-VO and ensure DSCP EF from the LAN remains high priority through the WLAN controller. Spell out remarking policies and where enforcement happens. The tradeoff is administrative complexity if many categories exist or if vendors handle classification differently. Reduce ambiguity by defining a narrow set of classes and a translation table from DSCP to WMM. When tuning collaboration traffic, consider this resource on how to translate voice and video requirements into QoS profiles. Include a verification step that samples packets at the AP and at the core to confirm markings persist and that queue depths align with latency objectives.

Section highlights

  • Define application classes with DSCP to WMM translation rules.
  • Verify marking persistence and queue behavior at multiple points.

4. Latency and jitter under load

Latency and jitter thresholds must be stated under a defined loaded condition, not idle. For instance, specify median and 95th percentile one-way latency across the WiFi hop during 70 percent airtime utilization and report jitter as inter-packet delay variation. This keeps thresholds aligned to user experience during busy hours. The tradeoff is that measurements can differ by tool and placement. Standardize on packet size, interval, and traffic direction. For framing targets by application class, review how to set latency and jitter thresholds that match voice and streaming tolerance. Include a method for synthetic load generation so teams can reproduce results, and declare acceptable variance when the RF environment changes or when DFS events temporarily alter channel availability.

Section highlights

  • Express latency and jitter at specified airtime utilization.
  • Standardize packet size, direction, and synthetic load method.

5. Airtime fairness and admission control

Document airtime fairness and admission control because they protect SLAs from overload. For example, enable load-based rejection for new video sessions when airtime exceeds a defined threshold and enforce per-client airtime caps for best effort traffic. These controls keep high-priority queues stable during peaks. The tradeoff is user perception when requests are deferred or downgraded. Communicate that deferral preserves critical services. If you need a refresher on mechanisms that stabilize load, see how admission control and airtime fairness keep SLAs stable. Calibrate thresholds by testing with synthetic voice and screen-share traffic so you do not starve important but non-real-time flows. Record any exceptions like maintenance windows where enforcement is relaxed.

Section highlights

  • Use admission control and per-client caps to guard priority queues.
  • Test thresholds with realistic mixed traffic before enabling network-wide.

6. Retry and packet loss budgets

Loss budgets need explicit retry assumptions because application goodput depends on both. For example, set a maximum MAC retry rate and a packet loss threshold for each class, such as lower loss for voice and higher tolerance for best effort. Include how aggregation and block acknowledgments impact measured loss so testers know what to expect. The tradeoff is stricter loss targets can reduce throughput in noisy environments by forcing lower MCS selections. Define remediation triggers, such as investigating interference when retries exceed a percentage over a rolling window. For voice and video expectations, align with guidance that relates loss to user experience instead of arbitrary values. Write the budgets next to measurement method so loss on the WiFi hop is not conflated with end-to-end path issues.

Section highlights

  • Pair packet loss thresholds with explicit MAC retry limits.
  • Trigger RF investigation when retries exceed defined rolling windows.

7. Roaming and handoff assumptions

State roaming performance in terms of handoff time, packet loss during transition, and supported methods like 802.11k, 11v, and 11r. For example, target sub-100 ms handoffs for voice-capable devices within designated coverage zones, measured with continuous RTP during AP transitions. The tradeoff is that fast transition may not be supported by all clients and can cause compatibility issues. Provide a fall-back behavior and explicitly list device classes that must use fast transition. Define test paths and walking speeds so mobility results are reproducible. Clarify that roaming targets apply only within planned coverage areas, not fringe signal conditions. Including these assumptions in SLAs prevents disputes when client firmware changes alter roaming decisions that the infrastructure cannot fully control.

Section highlights

  • Quantify handoff time, loss during transition, and supported methods.
  • Document fall-backs for clients lacking fast transition support.

8. RF environment and channel plan

Bind SLAs to the approved channel plan and expected noise floor because spectrum sets the ceiling on performance. For example, guarantee targets only when configured 5 GHz and 6 GHz channels remain available per design, with DFS events limited to an expected frequency. The tradeoff is external interference can violate assumptions. Mitigate by stating a process for spectrum scans and acceptable interference windows. Include transmit power ranges, spatial reuse settings, and nominal CCI levels so planners can reproduce results during audits. When capacity calculations depend on clean spectrum, note that temporary reductions during radar events or adjacent construction may lower throughput. Adding these RF assumptions prevents misattribution of performance issues to the SLA when the environment changed materially.

Section highlights

  • Tie guarantees to approved channels, power ranges, and reuse settings.
  • Define scan cadence and acceptable interference windows for audits.

9. Headroom and utilization thresholds

Reserve capacity headroom by declaring utilization thresholds that prompt action before service degrades. For example, set 70 percent sustained airtime as a soft limit that triggers AP tuning or additional capacity planning. This ensures latency-sensitive queues remain protected under growth. The tradeoff is that conservative thresholds can increase cost if upgrades happen early. Justify values by tying them to measured delay curves for your environment. For a structured approach, consider guidance on setting headroom and utilization thresholds to prevent brownouts. Record the remediation path, from channel adjustments to adding radios, so stakeholders understand timing and budget impacts when thresholds are crossed repeatedly.

Section highlights

  • Declare soft limits that trigger tuning before queues degrade.
  • Link thresholds to measured delay curves to justify costs.

10. Measurement locations and methodology

Define measurement points and tooling so results are reproducible. For instance, measure one-way latency between a controlled client in the coverage zone and a wired reflector near the gateway, using fixed packet sizes and intervals. Specify tolerated variance and the number of test iterations. The tradeoff is that over-constraining the method can miss real problems seen by diverse clients. Balance by including both synthetic tests and periodic sampling from typical endpoints. If teams need a template, align with an approach that emphasizes actionable metrics and measurement methods tied to capacity planning. Document time-of-day windows, roaming paths, and how failures are escalated so no dispute arises over whether a test reflected agreed operating conditions.

Section highlights

  • Fix measurement points, tools, sizes, and intervals for repeatability.
  • Combine synthetic load with real client samples to catch edge cases.

11. Upgrade cadence and capacity growth

Link SLA validity to a capacity growth plan so promises remain credible as demand rises. For example, define a quarterly review that compares observed concurrency and airtime to modeled expectations, with triggers for adding radios, enabling new bands, or optimizing channel width. The tradeoff is budget timing. Upgrades may lag usage spikes. Reduce risk by staging incremental changes and prewiring for additional APs where growth is predictable. Reference the modeling guidance mentioned earlier to keep changes grounded in measurable drivers rather than equipment refresh cycles. Declare how major RF changes reset baseline measurements, and note the communication process for stakeholders so user expectations adjust when growth necessitates phased improvements.

Section highlights

  • Schedule reviews that compare observed load to modeled demand.
  • Stage incremental upgrades and reset baselines after RF changes.

Looking ahead

Turn these assumptions into a living appendix to your SLA, then schedule periodic validation against observed telemetry. As deployments evolve, update concurrency, QoS mappings, and headroom values before symptoms emerge. Spread links to the modeling guide, latency framing, and measurement templates through your operations runbook so new team members can reproduce decisions and tests. With assumptions stated and reviewed, your WiFi 8 environment can scale predictably while keeping user experience at the center.

Section highlights

  • Convert assumptions into a maintained SLA appendix with reviews.
  • Feed measurements back into capacity models to stay ahead.
