Packet Capture on 6 GHz 320 MHz Channels

By Rachel Morgan • Published February 21, 2026

Capturing packets on 6 GHz with ultra wide 320 MHz channels requires deliberate planning, correct channelization, and predictable timing. This guide walks through a repeatable workflow that balances adapter capabilities with regulatory constraints and client behavior. You will set monitor mode properly, map primary and secondary segments, and validate that captures show expected management and data frames. The process uses common tools and emphasizes why signal to noise margin, bandwidth configuration, and capture windows matter more than raw throughput targets. We assume access to at least one 6 GHz capable adapter and administrative rights on your capture machine. The environment can be macOS, Linux, or Windows with suitable drivers, though monitor mode support varies widely across platforms and chipsets in practice.

Key takeaways

  • Use monitor mode with correct primary channel and 320 MHz bandwidth.
  • Time captures to client association and trigger predictable traffic bursts.
  • Validate center frequency and bandwidth using tool metadata fields.
  • Record a short control trace before any long capture sessions.
  • Prefer targeted windows over continuous runs to reduce noise.

Plan the work

Start with a crisp objective that defines which exchanges must be visible and why they matter for the hypothesis. For 6 GHz 320 MHz, decide if you need association frames, EHT management elements, or specific data flows like a unidirectional upload test. Scope influences capture bandwidth and placement, because wide channels magnify noise if the target is narrow. A practical rule is to map the smallest window that proves or falsifies the issue within five minutes. For example, troubleshoot slow join by capturing from probe request to first data frame. The tradeoff is missing long tail behavior, so pair a short targeted trace with a separate background telemetry view to preserve context without bloating files.

Define the RF footprint and the capture vantage point to avoid partial views that complicate interpretation. A 320 MHz channel spans multiple 20 MHz subchannels and your adapter must sit where both primary and secondary segments are reliably above noise floor. If your test bench sits near a reflective surface, plan for multi path that can degrade modulation and obscure weak management frames. Choose a stable location and note obstructions like metal racks. When uncertain, perform a quick SNR check with a spectrum or survey tool and target at least a comfortable margin above client receive sensitivity. The cost of moving later is higher than a two minute survey upfront.

Sequence the timeline so the capture starts before key events and ends after expected responses without running needlessly long. A disciplined window for association might be sixty to ninety seconds, then a second window for data stress of thirty seconds under consistent traffic. Use scripted traffic generators or predictable actions like downloading a fixed file to ensure repeatability. Consider queuing DFS awareness checks if your channels intersect radar protected ranges, because sudden moves will invalidate the planned window. As a hedge, schedule a tiny control trace earlier in the day to confirm there is no regulatory hold that mutes 6 GHz transmit on your device. Planning timing avoids wasted runs.

Prepare environment

Choose a capture adapter and driver that truly support 6 GHz monitor mode with 320 MHz bandwidth, not just scanning. Some chipsets expose 6 GHz only for station mode or limit bandwidth to 160 MHz. Validate capabilities by checking driver release notes and by issuing a dry run that confirms monitor mode on the desired center frequency. On Linux, tools like iw and airmon-ng can reveal support, but you still need a packet tool to verify resulting radiotap fields. The tradeoff is that high end adapters may require proprietary utilities, increasing setup time. Favor hardware you can reconfigure quickly, because retries are cheaper when tool friction is low.

Map the channel plan to the correct center frequency and primary location. A 320 MHz allocation uses one primary 20 MHz channel for control and multiple secondary segments. Your sniffer must tune to the 6 GHz center and mark the correct primary so management and control frames parse correctly. Packet headers often encode center frequency and bandwidth in radiotap or vendor fields that Wireshark can display once saved. If you capture on the wrong primary, you can see data frames with missing context. When in doubt, inspect beacon fields from the AP to extract the operating class and primary location, then align your capture configuration to match that reference.

Stabilize the capture host to prevent throttling and timestamp drift that complicate analysis. Disable aggressive power management, pin CPU performance to a steady governor where possible, and ensure storage can sustain write bursts without buffering. For predictable timing, sync NTP or PTP before runs and avoid system updates during tests. A quick health check is to run a short monitor capture while moving no traffic and confirm steady timestamps with minimal gaps. The risk of ignoring host stability is subtle misalignment when comparing captures to controller analytics. Tight clocks and stable writes make correlating logs and packets straightforward during later validation.

Execute steps

Run targeted captures that bracket key events and include a traffic burst that exercises the 320 MHz path. The goal is to record management exchanges plus a clean data segment without unnecessary idle airtime. Start a short pre check trace to validate radiotap fields show 6 GHz center and 320 MHz width, then proceed to the main window. Trigger deterministic traffic like a fixed file transfer or a reproducible iPerf run so MCS distribution and retries can be compared later. Keep files manageable by limiting the burst to thirty seconds. The tradeoff is less variety in flows, but consistency yields stronger before and after comparisons across adjustments.

On Linux, a minimal tcpdump invocation can enable monitor mode and capture to PCAPNG while you control timing externally. Other platforms might require GUI tools or different flags, yet the principle is the same. Expect monitor mode to disrupt any concurrent client role on that adapter, so dedicate hardware for sniffing. If your driver does not set channel width automatically, use platform tools to specify 320 MHz before starting tcpdump. After the run, immediately check file size and radiotap fields to ensure nothing downgraded to 160 MHz mid capture. Catching that early avoids wasting later analysis time on incomplete traces.

Validate results

Verify technical fidelity first by inspecting radiotap fields for channel center and width to confirm the tool truly recorded 320 MHz on 6 GHz. In Wireshark, surface columns for channel frequency and present width to spot mismatches quickly. Then check that beacons and probe responses include expected EHT elements that match your AP configuration. If beacons show a different primary location than planned, the capture radio was misaligned. That mismatch can explain absent control frames. A simple cross check is to filter for association and the first data frame, ensuring timestamps align with your traffic trigger. If timing drifts, revisit host clock sync and power settings.

Assess completeness by sampling both management and data portions to ensure the story is coherent. For instance, you should see authentication, association, and key exchange when troubleshooting join issues, followed by data frames with reasonable MCS values. If MCS collapses or retries spike, the capture vantage point may be marginal. Re run the short control trace from the same location to test whether RF or configuration changed. When packet types appear inconsistent, consider interference or a channel change. Rapid channel move events can truncate a capture without obvious errors, so correlate with controller logs or AP event history to explain gaps.

Correlate the trace with analytics to validate conclusions rather than relying on packets alone. Controller platforms often report utilization, SNR, and latency trends that should echo what you observe in the capture. If analytics show rising noise while your trace appears clean, your vantage point may differ from impacted clients. Use comparative views to verify airtime allocation during the burst matches your expectations. Troubleshoot and optimize

When captures miss expected frames, isolate whether channel mapping or RF conditions are at fault. Start by confirming the primary location and operating class in recent beacons, then adjust the sniffer to match. If frames remain sparse, evaluate SNR and retry patterns to judge whether the vantage point is marginal. Moving a meter or changing antenna orientation can restore control frames without altering traffic. The risk of aggressive repositioning is creating a vantage unrepresentative of clients, so move in small increments and retest. If the AP uses puncturing, ensure your sniffer centers on the unpunctured resource units so control traffic remains decodable.

Reduce file size and increase signal quality by favoring shorter windows and checking radiotap metadata frequently. Rather than a continuous hour, capture several small segments around repeatable events and name files with timestamps. This approach simplifies review and allows quick rollback when a segment shows wrong width or center frequency. If your platform supports channel filters or frame type capture filters that do not distort timing, apply them to limit noise. The tradeoff is potential loss of context, so maintain at least one unfiltered control trace per session. Always sanity check that any filter does not exclude management frames needed for narrative coherence.

Iterate toward better consistency by scripting setup and teardown to minimize human error. Use small shell scripts or OS tasks to set monitor mode, tune channels, start prechecks, and restore managed state after runs. This reduces forgetting a crucial step that downgrades width to 160 MHz or leaves the adapter unavailable for normal use. Keep a checklist that includes clock sync and storage headroom. Over time, adjust durations and traffic triggers to fit your environment. If captures still conflict with analytics, investigate whether clients roam or APs change channels mid test. Those dynamics require tighter windows or pinned channel plans during evaluation.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a special adapter for 6 GHz 320 MHz capture?

Yes. The adapter and driver must support 6 GHz monitor mode and 320 MHz bandwidth. Many devices only scan 6 GHz or limit width to 160 MHz. Check driver documentation and verify by inspecting radiotap fields in a short test trace.

Why are management frames missing in my 320 MHz trace?

The sniffer may be tuned to the wrong primary or center frequency, or your vantage point has poor SNR. Confirm beacon operating class and primary location, then reposition slightly. Weak control traffic often returns when mapping and placement align.

How long should each capture window be?

Keep windows short and tied to specific events. Sixty to ninety seconds for join workflows and around thirty seconds for a deterministic data burst are typical. Short segments reduce noise and make validation easier without losing key exchanges.

Can filters reduce file size without breaking analysis?

Filters can help but use them carefully. Avoid removing management frames you need for context. Maintain at least one unfiltered control trace per session to anchor analysis and to catch any misconfiguration that filters might hide.

What if the AP changes channels during capture?

Channel changes will truncate the narrative. Correlate with AP logs or controller events and rerun with tighter windows or a pinned channel plan. If DFS rules force moves, schedule tests when radar events are unlikely or use non DFS channels.
