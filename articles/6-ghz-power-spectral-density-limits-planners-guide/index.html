6 GHz Power Spectral Density Limits: Planner’s Guide

By Olivia Adams • Published February 26, 2026

Power spectral density at 6 GHz determines how much power you can distribute across a given bandwidth, and those limits shape design choices that affect real performance. Understanding limits and their relation to EIRP helps you set channel width and antenna strategy without drifting into noncompliance. This guide targets planners who need a grounded path from regulation to configuration across indoor and outdoor contexts. We focus on practical conversions, like moving from per megahertz limits to total power, and on how automated frequency coordination modifies what an access point may radiate. By the end, you will recognize when to narrow channels, when to change antennas, and when to ask for an AFC regrant to maintain coverage while respecting incumbents.

Key takeaways

  • PSD limits cap power per megahertz, not total channel energy.
  • Wider channels require lower EIRP to meet the same PSD limit.
  • Antenna gain changes EIRP math and may demand narrower bandwidth.
  • AFC grants bound channels and power; recheck after topology changes.
  • LPI and standard power differ in PSD, indoor use, and coordination.

Concepts and definitions

Power spectral density expresses power per unit bandwidth, and 6 GHz rules commonly define PSD as dBm per megahertz so planners can scale to any channel width. This framing matters because a fixed PSD limit implies that total permitted EIRP decreases as channels widen. For example, doubling bandwidth from 40 to 80 megahertz adds 3 dB of spectrum, which the same per megahertz ceiling spreads thinner, lowering allowable total power. The immediate tradeoff is throughput potential versus cell edge robustness. Higher bandwidth can raise PHY rates but might shorten reliable range if you must reduce EIRP to remain within PSD. Understanding that relationship prevents a misstep where a fast link budget on paper fails at the coverage boundary.

EIRP, or equivalent isotropically radiated power, folds transmitter output and antenna gain into one metric that represents apparent radiated strength. PSD interacts with EIRP because regulators constrain energy density to protect incumbents and manage interference, while EIRP limits cap absolute radiated power. When both apply, the stricter limit controls, meaning you calculate total EIRP implied by the PSD ceiling and compare it to any explicit EIRP cap. A practical rule is to start with PSD, scale by channel width, then subtract cable and add antenna gain to estimate EIRP. The edge case appears with very high gain antennas where even narrow channels exceed EIRP limits before hitting the PSD threshold.

Low power indoor operation, often abbreviated LPI, and standard power regimes define different PSD and use conditions, creating distinct design envelopes. LPI typically requires indoor use with integrated antennas and prohibits outdoor operation to limit interference risk. Standard power often relies on automated frequency coordination that issues grants with channel and power constraints shaped by incumbent protection. These categories drive practical choices, like whether a stadium needs standard power with AFC and directional antennas, or an office can use LPI with consistent indoor boundaries. The limitation to note is that vendor implementations can mask complexity, so reading device data sheets for PSD and EIRP handling is still essential to avoid unintentional violations.

Frameworks and decision lenses

A reliable first lens is bandwidth selection based on coverage targets, where PSD forces explicit tradeoffs. Start by defining the minimum modulation and coding scheme you must sustain at the cell edge, then back into SNR needs that imply EIRP. If PSD constrains total power below what your edge requires at 80 megahertz, prefer 40 or 20 megahertz to keep range intact. A practical example is a warehouse with long aisles where narrower channels maintain handheld connectivity. The limitation is aggregate capacity, which may fall unless you add more radios or spatial reuse. The mechanism is straightforward: PSD caps energy density, and wider channels spread that energy across more spectrum, lowering field strength at the receiver.

The second lens is antenna strategy, balancing gain and beam shape against PSD-derived EIRP. Directional antennas can concentrate energy to improve SNR in targeted areas, but their gain counts toward EIRP and can push you into violations if transmitter power is not reduced. The rule of thumb is to recalibrate conducted power whenever antenna gain changes and verify both PSD and EIRP compliance. For example, replacing a 5 dBi omni with a 13 dBi panel might require cutting conducted power by roughly 8 dB to hold the same EIRP. The edge case arises when cable losses vary across runs, so document actual measured loss rather than catalog values. This keeps model assumptions aligned with field reality.

A third lens is coordination context, where automated frequency coordination influences final limits and channel availability. AFC grant responses may restrict certain frequencies, power levels, or both, which means your theoretical plan based on PSD and EIRP must be reconciled with the grant. A practical workflow is to propose channels and conducted power, request a grant, and adjust until PSD scaled EIRP fits the authorized envelope. When topology changes, such as mounting height or antenna swap, request a new grant because parameters that affect interference predictions have shifted. Examples and short scenarios

Consider an office floor with mixed laptop and phone clients where planners want consistent throughput. Start with 80 megahertz channels, compute total EIRP implied by the PSD limit, and check if cell edge SNR stays healthy at planned AP spacing. If the implied EIRP forces a short range, step down to 40 megahertz while increasing spatial reuse across nonoverlapping channels. This shift often improves median user experience while preserving regulatory headroom. A constraint appears with legacy clients that cannot utilize wider channels anyway, making the narrower plan more efficient. Now take a stadium concourse requiring outdoor coverage with roof mounted APs where standard power is desired. The team selects directional antennas to contain interference, then requests an AFC grant for candidate channels and power. The grant returns allowed frequencies and maximum EIRP that, when divided by bandwidth, must remain within PSD. If the combination restricts power too much at 80 megahertz, planners may adopt 40 megahertz channels with higher permitted EIRP to maintain walkway coverage. The limitation is peak throughput per user, but density is served by many cells. For practical context on coordination requirements and their impact on deployment decisions, consult an explainer focused on automated frequency rules that shape 6 GHz operation.

Finally, examine warehousing with long aisles and metal racks that attenuate signals strongly. Here, narrow channels at 20 or 40 megahertz with modest gain antennas simplify compliance under PSD and reduce cochannel contention. Planners document actual cable loss and verify that the sum of conducted power and antenna gain fits within limits. If handheld scanners require reliable roaming, lower bandwidth helps maintain a stable MCS at the edge without exceeding the EIRP cap. An edge case arises when forklifts create transient shadowing, so additional APs placed mid aisle can mitigate fades without raising conducted power. When standard power is used, remember to request new AFC grants if antenna location or height changes to keep authorizations aligned.

Looking ahead

Planner workflows will mature as controllers and cloud tools expose PSD aware knobs that map to regional rules. Expect more interfaces to compute total EIRP from PSD automatically as you change bandwidth or antennas, with warnings when the plan violates a constraint. The benefit is fewer manual errors and faster iteration on layouts. The tradeoff is reliance on vendor defaults that may lag regulatory updates, so always validate device firmware notes. A good habit is to export configuration summaries and preserve calculations that show channel width, antenna gain, and margins against PSD and EIRP caps. This documentation makes audits simpler and helps you spot unintended changes after maintenance events.

Automation around AFC will also improve, reducing friction between conceptual planning and authorized operation. Planners should anticipate grant preview features that test candidate locations and antennas before installation, turning coordination into a loop that blends field data and modeled constraints. The advantage is higher confidence in coverage predictions with fewer site revisits. The limitation is that any change to physical parameters still requires a new grant, so operational discipline remains crucial. The upside is consistency and speed when onboarding new locations. The risk is drift if local teams adjust antennas or cabling without revisiting the plan. Periodic reviews of site surveys and configuration drift reports help catch issues early. Frequently asked questions

How does PSD differ from EIRP in practice?

PSD limits power per megahertz, while EIRP caps total radiated power. You scale PSD by channel width to estimate total EIRP, then ensure both constraints are satisfied. The stricter limit governs your final configuration.

Does widening the channel always reduce allowed EIRP?

Yes when PSD is the controlling constraint. A fixed per megahertz ceiling means total allowed power decreases as bandwidth increases. If a separate EIRP limit is lower, that cap takes precedence regardless of width.

How do antennas affect PSD and compliance?

Antenna gain adds to EIRP, so you may need to reduce conducted power to stay within limits. Recalculate after any antenna change and account for cable loss. Verify both PSD scaled EIRP and any explicit EIRP cap.

When should I request a new AFC grant?

Request a new grant after changes that affect interference predictions, such as antenna type, height, azimuth, location, or mounting details. Also rerun coordination when channel plans or bandwidth settings materially change.
