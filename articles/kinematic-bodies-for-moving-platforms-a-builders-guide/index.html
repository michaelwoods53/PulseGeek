Kinematic Bodies for Moving Platforms: A Builder’s Guide

By Ethan Palmer • Published December 26, 2025

Kinematic platforms deliver predictable motion, so riders feel stable and controllable. This guide assumes a typical engine like Unity, Unreal, or Godot, a fixed physics step, and access to rigid body settings. We will plan trajectories, configure kinematic bodies, and handle motion transfer to characters without relying on full dynamics. The flow prioritizes correctness over flair, because subtle mistakes compound at contact points. Expect to measure delta movement, apply surface velocity, and gate updates through the physics loop. Along the way, you will validate results with simple probes and correct jitter by aligning timers. With that groundwork, moving platforms become deterministic Lego bricks that support varied puzzles, conveyors, and elevators with minimal surprises.

Key takeaways

  • Use kinematic bodies to script platform motion and preserve determinism.
  • Compute surface velocity from position delta and transfer it to riders.
  • Run motion and character sync in the fixed physics loop for stability.
  • Detect contact enter, stay, exit to apply and remove platform velocity.
  • Validate with known paths, then tune solver iterations and CCD carefully.

Plan the work

Begin with a claim worth testing first: stable platforms come from deterministic motion and explicit contact rules. Sketch your platform path as either parametric curves or waypoint segments with constant speeds to avoid mid-frame easing surprises. For example, a two-second linear ascent followed by a one-second pause helps riders anticipate state changes. Prefer constant velocity over spline tangents when passengers must remain aligned, because momentary acceleration spikes can break friction assumptions. The tradeoff is less cinematic motion, but reliability improves. Decide early how characters will acquire platform velocity. Passing position delta into the character’s motor yields minimal drift, while relying on friction can fail with low solver iterations. Planning these constraints steers technical decisions and narrows test cases.

Set conventions for timing and authority before touching code. Kinematic platforms should be updated by a single owner in the fixed physics step, not in variable rendering loops, so every contact sees the same delta per tick. If your engine separates Update from FixedUpdate, treat the latter as the authoritative clock. Mixing clocks invites jitter when riders sample old transforms. The edge case is camera smoothing that reads platform pose from the render pipeline, so provide interpolation for visuals while physics stays locked. Document that path lengths are multiples of fixed delta times where possible. That reduces micro rounding errors at endpoints and keeps the platform from overshooting anchors, which otherwise causes small corrective teleports and contact nudges.

Define data channels for surface velocity and contact state. The platform computes its linear velocity each physics tick as current minus previous position divided by fixed delta time. Riders should store a reference to the platform they stand on and apply that surface velocity in their motor during movement resolve. This explicit passing of velocity avoids guessing and removes dependence on friction coefficients. The downside is bookkeeping, because characters must clear the reference on contact exit. Add a guard for abrupt direction changes by clamping applied platform velocity to a reasonable magnitude, such as the platform’s configured max speed. That prevents rare spikes when topology changes or when the platform is teleported for level streaming.

  • Plan contact enter and exit bookkeeping to avoid stale platform references.

Prepare environment

Lock physics timing before building prefabs. Use a fixed delta that balances CPU and stability, then keep it consistent across scenes. In Unity, for example, align any CharacterController updates with FixedUpdate so platform deltas match. Enable continuous collision detection only for fast movers or thin obstacles, because blanket CCD costs performance and can fight scripted motion. Configure collision layers so platforms interact with characters but ignore nonessential debris. The tradeoff is fewer emergent interactions, but ridership remains smooth. If your project synchronizes over the network, designate the server as the authority for platform transforms and replicate poses, not forces. Kinematic bodies accept teleport-like changes safely when clients interpolate visuals without touching physics state.

Create clear prefabs for platform and rider roles. The platform should use a rigid body marked as kinematic with a collider sized tightly to its surface. Attach a motion script that defines position over time and computes velocity each fixed tick. The character uses either a CharacterController, a kinematic body motor, or a rigid body with constraints depending on engine patterns. Store a small component on riders that tracks current platform, local contact point, and frame of last contact. This bookkeeping powers transfer of motion and helps diagnose slip. The limitation is maintenance overhead, but the clarity pays off when debugging rare desyncs at path corners or elevator stops.

Document a test scene that isolates variables. Include one straight elevator, one horizontal mover that changes direction, and one diagonal segment at a gentle slope. Place simple cubes as passengers and a controllable player character with a visible velocity debug readout. Add sensors that print contact enter, stay, and exit events to a console so you can verify timing. For extra verification, include one fast conveyor using texture scroll to compare perceived speed to computed surface velocity. This solar-system style layout keeps experiments local and lets you move assets between tracks quickly. Execute steps

The execution path follows a simple loop: move the platform deterministically, compute surface velocity, then inject that velocity into riders while they resolve their own motion. Begin by scripting platform transforms in the fixed step using a time parameter t that advances by fixed delta. The platform script stores previous position to compute velocity. Next, riders detect grounded contact and remember the platform reference for the current tick. Finally, during the rider’s motor update, add platform velocity before processing player input to ensure traction. A drawback appears when characters step off during acceleration, so clear the reference on exit and avoid inertia remnants. This sequence keeps ownership consistent and eliminates reliance on friction tuning.

To make the transfer concrete, introduce a velocity handoff method. The rider motor should fetch the platform’s computed velocity and add it to its desired motion for the tick, then solve collisions as usual. If the engine operates with CharacterController-like movement, add the delta displacement directly to the move call. When using a rigid body motor, accumulate the platform component into the velocity vector before casting and iterative resolution. The catch is double counting when your camera smoothing also samples platform pose; keep rendering interpolation separate from physics. When direction reversals occur mid tick, clamp the imported velocity to platform speed so sudden spikes do not launch riders at corners or endpoints.

The last essential step is guarding order of operations. Update the platform first, then riders, then visuals, all under the fixed step umbrella. Many jitter bugs hide in a rider-first schedule where the platform moves after character resolution. Maintain a per-tick integer stamp so a rider cannot apply a stale platform velocity twice. For edge platforms with moving colliders and triggers, ensure that layers and contact queries are consistent with kinematic bodies. If a rider uses raycasts to find the ground, insert a short skin width offset and ensure the platform’s collider is included. These small sequencing rules keep interactions crisp and make timing assumptions verifiable during testing.

  2. Compute surface velocity: derive linear velocity from position delta divided by fixed delta.
  3. Track contact state: set platform reference on contact enter and clear it on exit.
  4. Inject platform velocity: add surface velocity to rider movement before solving input motion.
  5. Enforce update order: move platforms, then riders, then visuals to prevent jitter.

Validate results

Validation begins with a simple claim: if motion transfer is correct, a rider remains aligned with the platform over time without drift. Place a cube on the platform and log the local offset from the contact point each physics tick. Over ten seconds, the offset should remain within a small tolerance like a few millimeters, subject to collider resolution. Test pauses and direction changes while watching for spikes in imported velocity that would indicate order problems. Repeat with the player character, then with multiple riders to confirm solver workload does not change outcomes. This approach catches mismatches between fixed and render clocks and reveals whether your platform velocity is applied before or after input resolution.

Next, probe corner and slope transitions. Move a horizontal platform to a vertical elevator and observe contact enter and exit timing. The rider should switch references without a visible snap. If you see a jump, print the frame identifiers to ensure the new platform reference is set before the next motor step. For sloped platforms, verify that your ground detection treats the surface as walkable and that surface velocity is decomposed along the plane as needed. The limitation is that very steep slopes can produce sliding unless you cap the projected component. Document thresholds for walkable angles and stick to them for consistent player expectations across levels.

Finish by testing stress conditions. Increase platform speed until characters begin to separate, then enable continuous collision detection selectively on the rider or the platform to reduce tunneling. If CCD fixes separation but increases CPU, consider slightly thicker colliders and lower peak speed. Add scene load events that teleport platforms to new segments and verify that your bookkeeping clears old references. Finally, monitor iteration counts on your physics solver under multiple riders. If constraint iterations drop or frame time spikes, you may see minor jitter that disappears when load is reduced. These experiments tell you where your design bends and where it breaks so you can set safe operating limits.

Troubleshoot and optimize

Most jitter stems from clock mismatches. If riders shake while standing still on a moving platform, ensure both platform updates and the rider motor run in the fixed loop. If your camera samples transform positions in the render loop, add interpolation on visuals only and keep physics authoritative. Another common edge case is double applying platform velocity when the reference persists longer than one tick after exit. Reset the platform reference every fixed step and reacquire it only on ground contact. This design prevents ghosts from old frames. When debugging, log the sequence of platform move, rider resolve, and animation sample along with frame stamps to see ordering clearly.

When riders slip during reversals, clamp the imported platform velocity to a configured maximum and adjust acceleration limits in the rider motor so it can keep up. If sloped platforms cause gradual drift, decompose surface velocity into components parallel and perpendicular to the surface plane and add only the parallel component. That aligns with expected traction. For high speed conveyors, increase the skin width or ground ray length enough to catch the surface between ticks. Avoid raising friction coefficients blindly, because they mask timing issues and can create sticky behavior when the rider steps off. Instead, fix order and transfer logic first, then tighten materials if needed.

Optimize cost by reducing per-tick work that does not change outcomes. Cache platform path samples and precompute waypoint durations so FixedUpdate only evaluates simple formulas. Only enable continuous collision detection on the rider or on the fastest platform, not both. If multiple riders share the same platform, compute surface velocity once and share it, rather than reading transforms repeatedly. Consider solver iteration increases only when contact jitter persists after timing fixes, and raise counts incrementally while watching frame time.   • Fix clock order before touching friction or materials settings.
  • Clamp platform velocity and project along slopes to prevent drift.
  • Limit CCD and share computations to keep frame time under control.

Quick start checklist

  1. Lock physics timing: run platform and rider updates in the fixed step.
  2. Script kinematic motion: parameterize paths and move platforms before riders.
  3. Compute surface velocity: store per-tick delta divided by fixed delta time.
  4. Track grounded state: set platform reference on contact and clear it each tick.
  5. Apply velocity first: add platform velocity to the rider motor before inputs.
  6. Validate for drift: monitor local offsets and correct timing or projection.

Looking ahead

Once the baseline platform carries riders smoothly, branch into variants. Rotating platforms add angular components that require transforming surface velocity into world space using the cross product of angular velocity and radius to the contact point. Multi-car elevators need synchronized instances that share timing parameters so riders can step across seams. For networked games, replicate platform poses with timestamps and let clients interpolate visuals while applying the server’s physics tick for motion transfer. If you want richer motion, layer ease-in only on visuals and keep physical movement constant. Finally, extend tests to crowd scenarios where several riders board and disembark, confirming that contact bookkeeping scales and that solver load stays within budget.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my character jitter on a moving platform?

Jitter usually means mixed clocks. Ensure both platform motion and the rider motor run in the fixed step, then separate render interpolation from physics. Also verify update order so platforms move before riders.

How do I stop slipping when the platform reverses direction?

Clamp imported platform velocity to the platform’s configured max speed and apply it before player input. Clear the platform reference on exit events to avoid stale velocities during reversals or pauses.

Should I enable continuous collision detection on platforms?

Enable CCD selectively when speeds are high or geometry is thin. Start with the rider only. If separation persists, enable it on the fastest platform and measure the performance cost before enabling it everywhere.

Is friction enough to carry riders without explicit velocity transfer?

Friction can work in simple cases, but it is solver dependent and fails under load or quick reversals. Explicitly passing surface velocity to the rider motor is more reliable and easier to debug across scenes.

How do I test for drift over time?

Log the rider’s local offset relative to the initial contact point each physics tick. Over several seconds, the value should stay nearly constant. Spikes indicate order issues or incorrect projection on sloped platforms.
