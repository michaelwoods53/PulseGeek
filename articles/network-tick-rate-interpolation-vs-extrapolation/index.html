Network Tick Rate: Interpolation vs Extrapolation

By Ethan Palmer • Published December 15, 2025

Choosing between interpolation and extrapolation for network tick rate is ultimately about what players feel under jitter and delay. Interpolation buffers recent authoritative states and blends movement for stability when packets arrive irregularly. Extrapolation predicts forward to hide delay, gambling on short windows where motion remains plausible. We will compare both through the lens of latency budgets, fairness, and rollback cost, then map choices to genre norms and platform limits.

Key takeaways

  • Interpolation favors fairness by showing only server-confirmed positions.
  • Extrapolation favors responsiveness by masking delay with short predictions.
  • Lower tick rates increase buffer needs and raise misprediction risks.
  • High jitter demands larger interpolation windows or conservative prediction.
  • Competitive shooters prefer tiny buffers and limited client prediction.

Evaluation criteria and how to weigh them

Start with a practical budget: players tolerate around the sum of server tick interval, one-way network time, and any client buffer. Interpolation trades extra client delay for consistent motion, which suits variable jitter patterns where packets may clump. Extrapolation reduces perceived delay by forecasting from the last state for a short window, which helps snappy input feel. The deciding factor is whether stability or immediacy matters more to the experience. For example, a racing game benefits when vehicles appear continuous even at 60 to 80 ms RTT, while a twitch shooter values instant feedback more. The drawback of extrapolation is visible correction when predictions fail under sudden turns or collisions, so you must bound prediction length tightly.

Next, define fairness as what opponents see relative to the authoritative server timeline. Interpolation aligns views with server-confirmed history, which prevents phantom peeks that happen when extrapolation briefly shows entities ahead of truth. That difference can influence competitive integrity on ranked ladders. The tradeoff is that players perceive slightly older positions, especially at lower tick rates like 20 Hz where buffers need more frames to cover jitter. Extrapolation can be tuned to cap the lead, such as limiting forecasts to half the measured RTT or a fixed 50 ms. Doing so reduces advantage swings but still risks corrections. The art is minimizing advantage while keeping the experience responsive and believable.

Finally, consider cost to correct. Corrections propagate through aim assistance, collision resolution, and hit validation systems. Interpolation shifts the cost from logic to perceived delay, because fewer corrections occur and fewer rollbacks are required client-side. Extrapolation shifts cost into frequent small rewinds and reconciliations when the authoritative snapshot disagrees. That load grows with aggressive prediction or noisy inputs like rapid strafing. If your netcode already implements input reconciliation for local movement, extrapolation aligns well and incremental corrections feel acceptable. If rollback touches many systems, including complex physics or destructible props, prefer interpolation so you constrain correction scope to narrow, predictable cases.

  • Limit prediction windows to cap corrections and reduce advantage swings.

Deep dives per attribute with tradeoffs and concrete examples

Perceived latency depends on tick interval, RTT, and any client buffer. Interpolation adds a deliberate window, often 50 to 100 ms, that smooths jitter and ensures both players observe consistent history. Consider a 30 Hz server with 40 ms one-way delay and a 66 ms interpolation buffer, yielding roughly 106 ms display delay. Motion looks stable across loss bursts, but hit confirmation feels slightly delayed. Extrapolation instead draws movement immediately from the last state and input, which can feel 40 to 60 ms quicker to the local player. The drawback emerges when direction changes sharply, causing a correction snap as the next snapshot arrives. Limit prediction to a narrow slice to preserve the perceptual win without frequent jolts.

Fairness ties to how often a client shows entities ahead of the authoritative timeline. Interpolation rarely grants premature line of sight because it renders the past, letting visibility and hits align with server decisions. That is beneficial for ranked modes where integrity outweighs instant feel. Extrapolation can show an opponent marginally further than the server yet agrees a moment later, which might produce perceived unfair hits or peeks. To moderate this, clamp prediction distance, blend corrections over a few frames, and avoid predicting through occluder boundaries. For shooters, combine limited extrapolation for self-movement with interpolation for remote players to keep opponents grounded in server-confirmed positions.

Jitter management decides how your system behaves on noisy networks that spike and recover. Interpolation handles bursts by expanding buffers slightly above the measured jitter percentile, for instance targeting the 95th percentile of delay variation. That technique keeps motion continuous even when packets arrive in clusters. The cost is more display delay, which can feel heavy at low tick rates. Extrapolation tolerates minor jitter if the forecast window stays below typical snapshot intervals, but it breaks under burst loss or sudden RTT jumps. To mitigate, integrate adaptive prediction that shrinks during volatility and fades visual corrections over time, trading momentary squishiness for fewer harsh snaps under bad conditions.

Correction and rollback cost should be quantified because it compounds across systems. Interpolation minimizes correction frequency so fewer subsystems need to reconcile, which reduces CPU spikes and logic complexity. The tradeoff is that you must budget the added delay and ensure aim assist or input buffering communicate that pacing to players. With extrapolation, expect frequent micro-corrections. Keep recovery cheap by storing compact state deltas and reconciling in constant time per entity. If physics is deterministic and cheap to rewind a few frames, extrapolation pays off. If interactions depend on heavy simulation or non-deterministic events, favor interpolation so reconciliation stays rare and predictable during heavy encounters.

Fit by scenario with short, explicit recommendations

Competitive shooters typically combine interpolation for remote players with restricted client prediction for self, tuned to short horizons like 30 to 50 ms. That setup preserves fairness by keeping enemy positions server-grounded while preserving snappy local input. On 60 to 120 Hz servers, small buffers absorb jitter without bloating delay. On 30 Hz servers, keep interpolation tight and rely on visual smoothing to avoid sluggish feel. For console targets with uniform hardware and stable connections, interpolation can be a safe default. Use a practical profiling guide to find and fix CPU and GPU bottlenecks such as locating CPU and GPU bottlenecks so your network choice is not conflated with frame stalls.

Racing and sports benefit from interpolation as the primary view, focusing on continuity and contact stability. Vehicles and players maintain believable arcs when buffer windows cover jitter percentiles, reducing phantom overlaps. To keep responsiveness, add client-side steering input smoothing that does not require authoritative correction. If drafting or ball interactions require strict fairness, reserve extrapolation for local feedback only and never predict critical collisions. For casual modes with higher RTT variance, increase the interpolation window gradually with gentle camera damping to hide the added delay. This approach reduces spectator confusion during replays, since the rendered timeline aligns closely with server truth without abrupt fixes.

Action RPGs and co-op shooters tolerate more prediction for self because PvE interactions are less sensitive to fairness between players. Use extrapolation for local movement with capped windows and interpolation for other entities. On mobile networks where RTT and jitter vary widely, interpolation windows must be larger and tick rates often lower to conserve battery and thermals. Supplement with minor extrapolation only for very short spans to avoid visible jumps when radio conditions change. Frequently asked questions

Does a higher tick rate always beat a larger interpolation buffer?

No. A higher tick rate reduces snapshot age, but a buffer still covers jitter and loss. If jitter is significant, a modest buffer at a moderate tick rate can feel smoother than a high tick rate with an undersized buffer that exposes choppiness.

How long should an extrapolation window be?

Keep it short, typically below one snapshot interval or a bounded fraction of one-way latency. Many teams cap it between 20 and 50 ms so motion feels responsive while limiting corrections when inputs change or collisions occur.

When should I predict remote players instead of only the local client?

Predicting remote players risks fairness and visible snaps. Consider it only for casual modes or cooperative play where slight advantage is acceptable, and keep predictions conservative with gentle blends on correction to avoid harsh visual artifacts.

Is rollback required if I use interpolation?

Interpolation reduces the need for rollback because states are server-confirmed, but you still need limited reconciliation for events that arrive late or for client-side effects that depend on past inputs. Keep the rollback scope small and deterministic.

How do I size an interpolation buffer under variable jitter?

Measure the distribution of inter-arrival times and target a percentile that matches your quality bar, often the 90th to 95th percentile. Adjust in small steps and verify visually so added delay does not overwhelm responsiveness.
